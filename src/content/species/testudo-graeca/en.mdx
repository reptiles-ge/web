overview: >-
  The Mediterranean tortoise (Testudo graeca) is Georgia’s only land tortoise and
  a native, confirmed species in the country. In eastern Georgia, the form T. g.
  ibera is widespread; T. g. nikolskii, described from western Georgia, is
  considered rare. Based on specimens measured in eastern Georgia, male carapace
  length averages about 18 cm and female carapace length about 21 cm. Females are
  usually larger. Georgia’s 2014 Red List lists the species as Vulnerable (VU).
  Globally, IUCN also assesses it as Vulnerable, though that assessment is old.
habitat: >-
  The Mediterranean tortoise lives in dry, open terrestrial habitats: steppe,
  shrubland, semi-desert, and the edges of open woodland. It does not live in
  water. In Georgia it is especially widespread in the eastern part of the
  country. Records named on this page include Gori, Tbilisi, Bolnisi, and
  Vashlovani National Park. In western Georgia, the species is much rarer.
  Literature also describes its Georgian range up to about 800–1000 m elevation,
  though this is not a strict upper limit for the species.
diet: >-
  The Mediterranean tortoise feeds mainly on plant food: grasses, other
  herbaceous plants, and flowers. Animal food has occasionally been recorded in
  other parts of its range, but its diet in Georgia has not been studied fully
  here. Do not feed wild tortoises.
behavior: >-
  The Mediterranean tortoise lives entirely on land. In warm weather it is
  active by day; in strong heat it often shelters at the base of shrubs, in
  burrows, or under other cover. A full seasonal calendar for Georgia is not
  described separately here. Data from the neighbouring Caucasus suggest an
  active period from roughly spring to autumn, but that should not be treated as
  a direct Georgian calendar. The species lays eggs. Several-egg clutches are
  known at species level, but full breeding data for Georgia are not available
  on this page.
conservation: >-
  The Mediterranean tortoise is a native and confirmed species in Georgia.
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List lists it as Vulnerable (VU). Globally, IUCN also
  assesses it as Vulnerable, though the current global assessment is considered
  outdated. The species is on CITES Appendix II, so international trade in wild
  individuals is regulated. Main threats include habitat loss, fire,
  agriculture-related changes, and collection of tortoises from the wild. Do not
  take a wild tortoise home.
interaction: >-
  The Mediterranean tortoise (Testudo graeca) is Georgia’s only land tortoise.
  It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. When disturbed, it usually
  withdraws into its shell, though it may bite defensively if picked up. In
  Georgia the species is found mainly in the eastern part of the country. The
  form described from western Georgia is considered rare. You are most likely to
  encounter it in dry, shrubby, grassy places. Do not take a wild tortoise home
  or move it elsewhere. If a tortoise bites you, wash the injured area with soap
  and clean water. Seek medical help for heavy bleeding, a deep wound, or signs
  of infection. In Georgia, the Mediterranean tortoise is a Vulnerable (VU)
  species. Threats include habitat loss, agriculture-related changes, and
  collection from the wild.

  traits:
    - >-
      The shell is high and domed, unlike the flatter shells of water turtles.
      Colour ranges from yellowish brown to dark brown and often includes dark
      marks. Shell pattern varies greatly among individuals, so colour or pattern
      alone is not reliable for subspecies identification.
    - >-
      The legs are thick and columnar. It has five claws on the front feet and
      four on the hind feet. It lacks the swimming webs typical of water turtles.
      The head is rounded, and the mouth ends in a horny beak. It has no teeth.
    - >-
      It usually has one small conical horny spur on each hind thigh. Georgia has
      no other land tortoise, so the simplest marks for separating it from water
      turtles are the high shell and thick legs without swimming webs.
    - >-
      Based on specimens measured in eastern Georgia, male carapace length
      averages about 17.9 cm and female carapace length about 21 cm. Females are
      usually larger. Males have a longer tail and often a slightly concave
      plastron. Juveniles are smaller, and their shell pattern is often more
      contrasting.
    - >-
      The European pond turtle (Emys orbicularis), Caspian turtle (Mauremys
      caspica), and introduced red-eared slider (Trachemys scripta) are
      associated with freshwater and have flatter shells and feet better adapted
      for swimming. The familiar form of the red-eared slider, T. s. elegans, has
      a broad red stripe behind the eye. Testudo hermanni is not confirmed in
      Georgia.
    - >-
      Two forms of the Mediterranean tortoise are named for Georgia: T. g. ibera
      from the east and the rare T. g. nikolskii from the west. Different sources
      treat the recognition of T. g. nikolskii differently, and its genetic
      separation is not finally demonstrated. For that reason, assigning a wild
      animal to subspecies by appearance alone is not reliable.
faq:
  - question: Is it found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The Mediterranean tortoise is a native, biologically confirmed species
      in Georgia. It is the country’s only land tortoise.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, the Mediterranean tortoise is found mostly in dry, open places
      in the east. Places named on this page include Gori, Tbilisi, Bolnisi, and
      Vashlovani National Park. In western Georgia, the species is rare, and
      these records do not mean it is evenly distributed across the whole country.
  - question: Is this a water turtle?
    answer: >-
      No. The Mediterranean tortoise is a land species. The European pond turtle,
      Caspian turtle, and red-eared slider are associated with freshwater.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Look for a high, domed shell, thick columnar legs, and no swimming webs.
      It often also has small horny spurs on the hind thighs.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      In individuals measured in eastern Georgia, carapace length was mostly
      about 15–25 cm. Females are larger than males on average. An older Georgian
      source gives a maximum size of about 26 cm.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous?
    answer: >-
      No. The Mediterranean tortoise has no venom and is not dangerous to people.
      It may bite defensively if picked up, so do not disturb it and leave it in
      place.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It feeds mainly on grasses and other herbaceous plants. Its diet in Georgia
      has not been studied fully here.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      Georgia’s 2014 Red List lists the Mediterranean tortoise as Vulnerable
      (VU). The species is also on CITES Appendix II. Taking wild tortoises from
