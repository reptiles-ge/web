overview: >-
  The tawny owl (Strix aluco) is a medium-sized woodland owl (family Strigidae)
  confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident. The 2024 annotated checklist
  of the birds of Georgia codes it YR-R: a breeder present throughout the year.
  Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality is high
  (H), with photographs and sound recordings. SABUKO lists the Georgian name
  ტყის ბუ (forest owl); ჩვეულებრივი ბუ, რუხი ბუ and ჩვეულებრივი ტყის ბუ are
  also used. Adults are about 37–46 cm long at species level, with no ear
  tufts and dark eyes. It is not a threat to people. IUCN lists the species as
  Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns the same category nationally.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look in woodland, forest edge, and parks or gardens with old
  trees — not evenly on treeless high plateaus or open steppe. This page does
  not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024 checklist confirms
  country-level resident status but does not publish a regional gazetteer.
  Species-level habitat is deciduous or mixed forest, also floodplain woodland,
  cemeteries, large gardens and city parks with mature trees; a cavity is
  needed for nesting. Closed boreal conifer forest is less typical. A separate
  national elevation band for Georgia is not published here. Elsewhere in the
  range it breeds high in mountains (species-level literature cites about
  2,350 m in Turkey); that is not a measured Georgian limit. The atlas map is
  left uncoloured until named Georgian localities are taken from sources that
  actually list them.
diet: >-
  This page does not include a separate study of its diet in Georgia. In other parts of its range, it mainly catches small forest mammals, especially voles and mice. It also eats birds, amphibians, beetles and earthworms. In populated areas, birds often make up a larger share of its diet. It usually swallows prey whole. Later, it brings up the remains as a ball; bones and fur may be found at the base of a tree. This describes the species’ diet in general, not a diet measured in Georgia.
behavior: >-
  It is active mainly at night. During the day, it hides among tree branches or close to the trunk, so you will often hear it before you see it. It defends its territory and stays in the same area year-round; it does not fly long distances with the seasons. The male gives a wavering “hoo-hoo” to mark its territory, while a short, sharp “ki-vik” is used to call to one another. Both males and females can make both sounds. The familiar “tuit-tuo” is often a pair calling together, rather than a single call from one bird. It nests in a hollow tree, or more rarely in a hollow part of a building or a nest in a park. It also uses nest boxes. Birds of this species often lay two or three white eggs. Based on nests studied in the United Kingdom, incubation lasts about 28–30 days. A chick leaves the nest a few days before it can fly and hides on nearby branches. These figures for the number of eggs and incubation time are not based on nests studied in Georgia. According to 2024 records, it nests in Georgia and stays there year-round. If you find such a chick, leave it where it is and do not pick it up. Near the nest, a bird may attack in self-defence.
conservation: >-
  Georgia’s 2024 list assesses Strix aluco as having a low risk of extinction both globally and in Georgia (LC). It has the same assessment on the international Red List. The species is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Decree No. 190). Among owls, that list includes the barn owl (Tyto alba), assessed as endangered (EN). This page does not assess whether the number of Strix aluco in Georgia is increasing or decreasing. Known threats to the species include the loss of old trees with hollows, deaths on roads, and poisoning when a bird eats a rodent that has taken rodent poison. This list does not show which threat is more important in Georgia. The species is listed in CITES Appendix II, so international trade in it is regulated. Do not catch wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is harmless to people if you do not go near it. Near its nest, it may attack your head. Do not catch wild birds.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This is a medium-sized, stocky owl with a round head and dark eyes. It has no ear-like feather tufts on its head. According to 2024 information, it lives in Georgia year-round. It is often confused with the long-eared owl, which has these feather tufts and orange eyes.
  traits:
    - >-
      Birds of this species are about 37–46 cm long, with a wingspan of about 81–105 cm, and often weigh about 385–800 g. Females are usually about 5% longer and more than 25% heavier than males. The head is large and round. There are no ear-like tufts of feathers on the head. The feathers around the face form a distinct circle. The eyes are dark brown or black, not yellow. The beak is short, yellowish and hooked. The body is stocky, the wings are broad, and the tail is relatively short.
    - >-
      There are two main color forms: gray-brown and reddish-brown, with shades in between. In both forms, the underside is pale with dark lengthwise streaks. Light and dark patches on the back help it blend in. Young ones are fluffier and have crosswise stripes. Their eyes are dark too. Gray and dark brown color forms have also been described in the South Caucasus, including Georgia. In the wild, the species can be identified, but its color form cannot be determined.
    - >-
      Long-eared owl (Asio otus) is also a year-round resident on the 2024
      Georgian checklist; it has long ear tufts, orange eyes and a slimmer
      body. Short-eared owl (Asio flammeus) is an open-country bird, often
      active by day, with short ear tufts.       Eurasian eagle-owl (Bubo bubo) is
      much larger, with ear tufts and orange eyes; this atlas has a separate
      page. Little owl (Athene noctua) is small, with yellow eyes; this atlas
      has a separate page. Eurasian scops owl (Otus scops) is small, tufted,
      and a breeding migrant in Georgia; this atlas has a separate page. Barn
      owl (Tyto alba) has a heart-shaped face and is pale; it is on the 2014
      Red List and this atlas has a separate page. Tengmalm's owl (Aegolius
      funereus) is a smaller forest owl with yellow eyes; this atlas has a
      separate page. It does not yet have pages for long-eared owl or
      short-eared owl.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Do not handle it. A round-headed, dark-eyed
      owl without ear tufts hooting at night in Georgian woodland is this
      species, not eagle-owl.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows IOC
    and the OSME Region List for names: order Strigiformes, family Strigidae,
    genus Strix. IOC treats Maghreb owl (Strix mauritanica) as a separate
    species of north-west Africa; it is not on the Georgian list. Birds of
    the World still treats mauritanica as a subspecies in some accounts.
  - >-
    The subspecies expected in the Caucasus is S. a. willkonskii (Menzbier,
    1896). The name refers to a collector based in Batumi; that is taxonomic
    history, not a modern range map. NPLG assigns this subspecies to Georgia.
    Range accounts often place willkonskii from north-east Turkey to
    north-west Iran and Turkmenistan. Sharikov and co-authors (2015) separate
    Caucasian willkonskii from eastern European aluco by territorial calls;
    those recordings are from the Russian Black Sea coast, not a Georgian
    sample. Field identification is to species.
  - >-
    Georgian names in use are ჩვეულებრივი ბუ, ტყის ბუ (SABUKO), რუხი ბუ and
    ჩვეულებრივი ტყის ბუ. Avibase lists ბუ and ტყის ბუ. English names are
    Tawny Owl (IOC and the 2024 checklist), also brown owl and Eurasian tawny
    owl.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photo) and SR (sound recording).
    That is country-level confirmation, not a regional map.
