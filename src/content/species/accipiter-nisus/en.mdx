commonName: Eurasian sparrowhawk
location: Year-round resident and migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A small woodland hawk with short wings and a long tail. Confirmed in Georgia as
  a year-round resident and passage migrant on the 2024 national bird checklist.
overview: >-
  The sparrowhawk (Accipiter nisus) is a small hawk that nests in Georgia and can be seen there year-round. In spring and autumn, some sparrowhawks also pass through Georgia. Females are noticeably larger than males. The [goshawk](accipiter-gentilis) (Accipiter gentilis) is a different species.
habitat: >-
  The sparrowhawk nests in forests and also hunts along forest edges, in parks and gardens, and in green spaces in settlements. It commonly nests in Georgia and can also be seen here as it passes through and in winter. It is also found in the Batsara-Babaneuri Protected Areas. In autumn, you can watch sparrowhawks passing through near Batumi, Sakhalvasho and Shuamta. They often fly alone and low, making it hard to count them all. The map below shows only publicly posted records with photos on iNaturalist. It does not show everywhere the sparrowhawk occurs. If a region has no records, or only one, that does not mean the bird is absent there.
diet: >-
  The sparrowhawk mainly hunts birds. Males usually catch small birds, such as sparrows. Larger females can catch birds the size of a thrush, and sometimes even pigeons. Sparrowhawks occasionally catch small mammals, too. They often catch their prey with a sudden, fast attack from cover, at the edge of a forest or in a garden where small birds gather.
behavior: >-
  It is active during the day. In flight, it alternates short wingbeats with glides. It looks like the letter T in flight: its tail is long with an almost straight end, and its wing tips are blunt. It can change direction easily when flying through a forest. Its call is sharp: it rapidly repeats “kek-kek-kek” or similar sounds, especially while nesting. Often, the alarm call of its prey is heard first. It builds a nest from twigs in a tree. It usually lays four to five eggs. The female sits on the eggs for about a month, while the male brings her food.
conservation: >-
  Worldwide, the sparrowhawk is assessed as having a low risk of extinction. Georgia’s 2024 bird checklist also lists it as having a low risk of extinction nationally, but this comes from an update to Georgia’s Red List that has not yet been approved. The sparrowhawk is absent from Georgia’s current 2014 Red List, while the Levant sparrowhawk (Accipiter brevipes) is listed in the VU category. An estimate published in 2011 put the number of breeding sparrowhawk pairs in Georgia at several hundred; it is not a current count. Cases of illegal shooting of sparrowhawks have been recorded along the route used by migrating birds near Batumi. Cases of sparrowhawks being caught for training as hunting birds have also been recorded in Adjara. Do not catch a wild bird without a permit.
interaction: >-
  It is not dangerous to people. It may catch a small bird in a garden. Do not catch a wild bird; it has strong claws.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a small hawk with short, broad wings, a long tail and yellow eyes. The male is bluish grey above and has reddish bars below. The female is larger than the male and is brown. During migration, it may be mistaken for a sparrowhawk or, because of its size, a goshawk.
  traits:
    - >-
      Appearance: It has short, rounded wings and a long tail with an almost straight end. In flight, it alternates between flapping its wings and gliding. Its wings are not pointed like a falcon’s. Adults have yellow or orange eyes and long, yellow legs. Birds of this species are about 29–41 cm long. Males are about 29–34 cm long and weigh 105–196 g; females are 35–41 cm long and weigh 185–350 g. The size difference between males and females is one of the largest among birds of prey.
    - >-
      The male has a bluish-gray back and reddish streaks on its cheeks and chest. From a distance, its underside may look uniformly orange. The female’s back is dark brown or gray-brown, with brown streaks underneath. The eyebrow-like stripe above her eye is often more noticeable. A young bird resembles the female, but its streaks are broader or break up into spots, and its eyes are a paler yellow. A young bird usually lacks a dark stripe down the middle of its throat—a feature of the bird called kortskvita.
    - >-
      The Levant sparrowhawk (Accipiter brevipes; now Tachyspiza brevipes according to the IOC) is listed in the 2024 checklist as a migratory bird that breeds in Georgia. The 2014 Red List, which remains in force, assesses it as facing a risk of extinction. Compared with the sparrowhawk, it has more pointed wings. The wingtips are dark, and there are fewer bars on the undersides of the wings. Adults have dark eyes, not yellow ones. During migration, Levant sparrowhawks often fly in groups, while sparrowhawks usually fly alone. The goshawk (Accipiter gentilis; Astur gentilis according to the IOC) is more heavily built than the sparrowhawk, with broad wings, a white stripe above the eye and a more rounded tail. At a glance or from a distance, a large female sparrowhawk can be mistaken for a goshawk, but it has a lighter build. A young goshawk has lengthwise marks on its underside, while a young sparrowhawk has crosswise bars. The northern shikra (Accipiter badius) is listed in the same checklist as an occasional visitor to Georgia; identifying it in the wild requires specialist knowledge. A young [peregrine falcon](falco-peregrinus) (Falco peregrinus) can resemble a sparrowhawk when flying fast and straight. Seen clearly, its pointed wings and short tail relative to its body size set it apart. The [kestrel](falco-tinnunculus) and the ageri are falcons; both have pointed wings. This atlas has a separate page for the goshawk, but not yet for the Levant sparrowhawk or the shikra.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 in Systema Naturae. The 2024 Georgian
    checklist follows the OSME Region List for names: order Accipitriformes,
    family Accipitridae, genus Accipiter. IOC World Bird List keeps Accipiter
    nisus in Accipiter; northern goshawk is now Astur gentilis and Levant
    sparrowhawk Tachyspiza brevipes. Field identification is to species; the
    checklist still uses the older genus names.
  - >-
    Handbooks assign Caucasus birds to the nominate subspecies, A. n. nisus
    (Europe and Asia Minor east to western Siberia and Iran). Eastern A. n.
    nisosimilis is a more Siberian form and is not a field split in Georgia.
    Macaronesian A. n. granti does not occur here.
  - >-
    The usual Georgian name is მიმინო. English names in use are Eurasian
    Sparrowhawk (2024 checklist), northern sparrowhawk, and sparrowhawk.
    Russian is ястреб-перепелятник. ქორი (goshawk) and ქორცქვიტა (Levant
    sparrowhawk) are other species.
  - >-
    In the 2024 checklist, YR-R means resident breeder and M means passage
    migrant. Seasonality has quality code A, occurrence quality H, and PH means
    a photograph or video. These codes describe the species at country level;
    they do not establish its complete regional distribution.

  - question: When is it present in Georgia?

  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. In the 2024 list, YR-R means the bird lives there year-round and nests there.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
