overview: >-
  The eastern kingsnake (Lampropeltis getula) — also called the chain kingsnake
  — is a harmless medium-to-large constrictor in the family Colubridae. It
  ranges across the southeastern United States from southern New Jersey through
  Florida and west into Alabama. Adults typically measure about 90–122 cm;
  record specimens reach roughly 208 cm. The genus name Lampropeltis means
  "shiny shield" in Greek — the scales are smooth and glossy. The pattern is
  black or dark brown with 17–36 narrow white, cream, or yellowish chain-like
  crossbands. It is classified as Least Concern by the IUCN, though some
  regional populations are declining.
habitat: >-
  It favors pinewoods, mixed forests, grasslands, and landscapes near wetlands —
  sandy uplands, river floodplains, and leaf litter. Studies show it spends most
  of its time concealed under soil and leaf litter; it is often found with dense
  shrubbery and a thick leaf layer.
diet: >-
  It is a dietary generalist: it eats rodents, birds and their eggs, lizards,
  amphibians, turtle eggs, and other snakes — including venomous copperheads and
  rattlesnakes (Crotalinae), to whose venom it is resistant. It subdues prey by
  constriction and is among the strongest constrictors relative to body size.
behavior: >-
  It is diurnal, especially in spring; in heat it becomes more secretive and
  seeks cover. Mating usually occurs in spring. It is egg-laying: clutches are
  roughly 3–24 eggs; incubation lasts about 60–75 days. When threatened it may
  vibrate its tail (mimicking a rattlesnake), musk, or bite — but it is not
  venomous.
conservation: >-
  It is Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. Even so, declines have been recorded
  in some southeastern populations — from habitat fragmentation, roadkill,
  persecution, invasive fire ants, and locally excessive collection. It is
  legally protected in Georgia and South Carolina. It benefits ecosystems by
  controlling rodents and venomous snakes.
