overview: >-
  The white stork (Ciconia ciconia) is a migratory bird that nests in Georgia. The table accompanying this page’s map confirms its presence in Samtskhe–Javakheti, Kvemo Kartli and Adjara. There are only isolated records from Shida Kartli and Abkhazia, and these regions are not counted as part of its range. The 2024 survey confirmed nesting in Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli. Its presence in Adjara is confirmed, but the 2024 survey did not confirm nesting there. Worldwide, the species has a low risk of extinction. Georgia’s 2014 Red List, however, lists it under the name “laklaki” as a species at risk of extinction.
habitat: >-
  During the nesting season in Georgia, white storks are found in and near settlements in Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli, close to mountain meadows and to lakes and marshes high in the mountains. The distribution map also marks Adjara alongside these two regions. White storks have also been recorded in Shida Kartli and Abkhazia, but those regions are not considered part of their range. In 2024, pairs of white storks were nesting in 43 of the 59 settlements surveyed. Every nest counted was in a settlement — on a roof, a pole or another support.
diet: >-
  In open meadows and wet areas, this species catches animals without backbones, amphibians, reptiles, small mammals and fish. When looking for food, it walks on the ground, lowers its head and grabs prey with its beak.
behavior: >-
  It is active during the day. It often soars high with the help of rising warm air. In flight, it keeps its neck stretched out rather than tucked in like a heron’s. Near the nest, its most noticeable sound is the clatter of its beak. A pair builds a nest together or repairs an old one. Both parents sit on the eggs and bring food to the chicks, bringing it back up into the nest. During a count in Georgia in 2024, most nests had two or three chicks. Many migratory birds of this species spend the winter in Africa. The sound recording on this page was not made in Georgia.
conservation: >-
  Worldwide, Ciconia ciconia is assessed as having a low risk of extinction. Georgia’s current 2014 Red List considers the stork at risk of extinction because its population is small. A 2024 bird checklist assesses its risk of extinction in Georgia as low. However, that assessment is based on an updated list that has not yet been approved and does not change its official status under the 2014 Red List. In 2024, 202 nests used by storks were counted; 105 such nests were known in 2018–2020. These figures cannot be compared directly because 36 additional settlements were checked in 2024. In the 23 settlements surveyed in both periods, the number of nests rose by 40%, though the increase may be due to chance. Future development of wind power stations in Samtskhe–Javakheti may pose a threat to storks. Storks are also known to collide with power lines and suffer electric shocks.
interaction: >-
  It is not dangerous to people. It nests in villages in Georgia, so being near people is normal for it. Still, it has a strong beak: do not approach or feed it, and do not pick up its chicks. Leave moving a nest or removing it from a power line to a specialist.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a large white bird. Its wingtips are black, and its bill and legs are long and red. According to the 2024 list, this migratory bird nests in two regions of southern Georgia. It is often confused with the black stork or the large white heron.
  traits:
    - >-
      Birds of this species are about 100–115 cm long. Their wingspan is often about 155–215 cm, and they often weigh about 2.3–4.5 kg. The body and neck are white. The feathers on the wings that help them fly are black and stand out clearly in flight. Adults have red beaks and legs. The skin around the eyes is dark. Females are slightly smaller than males on average, but in the wild, size alone is not a reliable way to tell them apart.
    - >-
      A young bird has a dark or brownish-black beak rather than an entirely red one; its legs are darker too. Up close, beak color is the best clue to its age. In flight, it stretches out its long neck and holds its long legs behind it. Its broad wings have a black trailing edge. A heron folds its neck in flight.
    - >-
      The black stork (Ciconia nigra) is listed in the 2024 list as a migratory bird that nests here. Its national assessment in that list is NT. The 2014 list records it as VU under the name “stork.” Its back, neck and wings are dark, while its belly is white. It nests in forests and river valleys, not on village roofs. The great white egret (Ardea alba) is entirely white, with no black feathers on its wings. It has a yellow bill and an S-shaped neck. Pink and crested pelicans are much larger and have pouched bills. The oriental white stork (Ciconia boyciana) is not in the 2024 list. This atlas does not yet have a separate page for the black stork.
    - >-
      In Georgia, the names of these birds are used in different ways. A 2014 list calls C. ciconia “ლაკლაკი” and C. nigra “ყარყატი”. This page calls C. ciconia “თეთრი ყარყატი” (“white stork”) because the name directly describes its color. “ლაკლაკი” is also an official name for it. In legal texts, “ყარყატი” on its own refers to the black species. It poses no direct threat to people. A white bird with black wings and a red beak perched on a pole in a village is this species.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758, originally as Ardea ciconia. The 2024
    Georgian checklist follows the IOC World Bird List and the OSME Region List
    for names: order Ciconiiformes, family Ciconiidae, genus Ciconia. It is
    entered as Ciconia ciconia ciconia.
  - >-
    Widely recognised subspecies are C. c. ciconia (Europe, north-west Africa,
    western Asia) and C. c. asiatica (Central Asia). The nominate form is the
    one the 2024 checklist assigns to Georgia. Field identification is to
    species.
  - >-
    Georgian is თეთრი ყარყატი; the official 2014 Red List name is ლაკლაკი.
    English is white stork; the 2024 checklist uses Western White Stork to
    separate it from Ciconia boyciana.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: BB; M, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC (the national figure reflects a draft update), occurrence quality H,
    evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    status. The map on this page takes regions from the records table and treats
    only confirmed status as distribution. Breeding localities come from the
    separate 2024 census — Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli — not from a
    checklist gazetteer. Black stork is listed separately as BB; M.
faq:
  - question: Is the white stork found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. As of 2024, it is confirmed to breed in Georgia and is also seen there during migration.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The map’s table confirms that this species occurs in Samtskhe–Javakheti, Kvemo Kartli and Adjara. Only isolated records exist for Shida Kartli and Abkhazia, so those areas are not counted as part of its range. During the 2024 nesting season, 202 occupied nests were counted in 43 settlements in Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      It has a large white body, black feathers on its wings, and a long red bill and red legs. It flies with its neck outstretched. The black stork has a dark back. The great egret has no black feathers at the tips of its wings and folds its neck in flight.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      Birds of this species are about 100–115 cm long, and their wings often span about 155–215 cm.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      In open, often wet areas, it feeds on available prey: animals without backbones, amphibians, and small animals with backbones.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It is found during the nesting season and while migrating. The 2024 list does not count it as a bird that stays through winter.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 survey confirmed that it nests only on supports in settlements in Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      The stork is harmless. Its risk of extinction is low worldwide. The 2014 Red List listed it as facing a risk of extinction. A 2024 list rates that risk as low in Georgia, but the updated assessment has not yet been approved. Do not disturb wild birds or their nests.
