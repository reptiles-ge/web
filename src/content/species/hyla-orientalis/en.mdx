commonName: "Eastern Treefrog"
location: "most of Georgia"
description: >-
  The Eastern Treefrog is a small, non-venomous frog that lives across most of Georgia. In the dry far south-east, the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog takes its place.
overview: |-
  The Eastern Treefrog (Hyla orientalis) is a non-venomous treefrog found across most of Georgia, except the dry far south-east. There, as a rule, a treefrog is the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog (Hyla savignyi). Adults are usually about 3–5 cm from the tip of the snout to the end of the body, and they are not dangerous to people. It lives in open woodland, scrub, gardens and near water, and is common in the Colchis lowland. It has been recorded in Batumi, Poti, Kutaisi, Borjomi, Tbilisi, Kvareli and Lagodekhi. Don't look for it in the Vashlovani steppe.

  In the 2026 checklist of Georgia's species it is listed as a separate species, but so far only as a "candidate". It was split from the European H. arborea mainly on data from one type of DNA, and this still needs checking with data from many genes. The DNA samples come from Lake Chili in Tbilisi and the Tskordza area, while the frog pictured in the checklist is from Gaghma Dvabzu in Guria. Older books often called this treefrog Hyla arborea or Hyla arborea schelkownikowi. The Yellow-Lemon Treefrog, by contrast, is a confirmed species in the checklist. In eastern Georgia the ranges of the two treefrogs meet. There, including around Telavi, it is not yet fully clear how far genes pass from one species to the other. Candidate status does not mean the Eastern Treefrog is rare in Georgia.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia this is a treefrog of woodland and humid country, not of dry
  steppe. Look for it in open broadleaf or mixed forest, shrub, gardens and
  near water — ponds, pools, lake edges or slow streams. It usually avoids
  dense dark forest. The 1996 review places it in forested and farmed
  landscapes, especially orchards, across almost all of Georgia except the far
  south-east. Named sites include the Black Sea coast (Batumi, Kobuleti,
  Poti), Kutaisi, the Borjomi–Bakuriani area, the Tbilisi district (Lisi Lake,
  Kojori, Tskneti), Kvareli and Lagodekhi. The 2026 DNA samples are from Chili
  Lake (Tbilisi) and the Tskordza area; the figure locality is Gaghma Dvabzu
  in Guria. In Lower Svaneti around Lentekhi, breeding calls were recorded in
  2022 and the authors treat the frogs as H. orientalis on call and range.
  The map table marks most regions as confirmed range, including Kakheti, where the contact zone lies. Shida Kartli, Tbilisi and Racha — Lechkhumi — Kvemo Svaneti have too few photo observations so far and are not shaded, although Tbilisi has a DNA sample and calls were recorded near Lentekhi; in the dry Iori valley and Vashlovani a treefrog is more likely H.
  savignyi. The Telavi population shows mixed characters and is not treated
  here as pure either species. The upper elevation in Georgia is not
  established on this page. Do not expect it on the alpine belt of the Greater
  Caucasus. The range continues in the North Caucasus, Anatolia and around the
  Black Sea.
diet: >-
  Adult treefrogs catch invertebrates, mainly insects. Tadpoles feed differently: they eat algae and plant debris.
behavior: |-
  It is more active at dusk and at night. By day it often sits on a leaf or a shrub, or hides. Sticky pads on its toe tips help it climb plants.

  It breeds in small, mostly still water with thick grass. In the Borjomi Gorge it breeds in open places. During the breeding season males call, and their call differs from that of the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog. In Lower Svaneti, calling was recorded in spring and grew stronger toward late May. That is one local observation, not a calendar for the whole country.

  If you come across one, watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up or move it from one body of water to another. It is not venomous, but wash your hands after touching it.
conservation: >-
  Globally, the Eastern Treefrog is considered a species of least concern. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Its numbers may still fall locally if the pools where it lays its eggs dry up or become polluted. Its “candidate” status in the species checklist is about whether it counts as a separate species, not about how threatened it is. In eastern Georgia, its range meets that of the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog (H. savignyi), and this zone has not been fully studied.
interaction: |-
  The Eastern Treefrog (Hyla orientalis) is not venomous and poses no danger to people. It is a small, harmless amphibian, and when it sees a person it usually tries to escape or hide. It is widespread in Georgia and lives in forests, gardens, shrubland and near water. It is especially active at dusk and at night. You may spot it on plants, on leaves or at the water's edge.

  If you meet a treefrog, it is best not to touch it and to leave it in peace. Do not pick it up: amphibian skin is very sensitive, and substances on human hands may harm it. If you touch one by accident, wash your hands well with soap and water and keep them away from your eyes and mouth. Treefrog secretion is usually not a medical danger to people. If it gets into your eye, rinse it with clean water. Do not take the treefrog home or move it to another place. For a wild amphibian, the safest choice is to stay in its natural habitat.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small treefrog with smooth skin and sticky pads on its toe tips. In Georgia it is most often confused with the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog. To tell them apart, where you saw the frog is often more reliable than its colour.
  traits:
    - >-
      Adults are usually about 3–5 cm long from the tip of the snout to the end of the body, not counting the legs. The body is short and the skin smooth, and the toe tips carry rounded sticky pads. These pads are how you tell a treefrog from a Marsh Frog or a toad.
    - >-
      The back is green, but it can turn grey or brownish. Its colour shifts with temperature and background. The belly is usually pale, white or yellowish. A dark stripe often runs along the side and forms a loop at the groin. This feature varies: some Eastern Treefrogs lack the loop, while some Yellow-Lemon Treefrogs have one. So do not tell these two treefrogs apart by the side stripe alone.
    - >-
      The eyes are large, with a horizontal pupil. The eardrum is visible and smaller than the eye. Males have a vocal sac on the throat. These features help with identification, but on their own they will not let you tell the Eastern Treefrog from the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog.
    - >-
      The Yellow-Lemon Treefrog (Hyla savignyi) belongs to the same genus but lives elsewhere: in dry, treeless places south-east of Tbilisi. The Eastern Treefrog is found across most of Georgia, in woodland and damper places. Their mating calls differ too. Where the ranges of the two treefrogs meet, including around Telavi, a frog may look like both at once. So in such places, don't rely on looks alone.
    - >-
      The Marsh Frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) is larger than a treefrog, stays in the water and has no sticky discs on its toes. Toads have warty skin and do not climb trees.
