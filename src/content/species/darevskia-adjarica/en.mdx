commonName: "Ajarian Rock Lizard"
location: "Adjara (Shuakhevi) and Abastumani"
description: >-
  A harmless rock lizard of Adjara and Abastumani. Colour is not enough to
  identify it.
overview: >-
  The Ajarian rock lizard (Darevskia adjarica) has been recorded in Georgia in Adjara and around Abastumani. It belongs to the true lizard family (Lacertidae). Once treated as a subspecies of D. parvula, it is considered a separate species in studies published in 2018 and 2022. The 2026 Georgian checklist still lists it as a candidate species because populations near the contact zones have not been studied sufficiently. The type specimen comes from Abastumani; more recent specimens and DNA barcodes come from Shuakhevi. The spelling ajarica in the checklist refers to the same taxon.
habitat: |-
  A 2026 review lists southwestern Georgia as its range, though the information is considered moderately reliable. An image of its head scales and DNA records come from Shuakhevi. A specimen was also collected there in 2019 at about 600 m. The specimen used to describe the species came from Abastumani, at about 1,400 m; another specimen was collected there in 2019 at about 1,300 m. A DNA record from Ajaristsqali, stored under the name D. parvula, matches the Shuakhevi records by 99.21% and is linked to this species in the list. The interactive map confirms Adjara and Samtskhe-Javakheti on the strength of the published Shuakhevi and Abastumani samples, respectively; geolocated photos and public observations show individual points, not presence throughout either region. That does not mean the species occurs only in these two places, or that every suitable valley has been checked. An older description also mentions a possible isolated population near Bakuriani, which is not added to this atlas map.

  It occurs on rocks, stones and in crevices in wooded valleys.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day, warming itself on stones and sheltering in crevices. Specimens were collected in Shuakhevi and Abastumani on 8 September 2019, confirming activity in Georgia in early September. It lays eggs.
conservation: >-
  The 2026 checklist treats D. adjarica as a candidate species: populations near possible contact with D. parvula have not been studied sufficiently to settle their separation. The IUCN’s 2009 Least Concern assessment was published under D. parvula and cannot serve as a separate assessment of D. adjarica. D. adjarica is not listed on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. The sources here do not establish a population trend for it in Georgia.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and poses no health risk. If you pick it up, it may bite, and its tail may break off. Leave it where it is. Do not keep a wild animal.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a medium-sized rock lizard found in southwestern Georgia. Color alone is not enough to identify it. To distinguish it from other Darevskia species in the wild, consider its scales, the type of place it lives in, and the areas where it is found.
  traits:
    - >-
      This rock-dwelling Darevskia has well-developed legs and a relatively flat head. It is found on rocks and in crevices, unlike the forest-floor derjugini and the large, ground-dwelling Lacerta. Its body is stockier and more rounded than that of D. parvula. The middle of its back often stands out clearly from the dark stripes that pass through the temple area. A pale stripe or a row of whitish spots is often visible along the edges of these dark stripes. Adult males often have orange or brick-red bellies; blue spots near the armpit are sometimes present. Any greenish tint comes from a sheen, not a separate pigment. These are features of its appearance, and color alone cannot be used to tell the species apart.
    - >-
      Head and body length is often about 5–5.5 cm. In one study, it was about 4.2–6.2 cm among the animals examined. Most were from Turkey, with others from Abastumani and Shuakhevi. This page does not give separate measurements for the specimens from Georgia. The tail is usually longer than the body. Total length is not given in the table.
    - >-
      The sixth scale under the jaw can help tell them apart. According to the original description, it is triangular with outward-curving edges in about 95% of cases, while this scale is often flat in D. parvula. In about half of cases, there is one large scale in front of the scale by the rear opening; in the remaining cases, there are two. The images from Shuakhevi in the list show the scales on the head and those in front of the rear opening, rather than the animal’s coloring. These features are hard to make out in the wild without a magnifying glass.
    - >-
      Clark's rock lizard (D. clarkorum) is a separate species. Its presence in the Charnali valley in Adjara has been confirmed. D. derjugini may also occur in the same valley. It lives on the ground in forests and has a more rounded head. The Colchian rock lizard (D. mixta) is found only in western Georgia, but not in Adjara. It was formerly called “Ajarian lizard” in English; it should not be confused with the lizard discussed here. The true D. parvula is not on Georgia's species list. D. tuniyevi occurs in Turkey. One paper suggests that it may extend in a narrow strip as far as Ajaristsqali, but its presence in Georgia has not been confirmed. They cannot be told apart by colour alone.
faq:
  - question: Is the Ajarian rock lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is listed as a candidate species in the 2026 list. The specimen used to describe the species came from Abastumani. The barcodes and the image of its head are from Shuakhevi.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Published samples come from Shuakhevi (Adjara, about 600 m) and Abastumani (about 1,300–1,400 m). Public photo observations also come from sites around Batumi, Keda, Kobuleti and Khulo in Adjara. It occurs on rocks and stones in wooded valleys; an individual record does not establish presence throughout a region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      It is a medium-sized rock lizard with dark, distinct markings. Its belly is often orange or brick-red. Color alone is not enough to identify it. Compare it with clarkorum from Charnali and derjugini, which lives on the forest floor. Do not confuse it with mixta, which is not found in Adjara.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. If you catch it, it may bite you. Its tail may also come off. Leave it where it is.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      The head and body together are often about 5–5.5 cm long. Among the animals measured, this length ranged from about 4.2 to 6.2 cm. Figures for Georgia are not given separately on this page.
  - question: Why is it a candidate species?
    answer: >-
      It was formerly treated as a subspecies of D. parvula. Populations near their possible contact zone have not been studied sufficiently. Arribas and coauthors regard it as a separate species. This atlas follows the list’s assessment and treats it as a possible separate species.
  - question: Is this D. mixta or D. parvula?
    answer: >-
      mixta does not occur in Adjara. D. parvula is not on Georgia’s species list — it is a species from Turkey. An older DNA record from Ajaristsqali is listed as parvula in the database, but it almost matches the DNA records from Shuakhevi.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is active during the day. This page does not give a complete account of when it is active in Georgia throughout the year. The sample from Shuakhevi and Abastumani was collected in early September.
