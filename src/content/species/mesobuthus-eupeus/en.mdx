overview: |-
  The mottled scorpion (Mesobuthus eupeus) belongs to the Buthidae family. A 2022 revision restricted the species to the Caucasus region. Because the original specimen was lost, the authors selected a female collected in Tbilisi as the name-bearing replacement specimen.
  In Georgia, the mottled scorpion is found in Tbilisi, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Shida Kartli and Samtskhe-Javakheti. It prefers warm, dry places and often hides under stones, in cracks or in other sheltered spots.
  It also has a stinger and venom for subduing prey. A scorpion sting can be painful and may sometimes cause other symptoms. Avoid picking up a scorpion or touching one without care.
habitat: |-
  This page does not have detailed information about the types of places where this species is found in Georgia. In the Caucasus and neighboring areas, Mesobuthus eupeus is mainly found in warm, dry, rocky places or open areas covered with grass. It often hides in a shelter during the day.
  The regions shown on the map do not mean that the species is found equally in every type of habitat within each region. An individual field record does not establish confirmed distribution across a region.
diet: |-
  What the mottled scorpion eats in Georgia has not been fully studied on its own. Like other scorpions, it is a predator. It mainly hunts various insects and other small animals with no backbone and jointed legs.
  Scorpions grasp prey with their pincers; they do not use the stinger and venom every time.
behavior: |-
  Scorpions generally keep out of sight. During the day, they often hide under rocks, in crevices, or in other sheltered places. They become more active in the evening and at night.
  Do not catch or move a scorpion with your hands. If you need to move one safely in your home or yard, use a suitable container and a stiff card instead of touching it directly, or contact a specialist.

interaction: |-
  The mottled scorpion is venomous, but it does not seek out people or try to attack them. Stings usually happen by accident when someone touches or presses on a scorpion, or disturbs it in its hiding place.
  Do not touch a scorpion with your hands. Seek medical help promptly if the pain is severe or a child is stung. Call 112 if you have trouble breathing, lose consciousness, or have other severe symptoms beyond the sting site. Read about the first steps to take in the [guide](/scorpions/morielis-nakbeni).

  summary: >-
    Adults measure approximately 4–6 cm in total length. This yellowish-brown scorpion has two pincers and a tail made up of several sections, with a venomous stinger at the tip. A photo alone is not always enough to identify its exact species reliably, and an expert’s assessment is often needed.
  traits:
    - >-
      A scorpion has two pincers. Its tail has several sections, curves upward, and has a stinger at the tip. This is one of the main ways to tell a scorpion from a pseudoscorpion: a pseudoscorpion has pincers but no tail or stinger.
    - >-
      The body of Mesobuthus eupeus is often yellowish or brownish yellow. Dark spots or stripes may also be visible on its back. Color alone is not enough to identify this species accurately.
    - >-
      Many scorpions in the Buthidae family have relatively slender pincers and a well-developed tail. But these features alone are not enough to identify the species accurately. A good close-up photo or an expert’s assessment is more reliable.
    - >-
      Adult [Caucasian scorpions](olivierus-caucasicus) (Olivierus caucasicus) also have a brownish, dark-pigmented back and relatively slender pincers. The two species can be confused in a photo taken from above. Color and the general shape of the pincers are not enough for reliable identification; a specialist also checks finer characters, including the number of teeth on the pectines.
    - >-
      In older sources, the names Buthus eupeus or Mesobuthus eupeus may have been used in a broader sense. Following a revision of the classification in 2022, such older records should not automatically be treated as records of Mesobuthus eupeus as it is more narrowly defined today.
facts:
  - >-
    The accepted name is Mesobuthus eupeus (C. L. Koch, 1839). The protonym is
    Androctonus eupeus C. L. Koch, 1839; older sources may also use Buthus
    eupeus.
  - >-
    Kovařík et al. 2022 restrict Mesobuthus eupeus sensu stricto to the Caucasus
    region and recognize no subspecies.
  - >-
    The map marks distribution as confirmed in Tbilisi, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli,
    Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Shida Kartli, and Samtskhe — Javakheti. Individual field
    records elsewhere do not carry the same status.
  - >-
    The Georgian name used here is ჭრელი მორიელი. English sources often use
    mottled scorpion; older broad usage also used lesser Asian scorpion.

    answer: >-
      Yes, the presence of Mesobuthus eupeus in Georgia has been confirmed. The map marks distribution as confirmed in Tbilisi, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Shida Kartli, and Samtskhe-Javakheti.
  - question: Is it a scorpion, spider, or insect?
    answer: >-
      The mottled scorpion belongs to the arachnid group (Arachnida). Spiders belong to the same group, but scorpions are not spiders. Insects belong to an entirely different group.
  - question: How risky is it to people?
    answer: |-
