commonName: Grey wolf
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A large dog-like predator with grey-brown fur, erect ears, and long legs. It
  is resident in Georgia and is more common in the mountains than in the
  lowlands.
overview: >-
  The grey wolf (Canis lupus) is a mammal in the dog family (Canidae) — the
  largest wild canid in Georgia. IUCN lists Georgia in the native range. It
  appears on national mammal checklists. At species level total length is often
  about 1.3–1.6 m; mass spans a wide range. It has no venom. The main human
  risks are a bite, rabies, and uncommon reports of attacks, not poison. IUCN
  lists it as Least Concern. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia it is more common in the mountains than in the lowlands.
  Shakarashvili et al. 2020 confirm that pattern and also note an increase in
  numbers in recent decades, plus more sightings in the western lowlands,
  including near the Black Sea coast. Genetic samples in Pilot et al. 2014 come
  mainly from three Georgian areas: Kazbegi, Svaneti, and Colchis. Kikvidze and
  Tevzadze 2015 interviewed people in the Lanchkhuti area and Kazbegi District,
  linking more livestock and abandoned habitat with wolf conflict. A field
  photograph on this page is from the David Gareja desert, 7 December 2019 —
  that is a named locality, not a municipality list. Those are
  named study areas, not a complete regional map. This page does not map
  administrative regions. A municipality-by-municipality list is not compiled
  here. Do not assume even density across the whole country. Species-level
  habitat is broad: forest, mountains, open country, and sometimes the edge of
  settlement. A Georgia-specific elevation range in metres is not published on
  this page.
diet: >-
  There is no single national stomach-content study for Georgia on this page.
  Pilot et al. 2014 state that in Colchis and Kazbegi the main prey is often
  domestic ungulates (cattle, sheep, goats and others). The same paper names
  wild ungulates in the South Caucasus fauna and, among non-ungulate prey, the
  brown hare. A 2010 NACRES / GCCP questionnaire on Vashlovani winter pastures
  named the wolf as the main problem predator for most respondents; that is a
  survey, not a full verified kill census. At species level the wolf is a
  carnivore: ungulates, medium-sized mammals, carrion; the local menu depends
  on what prey is available.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at dusk and at night; it can also be seen by day where
  disturbance is low. It lives in a family group (a pack): usually the parents
  and their offspring. Animals also travel alone or as a pair. At species level
  gestation is about 63 days; pups are often born in spring, often 4–7. Those
  figures come from the wider range, not a Georgian den sample. Tracks resemble
  a large dog’s but are often more oval and larger; the forefoot has five toes
  (the dew claw often does not show in the print), the hind foot four, and claws
  usually show. Barking is uncommon; howling is more typical. Do not feed or
  handle wild animals.
conservation: >-
  Boitani, Phillips and Jhala assess Canis lupus as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List (2018 assessment, 2023 amended version); the global trend is assessed
  as stable, though some regional populations are threatened. It is not on
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution No. 190). It is listed as a
  hunting species; quotas and seasons are set by agency documents and are not
  copied here as current numbers. It is on CITES Appendix II (Georgia’s
  population); Appendix I applies only to the populations of Bhutan, India,
  Nepal and Pakistan. Shakarashvili et al. 2020 note an increase in Georgia in
  recent decades. A national census is not confirmed on this page. Local
  pressures can include hunting, livestock conflict, hybridisation with
  free-ranging dogs, and road mortality. Do not handle wild animals.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom. It can bite. It is a large predator; livestock killing is a
  documented conflict in Georgia. Kopaliani et al. 2014 note reports of more
  frequent livestock attacks since the 2000s and several reports of attacks on
  people — those are reports, not a verified clinical series on this page. In
  Georgia it is named among wild carnivores that can carry rabies. Do not feed
  or handle it. Keep dogs vaccinated. If you meet one, keep distance and back
  away slowly; do not run at it or crowd it.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large dog-like predator with grey-brown, often mottled fur, erect
    triangular ears, a thick muzzle, and long legs. It is resident in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level total length is often about 1.3–1.6 m, the tail about
      30–50 cm, shoulder height often about 70–90 cm. Mass is wide: Mech 1974
      gives about 18–55 kg for females and 20–80 kg for males; extreme northern
      males sit near the upper end. Caucasian animals are usually in the middle
      of that range. There is no separate measured Georgian series on this page.
      Males are usually slightly larger than females. The muzzle is thick, the
      ears relatively short and erect.
    - >-
      Colour is usually grey mixed with brown, black, and pale hair. The belly
      is paler. Fully black or nearly white morphs exist in the species, but a
      typical wild animal in Georgia is grey-brown. The tail hangs and is bushy;
      a white tip, as in the red fox, is not a reliable mark.
    - >-
      The golden jackal (Canis aureus) also occurs in Georgia: much smaller,
      shorter-legged, more uniformly yellowish-grey, with a finer muzzle. The
      red fox (Vulpes vulpes) is smaller still, rusty-red, with black ear backs
      and often a white tail tip. A free-ranging dog can match a wolf in size
      and colour; a wolf typically has more erect ears, a thicker muzzle, longer
      legs, and larger feet, but a hybrid is not always identifiable in the
      field.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. A bite or saliva can carry rabies risk. Do not handle
      it, especially if an animal approaches without fear or is aggressive.
      Daytime activity does not by itself mean rabies.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758. The accepted name is Canis lupus. Order
    Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Canidae, genus Canis. The domestic
    dog (C. l. familiaris) is a domestic form of the same species; this page
    covers the wild wolf.
  - >-
    Caucasus literature historically names C. l. cubanensis (Ognev, 1923). Pilot
    et al. 2014 do not treat Caucasian wolves as genetically isolated: haplotypes
    are shared with European and West Asian populations. Do not assign a
    Georgian individual to a subspecies in the field with confidence.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual names are მგელი and რუხი მგელი; some texts use
    ნაცრისფერი მგელი. In English: grey wolf or gray wolf; older texts also say
    timber wolf.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as native. It is in Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s 2002
    annotated mammal list. Eliava et al. 2007 list it as LC. That is
    country-level confirmation, not a regional map.
  - >-
    Kopaliani et al. 2014 document gene flow between wolves and livestock
    guarding dogs in Georgia: more than about 13% of sampled wolves had
    detectable dog ancestry. A hybrid may be less wary of people; that does not
    mean every wolf seen near a village is a hybrid.
faq:
  - question: Is the grey wolf found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. IUCN lists it as native in Georgia. It is on national mammal
      checklists and on the list of hunting species.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      More in the mountains than in the lowlands. Named study areas include
      Kazbegi, Svaneti, Colchis, and the Lanchkhuti area. This page includes a
      field photograph from the David Gareja desert (7 December 2019). More
      sightings in the western lowlands,
      including near the Black Sea coast, have been noted in recent decades.
      This page has no municipality list. The atlas map is not coloured.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A large, long-legged dog-like predator with grey-brown fur, a thick
      muzzle, and erect ears. A jackal is smaller and more yellowish; a fox is
      rusty-red and often has a white tail tip. Telling it from a dog is not
      always easy.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      At species level total length is often about 1.3–1.6 m; mass spans a wide
      range. There is no separate measured Georgian series on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It is a carnivore. In some parts of Georgia, including Kazbegi and
      Colchis, the literature names domestic ungulates as the main prey. Wild
      ungulates and hare also figure; a national menu has not been measured
      here.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      Mainly at dusk and at night. It can also appear by day. It is resident and
      does not migrate like many birds.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous. It can bite. It kills livestock. In Georgia it is
      named among wild carnivores that can carry rabies. Reports of attacks on
      people exist, but frequency is not measured on this page. Do not handle
      it. After a bite, wash the wound and seek medical care.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      It is globally Least Concern and is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. It is
      a hunting species; season and quota are set by official rules.
      International trade is regulated under CITES Appendix II.
