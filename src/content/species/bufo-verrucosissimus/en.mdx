commonName: "Caucasian Toad"
location: "humid forest"
description: >-
  The Caucasian Toad is a non-venomous toad of Georgia's humid forests, with brown, warty skin. Don't pick it up: its defensive secretion may irritate the eyes.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian toad (Bufo verrucosissimus) is a harmless toad of damp forests, confirmed in Georgia. It has no venomous bite and is not dangerous to people, but glands behind its eyes release a defensive fluid, so do not pick it up. As a rule, females are larger than males: among toads studied in Borjomi Canyon, males are about 7–8.5 cm long and females 10–13 cm. Globally it faces a low risk of extinction, and it is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. It belongs to the true toad family. The 2026 Georgian checklist keeps this name but lists it as a candidate species: whether it is a separate species from the common toad (Bufo bufo) is not yet settled. In English it is called the Caucasian toad or Colchic toad.
habitat: >-
  It occurs in Georgia. It is not evenly distributed across the country; do not
  assume it on the dry eastern plains. A 1996 Georgian review lists 20
  localities: in Abkhazia — New Athos, Sukhumi, Gagra, Bedia, Gudauta, Tsebelda,
  Jupshara and Lashipse; in the west and centre — Kvaliti, Kutaisi, Kharagauli,
  Kvereti near Chiatura, Akhaldaba, Tsagveri and Baniskhevi; in the east — the
  Aragvi valley, the Bursa valley (Kvareli) and Lagodekhi; in Adjara — Batumi,
  Mt Mtirala and the Charnali valley. The 1956 Tbilisi record is
  treated as likely an error in that paper and is not marked on this atlas map.
  The 2026 barcode is from Akhaldaba; the checklist figure is Borjomi,
  Akhaldaba. Photos on this page were taken on 16 May 2015 in Borjomi-Kharagauli
  National Park (Zauri Khachidze). Distribution confidence in the checklist is
  medium. The range resembles that of the Caucasian parsley frog: wooded southern
  slopes of the Greater Caucasus, the Meskheti range and the western foothills of
  Trialeti. Look for it in damp, shaded broadleaf or mixed forest, scrub, forest
  edges and clearings. It breeds in clear still or slow water — brooks, springs,
  puddles and seepage pools; it may also use a larger, more permanent water body.
  The Borjomi study site lies at about 900–1200 m — a local study range, not a
  country-wide elevation limit. At species level IUCN cites 0–3000 m.
diet: >-
  Adult toads hunt invertebrates on land. They search for food in fallen leaves and under stones and logs, where prey is more plentiful. Still, do not rely on them to fight garden pests.
behavior: |-
  It lives mainly on land and prefers wet, shaded places. In Borjomi Canyon, breeding peaks in April–June. Pairs often form on land. They then look for a new spawning site every year and rarely return to an old pond. If a larger, permanent body of water appears nearby, males wait for females at the water's edge. As in other forest “brown” frogs and toads, the female usually spawns once a season. This sets it apart from the Green Toad of open country.

  In Borjomi Canyon, the males studied were 2–9 years old and the females 3–10. Most females were 5–7 years old, most males 3–5. If you come across one, leave it where it is and do not pick it up. Wash your hands after any contact.
conservation: |-
  Under the 2023 global assessment, the Caucasian Toad is at low risk of extinction. In 2009, however, it was considered near threatened. It is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List: the amphibians listed there are the Caucasian Salamander and the Eastern Spadefoot. It is not listed under CITES either, so that convention does not restrict international trade in it. In Georgia it is also found in protected areas, including Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park, where it has been photographed.

  The main documented threats are logging, livestock grazing and pollution of the water where the toad lays its eggs. Toads are also killed on roads as they move to breed. In the Caucasus the introduced raccoon is named as a threat too, but the raccoon's effect has not been measured everywhere in Georgia. Do not catch wild amphibians.

  Like most authors, the 2026 checklist treats the Caucasian Toad as a separate species, but keeps it as a candidate and does not yet consider this settled. Some studies see no firm evidence that it is a separate species. The genetic difference between Georgian and European common toads is small, and no sharp border between them shows up in Anatolia. None of this changes how you recognise the toad in the wild. The subspecies that carries the species name is B. v. verrucosissimus.
interaction: |-
  The Caucasian Toad (Bufo verrucosissimus) is not venomous to people and, as a rule, poses no direct danger. It does have glands behind its eyes that release a defensive fluid. This fluid may irritate the eyes, mouth or other delicate, moist areas, so do not pick the toad up or disturb it. In Georgia it lives mainly in damp forests and along forest edges. You may come across it especially near streams, forest pools and wet spots. If you see one, do not catch it or take it home. Just watch it and leave it where it is: that is the best way to protect a wild amphibian.

  If the toad's fluid gets into your eyes or mouth, rinse right away with plenty of clean water. If the irritation is strong or lasts a long time, or if you have pain or your vision gets worse, seek medical help. If you touch a toad, wash your hands well, and do not touch your eyes, mouth or face until you have. Take special care with pets. A dog or other pet should not touch a toad with its mouth: the fluid may cause the animal strong irritation and poisoning-like symptoms.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A forest toad with a short body, warty skin and a brown or grey back. Large glands show behind the eyes, and the pupil is horizontal. In Georgia it is most often confused with the green toad.
  traits:
    - >-
      It has a stocky body and relatively short hind legs, and it often crawls rather than hops. Large rounded warts cover the back, with finer ones on the belly. The back is grey, light brown or reddish-brown, often with dark spots. The belly is grey or yellowish. It has no mosaic of green spots, so if you see one, it is not a Caucasian Toad.
    - >-
      Large glands sit behind the eyes, and the pupil is a horizontal slit. Among toads studied in the Borjomi Gorge, males are usually 7–8.5 cm long and females 10–13 cm. These glands release a defensive fluid that may irritate the eyes or mouth. So do not pick the toad up.
    - >-
      The Green Toad (Bufotes viridis) has irregular green or olive spots on its back. It lives in open country, while the Caucasian Toad is more of a forest animal. Both occur in Borjomi Canyon, but in different kinds of places. If you see green spots on the back and found the toad in open ground, that points to the Green Toad.
    - >-
      The Caucasian Parsley Frog and the Eastern Spadefoot have vertical pupils. The parsley frog is small and slender, with an oblique cross on its back. The spadefoot lives in eastern Georgia and has a hard “spade” on its hind foot. The long-legged wood frog and the Marsh Frog have horizontal pupils and smoother skin, with skin folds running along their sides. The Eastern Treefrog is green, has pads on its toes and climbs trees.
    - >-
      In damp forest, by a brook or a forest pool, a brown, warty toad with a horizontal pupil is more likely a Caucasian toad than a green toad. It is not venomous, but still do not pick it up.
faq:
  - question: Does the Caucasian toad occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It occurs in Georgia and is included in the 2026 Georgian checklist. By 1996, it was known from 20 places in the country. A specimen from Akhaldaba has also been checked by DNA.
  - question: Where might I find it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It lives in humid forests. There are enough records from Adjara, Abkhazia, Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti, Imereti, Racha-Lechkhumi-Kvemo Svaneti, Samtskhe-Javakheti (the Borjomi valley), Shida Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti (the Aragvi valley) and wooded Kakheti, around Lagodekhi and Kvareli. In Guria there are only a few records so far. An old record from Tbilisi is considered an error. Do not expect it on the dry eastern plains.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      The Caucasian Toad has a short body, warty skin and a brown or grey back. Large glands sit behind the eyes, and the pupil is horizontal. Its back has no mottled pattern of green spots, so if you see one, it is not a Caucasian Toad.
  - question: How does it differ from the green toad?
    answer: >-
      By the colour of its back and by where it lives. The Caucasian Toad is brown and lives mostly in forest. The Green Toad has green spots on its back and is found in open country. Both occur in Borjomi, but in different places. So look at the back, and also at where you saw the toad.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. The Caucasian Toad does not have a venomous bite. It does, however, have glands behind its eyes that release a fluid. This fluid can irritate the eyes or mouth. So don't pick it up, and if you touch it, wash your hands afterwards.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Males are usually about 7–8.5 cm long, and females 10–13 cm. As a rule, females are larger than males. These sizes come from toads studied in the Borjomi Valley.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It lives mainly on land. In Borjomi Canyon, breeding peaks in April–June. It often lays its spawn in a different place each year. So if you saw its spawn somewhere last year, you may not find it there this year.
  - question: Is it a separate species from the common toad?
    answer: >-
      The Caucasian Toad's scientific name is Bufo verrucosissimus. On the 2026 checklist it is a candidate species: it may be a separate species from the common toad (Bufo bufo), but this has not yet been finally confirmed. None of this changes how you recognise it in the wild: you tell it apart by the same features.
