description: >-
  A large goat-like mammal of the eastern Greater Caucasus. Adult males carry
  heavy horns that form an open spiral. In Georgia it is tied to high rocky and
  alpine slopes.
overview: >-
  The East Caucasian tur (Capra cylindricornis), also called Daghestan tur or
  eastern tur, is a large wild goat in the family Bovidae. It is a mammal, not a
  reptile. In Georgia the species belongs to the high eastern Greater Caucasus:
  Tusheti, Khevsureti, Kazbegi, and the Lagodekhi part of the Caucasus appear in
  scientific and protected-area sources, but this page does not map
  municipalities. Useful field marks are a heavy body, short legs, and male horns
  that make an open spiral rather than a simple backward scimitar. It has no
  venom and is not treated here as a food or medicinal resource. IUCN assesses it
  globally as Near Threatened; Georgia’s 2014 Red List lists the Daghestan tur as
  Vulnerable (VU).
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, look for this species in the context of high rocky slopes, alpine
  and subalpine meadows, scree, and inaccessible gorges of the Greater Caucasus.
  The Tusheti Protected Landscape page describes a summer range of about 330 km²
  from 2800 m above sea level to the highest peaks; only a small part of that
  range lies inside the protected landscape, in Tsovata and the eastern part of
  the Greater Caucasus ridge. The same source says most animals remain in
  inaccessible nival and subnival zones and descend to alpine meadows rarely in
  summer because those meadows are heavily used for sheep grazing.

  The full species range is the eastern Greater Caucasus — Azerbaijan, Georgia,
  and the North Caucasus part of Russia. Gavashelishvili’s work emphasizes rugged
  terrain, escape cliffs, snow-free grass slopes, and the effect of human
  disturbance. That does not mean the species is evenly distributed across every
  mountain region of Georgia. This page leaves the administrative-region map
  uncoloured because the sources mostly describe mountain blocks, protected
  areas, and habitat rather than a complete municipality checklist.
diet: >-
  This page has no separate Georgian diet table. At species level the tur is a
  herbivore: grasses, alpine herbs, leaves, shoots, and low shrubs, shifting with
  season. In winter it uses lower or less snowy slopes where snow cover and
  disturbance allow. It is not a predator. Ecologically it is a large mountain
  grazer and can be prey for wolves, lynx, and, where ranges overlap, Caucasian
  leopard.
behavior: >-
  East Caucasian tur is active in daylight, but in hot seasons it often rests in
  sheltered places during the middle of the day. Groups change by season and sex:
  females stay with young, young males form their own groups, and adult males are
  often solitary or in male groups. At species level the rut is linked to early
  winter, mainly December to January; young are born in late spring. Those dates
  describe the species range, not a full calendar measured for every Georgian
  population. Tracks are cloven hoofprints on rock and scree, but tracks alone
  are not a reliable species confirmation.
conservation: >-
  The 2020 IUCN assessment lists Capra cylindricornis as Near Threatened.
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List lists Daghestan tur as Vulnerable (VU). The Tusheti
  Protected Landscape source gives about 750 animals for that local population
  and treats the number as stable; this is not a modern national total for all of
  Georgia. Named pressures include illegal hunting, livestock overgrazing that
  reduces subalpine and alpine meadow biomass, habitat fragmentation, and,
  secondarily, disturbance from tourists, helicopters, and ski infrastructure.
  Gavashelishvili’s models also connect the species with cliffs, rugged terrain,
  and less disturbed slopes. Hunting and horn collection are conservation risks,
  not recommended activities.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous, poisonous, or a predator of people. Risk appears when a
  person tries to approach animals on cliffs, follows them for a photograph, or
  lets a dog chase them. Watch from a long distance, do not feed it, and do not
  follow it onto steep slopes. It is on Georgia’s Red List; illegal hunting and
  disturbance are real problems.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large wild goat with short legs, a heavy body, and open-spiralled horns in
    adult males. In Georgia it is a species of the high, rocky eastern Greater
    Caucasus.
  traits:
    - >-
      Size is strongly sexually dimorphic. At species level females are about
      138 cm in body length, 85 cm at the shoulder, and up to about 56 kg; males
      reach about 190 cm in body length, 104 cm at the shoulder, and up to about
      140 kg. These are general literature values, not a separate measured
      Georgian series.
    - >-
      Males have a short beard and a coat that shifts seasonally from darker
      brown to paler rusty brown. Females and young are more uniformly brownish.
      The male horn base is rounded; horns rise up and out, then curve down and
      inward at the tips, making an open spiral. Female horns are much shorter,
      often around 20–22 cm.
    - >-
      [Wild goat](capra-aegagrus) is a different wild caprine: males have
      scimitar-like horns curving backward and stronger dark striping, and the
      Georgian resident population is tied to steep, often wooded slopes. East
      Caucasian tur is larger and uses more open high-mountain slopes. Chamois
      (Rupicapra) is smaller, with short hooked horns. [Roe deer](capreolus-capreolus)
      and [red deer](cervus-elaphus) are deer: males grow antlers, not permanent
      goat horns.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. In the field, the practical combination is large size,
      rocky high-mountain habitat, a heavy goat-like outline, and the open spiral
      horns of adult males.

faq:
  - question: Is the East Caucasian tur found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is a high-mountain mammal of Georgia’s eastern Greater Caucasus.
      Sources name Tusheti, Khevsureti, Kazbegi, and the Lagodekhi part of the
      Caucasus, not the whole country.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Realistically from a distance, on high rocky slopes in alpine and
      subalpine zones. The Tusheti source describes summer range from 2800 m to
      the highest peaks. Close approach disturbs the animal and is risky for
      people.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Adult males have large open-spiralled horns, a heavy body, and short legs.
      Wild goat horns curve backward more like a scimitar; chamois is much
      smaller with short hooked horns.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      General literature gives males up to about 104 cm at the shoulder and up
      to about 140 kg; females are smaller, about 85 cm at the shoulder and up
      to about 56 kg. This page has no separate Georgian measurement series.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Plants — grasses, leaves, shoots, alpine herbs, and low shrubs, depending
      on season. It is not a predator.
  - question: Is it dangerous or venomous?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous and does not hunt people. The risk is approaching it on
      cliffs, disturbing groups, or letting dogs chase animals.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is listed as Vulnerable on Georgia’s Red List and Near Threatened
      globally by IUCN. Illegal hunting is one of the main threats.
