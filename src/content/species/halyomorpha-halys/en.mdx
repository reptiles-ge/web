overview: >-
  Brown marmorated stink bug (Halyomorpha halys) is an insect in the stink bug
  family Pentatomidae. Georgian official sources use the names აზიური ფაროსანა
  and აზიური ბაღლინჯო. The [Georgian Biodiversity Database](https://biodiversity.iliauni.edu.ge/en/species/14072)
  at Ilia State University lists it as Halyomorpha halys (Stål, 1855) and as an
  introduced species in Georgia.

  In Georgia this is not a native wildlife species. [EPPO reports](https://gd.eppo.int/taxon/HALYHA/distribution/GE)
  the first Georgian records from 2016, in Khobi municipality and Pitsunda, and
  still lists the country status as Present, restricted distribution. Georgia's
  [National Food Agency](https://nfa.gov.ge/Ge/Page/Brown%20Marmorated%20Stink%20Bug%20%28BMSB%29)
  links the 2016 spread with damage to hazelnut crops and highlights western
  Georgian outbreak areas in 2017.

  Treating it only as a western Georgian pest is now too narrow. [NCDC guidance](https://test.ncdc.ge/pages/user/LetterContent.aspx?ID=6f8dffa7-e31f-4b8f-b7f6-f188c761e44b)
  used on this page states that by the end of 2017 the insect had also appeared
  in eastern Georgia. This page's distribution map now has records from all 12
  Georgian regions, but by the count rule its spread is treated as confirmed in
  8 regions; the other 4 regions are recorded-only at this stage.

  For a reader in Georgia the useful answer is simple: this is an invasive,
  plant-feeding agricultural pest, especially relevant around hazelnut orchards,
  fruit trees, vegetable plots, gardens, and houses.

  Adults are about 12-17 mm long. The body is broad, shield-shaped, brown and
  marbled; useful field marks are the pale bands on the antennae, legs, and
  outer edge of the abdomen.
habitat: |-
  In Georgia, the brown marmorated stink bug is found mainly on farmland and in settled areas: hazelnut and fruit orchards, vegetable plots, yards, garden edges, bushes and trees where plants it feeds on grow. It is not a native species whose protection matters in forests or steppes; it is an invasive pest.
  The map on this page shows records from all 12 regions of Georgia. Its spread is confirmed in 8 regions; in the other 4, only individual sightings have been recorded. Reptiles.ge’s records confirmed by photos taken on site are shown separately for Chiatura, Kutaisi, Lagodekhi, Kvareli, Akhmeta, Mtskheta and Old Tbilisi. These are firsthand records. The location, date and author of each record are shown in the photo gallery and at the marked points on the map.
  The sources currently used do not allow a reliable determination of the elevations at which it occurs in Georgia.
diet: |-
  The brown marmorated stink bug feeds on plants and damages many kinds of them. Both adults and young bugs pierce leaves, shoots, fruit or seeds with their mouthparts and suck out the juices.
  It feeds on hazelnuts, pome and stone fruit trees, berry plants, vegetables, sunflowers and corn. Peaches, nectarines, apples, pears, raspberries, tomatoes and peppers are also singled out. The range of plants it feeds on is very broad.
  Feeding often leaves sunken, discolored or cork-like damage on fruit. This is an agricultural problem; it does not mean the bug attacks or bites people.
behavior: |-
  In Georgia, these bugs begin feeding in spring, in late April or May, and continue until late autumn. They lay their eggs in groups on the undersides of leaves. Young bugs hatch a few days later. They pass through five stages of development before becoming adults.
  Observations in western Georgia have recorded two complete generations. This finding applies to western Georgia and does not mean that they develop on exactly the same schedule everywhere in the country.
  In autumn, adult bugs look for dry, sheltered places to spend the winter. They may enter homes and attics, gaps around doors and windows, and other cracks. They may also shelter in tree hollows and fallen leaves. When trapped or crushed, they release a strong smell.
conservation: |-
  The main task in dealing with the brown marmorated stink bug is to watch its spread and take appropriate action. Government measures include monitoring the insect, using traps, planning action based on where it has been found, and informing the public. In a private garden, the first step is to check regularly. You can read about where the insect is found in Georgia, how it lives, and how it is controlled in [this overview](https://journals.org.ge/index.php/aans/article/view/125).
  In gardens and hazelnut groves, choose what action to take based on whether the insect is actually present and what stage of development it has reached. Official recommendations on chemical treatment pay particular attention to the nymph stage, before the insect becomes an adult. Use only registered products, follow the label instructions, wear personal protective equipment, and follow official agricultural advice.
interaction: |-
  The brown marmorated stink bug is not poisonous to people. It does not bite people or pets or transmit diseases to them.
  When disturbed, it releases a substance with an unpleasant smell. Some people may be sensitive or allergic to it, although the [NCDC guidance](https://test.ncdc.ge/pages/user/LetterContent.aspx?ID=6f8dffa7-e31f-4b8f-b7f6-f188c761e44b) does not describe it as life-threatening. There is no need to crush the bug by hand: the smell gets stronger and lingers on surfaces.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This bug is flat like a shield and brown with a marbled pattern. You can recognize it by the white bands on its antennae, alternating light and dark spots or stripes along the sides of its abdomen, and its broad body with a mosaic-like pattern.
  traits:
    - >-
      An adult is about 12–17 mm in size. Its body is broad and slightly flattened, and looks like a shield from above.
    - >-
      Colour alone is not enough to identify it: it can sometimes look greyer or dark brown. More reliable signs are the white stripes on its whiskers and the alternating light and dark patches along the outer edge of its belly.
    - >-
      Light-colored stripes are also visible on its legs. The corners of its shoulders do not have sharp spines, so simply seeing a “brown stink bug” is not enough to identify it with certainty.
    - >-
      Nymphs, or young insects that have not yet grown into adults, are smaller than adults. Their color changes as they grow: newly emerged nymphs may be reddish, then develop dark and whitish-brown coloring.
    - >-
      This shield bug can be confused with other shield bugs found in Georgia. To identify it accurately, take a clear photo from above that shows its antennae, the sides of its abdomen and its body shape.
