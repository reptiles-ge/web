commonName: "Green Toad"
location: "Georgia — open country"
description: >-
  A harmless toad with a green-spotted back. Common in open country in Georgia,
  scarce in dense forest.
overview: >-
  The green toad (Bufotes viridis) is a harmless, green-spotted toad of open
  country in Georgia. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as a biologically
  confirmed species and keeps the name Bufotes viridis. It belongs to the true
  toad family (Bufonidae). It has no venomous bite and is not dangerous to
  people. Species-level length is usually about 5–12 cm; females are generally
  larger than males. IUCN assesses it as Least Concern. It is not on Georgia’s
  2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  A 1996 Georgian review maps it across the Caucasian Isthmus — from forest to
  desert and into settled land — but it avoids closed-canopy forest, though it
  may appear in clearings. That paper lists 28 localities in eastern Georgia and
  14 in the west; it is abundant across the country except on the humid
  south-western Black Sea coast. Records from Batumi and Poti are older
  (Nikolsky 1913 and later citations) and it is scarce there. Distribution
  confidence in the checklist is medium. The 2026 barcode and checklist figure
  are from Pantishara Canyon (Dedoplistskaro Municipality, Kakheti). Breeding in
  Borjomi Canyon is confirmed by 1993–1994 field studies; that site lies at
  about 900–1200 m — a local study range, not a country-wide elevation limit.
  This atlas marks all 12 regions, including Kakheti (Pantishara) and
  Samtskhe–Javakheti (Borjomi); western coastal regions are included because of
  those older records and should be treated as scarce. Look for it in steppe,
  clearings, gardens, parks and other open settled land. It breeds in shallow
  pools, ditches and ponds — at species level usually not deeper than 50 cm.
diet: >-
  Adults hunt on land: beetles, spiders, ants and other crawling invertebrates.
  Ants are an important part of the adult diet; newly transformed young take
  smaller prey — springtails, small beetles, mites and flies. Tadpoles eat algae
  and decaying plant matter. There is no separate Georgian diet study on this
  page; these are species-level data.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at dusk and at night. By day it stays hidden. During
  breeding it may also appear by day around shallow water. In dry weather it may
  come to water at night to drink. In Borjomi Canyon the peak of breeding has
  been recorded in April–June. It is an open-country toad: in Borjomi females
  may lay more than one clutch in a season, unlike the forest toad, which
  usually lays one. Eggs are laid in long strings in water. Females there
  typically mature after three winters; the oldest females in that sample
  reached 9–12 years. It overwinters on land — in burrows, holes and under
  stones; sometimes in water. If you find one, leave it where it is and do not
  pick it up. Wash your hands after any contact. It is not dangerous to people.
conservation: >-
  The IUCN Red List assesses Bufotes viridis as Least Concern (2023). It is not
  on Georgia’s 2014 Red List — the amphibians listed there are the Caucasian
  salamander and the Syrian spadefoot. It often lives in gardens, parks and
  other places near people. Local threats include loss of meadows and drying of
  wetlands; forest clearance can favour this open-country species. AmphibiaWeb
  lists it on Appendix II of the Bern Convention.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A short, warty toad with irregular green or olive spots, a horizontal pupil
    and large glands behind the eyes. In Georgia it is most often confused with
    the Caucasian toad.
  traits:
    - >-
      The back is grey or olive, with irregular green or olive spots. The sides
      often show small red or orange warts. The belly is greyish. In the arid
      south-east of Georgia some individuals have also been described with a
      narrow mid-dorsal stripe.
    - >-
      Large glands behind the eyes — parotoid glands — produce a defensive
      secretion that can irritate the mouth or eyes. The pupil is a horizontal
      slit; a round eardrum is usually visible.
    - >-
      The skin is warty. Length is usually about 5–12 cm; females are generally
      larger than males. In the breeding season males develop rough pads on
      their fingers — first on the first finger, and at the peak of the season
      often on the second and third as well.
    - >-
      The Caucasian toad is brown or reddish-brown, without a green-spotted
      mosaic, and lives more in forest. The green toad is an open-country
      animal. Both occur in Borjomi Canyon, but in different microhabitats.
    - >-
      The Syrian spadefoot has a vertical pupil and a hard digging “spade” on
      the hind foot; it lacks large glands behind the eyes. Treefrogs have
      smooth skin and sticky discs on the toes.
faq:
  - question: Does the green toad occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is biologically confirmed in the 2026 Georgian checklist. A 1996
      review treats it as common in the country except on the humid Black Sea
      coast.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In open, dry or semi-arid country — steppe, clearings, gardens and settled
      land, especially in eastern Georgia. Named sites include Pantishara
      (Dedoplistskaro, Kakheti) and Borjomi Canyon. It is scarce on the humid
      Black Sea coast. The map marks all 12 regions; western coastal marks rest
      on older records and should be treated as scarce.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venomous bite and is harmless to people. Secretion from the
      glands behind the eyes can irritate the mouth or eyes; wash your hands
      after contact and do not pick it up. Mouthing by a dog can be harmful.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A short, warty toad with green or olive spots, a horizontal pupil and
      large glands behind the eyes. A plain brown forest toad is a different
      species.
  - question: How does it differ from the Caucasian toad?
    answer: >-
      The green toad has a green-spotted back and lives in open country. The
      Caucasian toad is brown, without that spotted mosaic, and is found more in
      forest. Both occur in Borjomi, but in different places.
  - question: Is sitibundus a separate species?
    answer: >-
      Not in this atlas. The 2026 Georgian checklist keeps the name Bufotes
      viridis: there is no evidence of geographic or genetic isolation between
      Caucasian and European green toads. Some studies call Caucasian
      populations B. sitibundus. In the field the animal is identified the same
      way.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Species-level length is usually about 5–12 cm. Females are generally
      larger than males. In Borjomi, young at the end of their second summer
      are about 3–5 cm.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up or move it from a
      pool. Wash your hands after contact. It is not a medical risk to people.
