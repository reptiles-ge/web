overview: >-
  The common kestrel (Falco tinnunculus) is a small falcon in the family
  Falconidae. Georgia's 2024 bird checklist lists it as a year-round breeding
  resident and passage migrant; the protected-area management plan lists it at
  Lagodekhi. A hovering hunt over open ground, a long tail and a spotted
  rufous back help identify it. It measures about 32–35 cm long. It poses no
  danger in an ordinary encounter, and its global assessment is Least Concern.

  On this page’s records-by-region map table, distribution is confirmed in 9
  regions. One region is recorded-only for now and is not treated as
  distribution; the full list is in the map table.
habitat: >-
  Only regions whose status is “distribution confirmed” on the records-by-region
  table count as distribution. At present that is 9 regions. Map confirmation
  is an observation status and does not mean breeding in every listed region.
  The national checklist confirms occurrence and breeding in Georgia, but does
  not map every municipality. The Lagodekhi protected-area management plan adds
  a named site in Kakheti; it does not establish breeding at every elevation
  there. Across its wider range, the species hunts over grassland, fields and
  other open ground, including grassy road verges. A verified Georgian elevation
  range is not available on this page.
diet: >-
  Species-level sources describe small mammals, especially voles and mice, as
  principal prey. It also takes insects, small birds and other small animals.
  It scans the ground while hovering and then drops on prey. These are general
  feeding patterns, not results from a separate Georgian diet study.
behavior: >-
  Active by day, the kestrel hunts from a perch or hovers with rapid wingbeats
  and a spread tail. Its call can be a sharp repeated “kee-kee-kee,” especially
  near a nest. It uses ledges, holes in buildings or old nests of other birds
  rather than normally building a twig nest. These are species-level traits;
  exact breeding months for Georgia are not established here.
conservation: >-
  Georgia's 2024 checklist gives the common kestrel LC (Least Concern) for both
  global and national assessments. The 2021 IUCN global assessment reports a
  decreasing trend; that does not establish Georgia's population trend. The
  lesser kestrel (Falco naumanni) is a separate species
  and is coded nationally CR in the same checklist. A current quantitative
  Georgian trend or locally assessed threat list is not available here.
interaction: >-
  It is not dangerous to people in an ordinary encounter. Do not handle a wild
  bird or approach its nest; a disturbed bird can defend itself with its talons.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small falcon with pointed wings and a long tail. The back is rufous with
    dark spots, the underside pale and spotted, and a dark mark sits below the
    eye. Hovering over open ground is a good clue, but not conclusive alone.
  traits:
    - >-
      Adult males have a grey head and tail, with one broad black band near the
      tail tip and a spotted rufous back. Females are browner, with several
      bars across the tail; juveniles resemble females. Typical species-level
      length is 32–35 cm and wingspan 71–80 cm, not Georgian measurements.
    - >-
      The lesser kestrel (Falco naumanni) also occurs in Georgia. Its adult
      male has a plainer back; at close range its claws are pale, whereas the
      common kestrel's are dark. Females and juveniles are often difficult to
      separate, so a distant hover or colour alone is insufficient.
    - >-
      The peregrine falcon (Falco peregrinus) is stockier, shorter-tailed and
      has a dark hood and broad moustache. The common buzzard (Buteo buteo)
      has broader wings and a different silhouette. This atlas has profiles
      for both; a lesser kestrel profile has not yet been published.

faq:
  - question: Does the common kestrel occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 national checklist lists it as a year-round breeding
      resident and passage migrant. It is also listed for Lagodekhi.
  - question: Where might I see one in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      On the records-by-region map table, distribution is confirmed in 9
      regions; recorded-only regions are not treated as distribution. Look for
      a hunting bird over open grassland or fields; Lagodekhi is a named site
      in Kakheti.
  - question: How can I identify a common kestrel?
    answer: >-
      Look for pointed wings, a long tail, a spotted rufous back and hovering.
      Males have grey heads and tails; females and young are browner. Hovering
      alone is not a conclusive identification.
  - question: How is it different from a lesser kestrel?
    answer: >-
      Adult male lesser kestrels have plainer backs. Close views show pale
      claws on the lesser kestrel and dark claws on the common kestrel.
      Females and juveniles often need better views to separate.
  - question: How large is a common kestrel?
    answer: >-
      Typical species-level measurements are about 32–35 cm long with a
      71–80 cm wingspan; these are not measurements from Georgia.
  - question: What does it eat, and is it dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      Small mammals are main prey, with other small animals also taken. It
      presents no danger in an ordinary encounter. Leave wild birds and nests
      undisturbed.
