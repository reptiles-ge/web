commonName: "Caucasian Salamander"
location: "western Lesser Caucasus"
description: >-
  The Caucasian Salamander is Georgia’s only true salamander. It has yellow spots, is not venomous and lives by shady mountain streams.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian salamander (Mertensiella caucasica) is Georgia's only true salamander and is included as a confirmed species on the country's 2026 species list. The [newt](/amphibians/tritoni-salamandra) is not a true salamander. The Caucasian salamander lives by shaded mountain streams in beech, conifer and mixed forests. In Georgia, it has been found in the western part of the Lesser Caucasus and around Borjomi and Tashiskari. Known sites include Bakhmaro, the Borjomi Gorge, Tashiskari, Mtirala and Kintrishi. Its body has yellow spots. It is not venomous and poses no danger to people.
habitat: |-
  The Caucasian Salamander lives beside narrow, shaded mountain streams. Such streams are usually 1–1.5 m wide and about 20–30 cm deep in spring. The banks have a thick layer of fallen leaves, along with moss, stones and logs. Adults hide under stones, logs or roots. Near Borjomi, they have also been found 200–300 m from a stream. It occurs in the forest zone and in the mountain belt above it, and avoids places altered by people. Don't look for it in a large river or an open pool.

  In Georgia, it has been found in Adjara, Guria, the Borjomi–Meskheti area and around Tashiskari. Known places include Bakhmaro (Bakhvistskali), the Borjomi Gorge, Akhaldaba, Tashiskari, Mtirala and Kintrishi. These are separate locations and do not mean the salamander occurs continuously throughout the regions listed. The edge of its range runs roughly by Batumi–Kintrishi and Borjomi–Nedzura, then by Abastumani–Goderdzi. The species was first described from the area around Zekari Pass.
diet: >-
  Adult salamanders hunt mainly on land. They catch woodlice, spiders, beetles and other animals without a backbone that live in the soil and along banks. The young, while they are still growing in the stream, eat animals without a backbone that live in the water. These include crustaceans (Gammaridae) and insect larvae.
behavior: |-
  It is mostly active at night. By day it hides and is rarely seen. At middle elevations in the Borjomi Gorge, adults appear in early May and are last seen in September. Around Batumi and in the high mountains, these dates may differ.

  It lays eggs in late spring and summer. Mating takes place on land or in water. The female attaches her eggs one by one in shaded shelters, and sometimes also lays them in damp places away from the stream. In the Borjomi Gorge, a female lays an average of 17 eggs. The young that hatch stay in the stream. Observations in Borjomi showed that it took a couple of years to get from the egg to the change in body form. If you see a salamander, leave it where it is and do not pick it up. Wash your hands after touching it.
conservation: |-
  The 2009 global assessment found the Caucasian Salamander to be at risk of extinction. Georgia's Red List is based on the same assessment and also lists it as at risk of extinction. All the places where it lives add up to less than 2,000 km². They are badly broken up into separate patches, and their condition is getting worse.

  In Georgia, the main threats are forest clearing, using streams to move timber, and damage caused by livestock. When trees along a stream are cut, there is less shade, the water warms up and the salamander's shelters are damaged. It is also caught to be kept as a pet. It occurs in protected areas too, including Borjomi–Kharagauli National Park and Kintrishi. If you see one, do not pick it up or move it elsewhere.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This is Georgia's only true salamander. It has a slender, dark body with yellow or orange spots and a long, round tail. These features let you tell it apart from the newts that live in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      It has oval yellow spots on its back and sides, often in two uneven rows. The back is black, dark brown or brownish orange, and the belly is brownish. Along the sides of its long, slender body, 11–12 grooves are visible between the ribs.
    - >-
      The tail is long and round. It is usually longer than the head and body combined. Total length is usually about 13–19 cm, with the body about 6–8 cm long. The salamander can shed its tail, which then grows back. When newts lay eggs, their tails are flattened from side to side. The salamander's tail is round, so at that time you can tell them apart by the shape of the tail.
    - >-
      The male has a horn-like point on the upper side at the base of his tail; the female has none. In the breeding season, the male also develops rough pads on his front legs. Males and females differ little in size, though in Borjomi males are slightly larger on average.
    - >-
      The salamander has yellow spots and a rounded tail, and it is found along stream banks. Georgia's newts are the [Caucasian Smooth Newt](lissotriton-lantzi), the [Northern Banded Newt](ommatotriton-ophryticus) and the [Eastern Crested Newt](triturus-karelinii). When they lay eggs, their tails are flattened from side to side, and males grow a crest along the back. At this time, newts are more often found in pools or lakes. So in this season, look at the shape of the tail and also note where you saw the animal.
    - >-
      Around Mtirala and Batumi, some salamanders are lighter in color and have fewer spots. This form is called M. c. djanaschvilii. Still, such an animal seen in the wild is considered the same species, the Caucasian Salamander.
faq:
  - question: Does the Caucasian salamander occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Georgia's 2026 species list includes it as a confirmed species. In Georgia it has been found in the western part of the Lesser Caucasus and around Borjomi–Tashiskari. This does not mean it occurs throughout the country.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It lives by shaded mountain streams in Adjara, Guria, the Borjomi Valley and on the northern slopes of Meskheti. Such places include Bakhmaro, Tashiskari, Akhaldaba, Mtirala and Kintrishi. The atlas map marks Adjara, Guria, Samtskhe-Javakheti and Shida Kartli from confirmed records. This does not mean it lives everywhere in these regions.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No, its bite is not venomous. Even so, do not pick it up. If you touch it, wash your hands.
  - question: How do I tell it from a newt?
    answer: >-
      Look at the tail and the spots. The salamander has yellow spots on a dark body and a long, rounded tail. During breeding, a newt has a flattened tail, and the male also has a ridge along its back. Newts are found more often in pools and lakes than along the banks of narrow streams. So besides the shape of the tail, also note where you saw the animal.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Including the tail, it is usually about 13–19 cm long. The body is about 6–8 cm, and the tail is longer than the body.
  - question: Is it endemic?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The Caucasian Salamander lives only in the western Lesser Caucasus, in Georgia and northeastern Turkey. In Georgia, it has been found in the western Lesser Caucasus and around Borjomi–Tashiskari. It occurs nowhere else in the world.
  - question: What should I do if I find one?
    answer: >-
      Watch it and leave it where you found it. Do not pick it up or move it away from the stream. If you do touch it, wash your hands.
  - question: Is djanashvilii a separate species?
    answer: >-
      No. The 2026 list does not treat M. djanashvilii as a separate species. The light-coloured salamanders from Mtirala are genetically no different from salamanders of the same species living elsewhere. In Borjomi–Meskheti, however, there is a genetically distinct group (M. sp. 1). Even so, in the wild you can identify these animals by the same features as any other Caucasian Salamander.
