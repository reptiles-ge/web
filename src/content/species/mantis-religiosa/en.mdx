overview: >-
  The European mantis (Mantis religiosa), also called the praying mantis or
  European praying mantis, is an insect, not a reptile. It belongs to Arthropoda,
  Insecta, Mantodea, and the family Mantidae. The accepted scientific name is
  Mantis religiosa; an important older synonym is Gryllus religiosus Linnaeus,
  1758.

  The species is supported for Georgia at country level. The IUCN global
  assessment lists Georgia among native countries, and the Georgian Biodiversity
  Database includes Mantis religiosa for Georgia. The sources used here do not
  provide enough precise Georgian localities or administrative regions to fill a
  regional map by inference.

  The European mantis is recognized by its mobile triangular head, large compound
  eyes, long prothorax, and raptorial forelegs folded in the familiar "praying"
  posture. Colour is variable: green, brown, straw, or ochre. Adults are large;
  females are usually bulkier, while males are generally slimmer and more mobile.
habitat: >-
  The general habitat is warm, sunny, open vegetation: tall grass, shrubland,

  The IUCN assessment associates the species with open unmanaged places, dry
  bushes, and tall grass; urban gardens can also be suitable where vegetation and
  prey are available.

  For Georgia, the practical answer is cautious: it can be expected in warm,
  sunny vegetated places, including gardens and field edges. Exact regions should

diet: >-
  The European mantis is an ambush predator. Its main prey is other insects and
  small arthropods, seized with the spined raptorial forelegs. Large females can
  sometimes take relatively large prey, but this page does not cite a separate
  Georgian diet series.

  Seeing one in a garden does not mean a pest has appeared. The mantis is a
  natural predator in the ecosystem, although it should not be advertised as a
  guaranteed pest-control tool.
behavior: >-
  A mantis often waits motionless on grass or shrubs and strikes when prey comes
  close. The head is very mobile, the eyes are large, and the forelegs close
  quickly around prey. Adults have wings; males are usually lighter and more
  mobile than females.

  The life cycle does not include complete metamorphosis. A nymph hatches from
  the egg mass and resembles a small adult, but without fully developed wings.
  The species overwinters as an ootheca. Adults are especially visible in summer
  in many northern populations; a precise seasonal calendar for Georgia is not
  established by the sources used here.
conservation: >-

interaction: >-
  The European mantis is not venomous or poisonous to people and is not treated
  as a medically significant danger. If handled, it can grip with the forelegs,
  scratch, or bite, so handling is unnecessary.

  If you find one in a garden, it is best left in place. Observe from a distance,
  photograph it without touching, and do not kill it because it looks unusual.
