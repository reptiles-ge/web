commonName: European turtle dove
location: Breeding migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A small migratory dove with chestnut-and-black scaly upperparts and a
  black-and-white striped neck patch. Confirmed in Georgia as a breeding
  migrant; globally Vulnerable. A national VU assessment appears in a 2024
  conservation order, but the species is absent from Georgia's 2014 Red List.
overview: >-
  The European turtle dove (Streptopelia turtur) is a small dove that breeds in
  Georgia and also passes through in spring and autumn. Its chestnut-and-black
  scaly back and black-and-white neck patches help identify it. It is harmless
  to people.

  According to this page's records-by-region table, distribution is confirmed
  in Adjara and Kakheti. Abkhazia has only isolated records, so that region is
  not treated as part of the range. One observation labeled Gldani, Tbilisi,
  has a public coordinate that falls in Mtskheta-Mtianeti instead; neither
  Tbilisi nor Mtskheta-Mtianeti is treated as distribution on that single
  point. Record counts do not show population density. The 2024 checklist
  confirms the species as a breeding migrant at country level; that does not
  mean it is distributed in every region.

  The IUCN assesses it as globally Vulnerable (VU). A national VU assessment
  appears in both the 2024 Georgian bird checklist and the annex to a ministerial
  conservation order. The species is absent from Georgia's 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, the European turtle dove is present during the breeding season
  and on passage; it usually does not remain in winter. The map table confirms
  distribution in Adjara and Kakheti. Batumi is among those Adjara records and
  is also described as a migration watch point. The Kolkheti monitoring list
  records the species on passage; that listing does not make Guria or
  Samegrelo–Zemo Svaneti a distribution region.

  For nesting, it favours woodland edges, scrub, and places with trees and
  bushes near farmland. It usually builds a nest of thin twigs in a bush or
  tree.
diet: >-
  The European turtle dove feeds mainly on plant food taken from the ground,
  especially small seeds of weeds and cereal plants. It also sometimes eats
  invertebrates, but rarely.

  Both parents feed chicks with a secretion produced in the crop, known as crop
  milk.
behavior: >-
  The European turtle dove is active by day. Its soft, rhythmic “turr-turr”
  purr is often heard from a tree or bush. Flight is fast and direct; the white
  edges of its dark tail show in flight.

  Females usually lay two white eggs. Both parents incubate them. Incubation
  typically lasts 14–16 days, and the young leave the nest after about 18–19
  days.

  The European turtle dove migrates long distances and spends winter in Africa
  south of the Sahara.
conservation: >-
  The European turtle dove is globally Vulnerable (VU) according to the IUCN.
  The annex to a 2024 ministerial conservation order also gives it a national
  VU assessment; the species is absent from Georgia's official 2014 Red List.

  Its numbers have declined in Europe. Reduced availability of seeds and
  nesting scrub in farmland, as well as hunting on migration routes, are
  linked to the decline. A Georgian conservation document names unregulated
  take and the loss of shelterbelts as local threats.
interaction: >-
  The European turtle dove is harmless to people. Do not catch it, take it
  home, or disturb its nest. Streptopelia turtur is absent from Georgia's
  official 2026 hunting-season list; the Eurasian collared dove (Streptopelia
  decaocto) is listed separately.

  summary: >-
    Adult European turtle doves have a chestnut-and-black scaly back and
    black-and-white striped patches on both sides of the neck. Juveniles lack
    clear neck patches, so also check the pattern of the back and tail.
  traits:
    - >-
      Adult European turtle doves are about 26–28 cm long, with a wingspan of
      about 47–53 cm.

      The back is chestnut and black with a scaly pattern. The head and
      underparts are grey or pinkish-grey, and the breast often shows a light
      pink wash. Each side of the neck has a black-and-white striped patch.
      Adults show a reddish ring around the eye. The dark tail has a white tip
      and sides, especially visible in flight.
    - >-
      Young European turtle doves are duller than adults and do not yet have the
      clear black-and-white neck patch. Males and females look similar in the
      field.

      The song is a soft, repeated “turr-turr” purr. European turtle doves often
      feed on the ground in open places, and a sharp wing sound may be heard
      when they take off.
    - >-
      Eurasian collared dove (Streptopelia decaocto) occurs in Georgia year
      round. It is paler and more uniform grey-buff, with a single black
      half-collar on the nape, and it lacks the chestnut-and-black scaly back of
      the European turtle dove.

      Oriental turtle dove (Streptopelia orientalis meena) occurs in Georgia on
      migration. It is larger and darker, with a different neck pattern.
      Laughing dove (Spilopelia senegalensis) is smaller and warmer pink-brown,
      with a spotted neck pattern. At a distance, a young
      [common woodpigeon](columba-palumbus) (Columba palumbus) may be mistaken
      for a young European turtle dove:
      neither yet shows its adult neck patch. The woodpigeon is much larger and
      bulkier, and even young birds retain a broad white wing band. The turtle
      dove has a chestnut-and-black scaly back and a dark tail edged with white.

    Streptopelia (Bonaparte, 1855). The 2024 Georgian checklist uses
    Streptopelia turtur, follows the IOC World Bird List for taxonomy, and the
    OSME Region List for names.

faq:
  - question: Is the European turtle dove found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The European turtle dove (Streptopelia turtur) is a confirmed
      breeding migrant and passage species in Georgia. In the non-breeding
      season, including winter, it usually does not remain in the country.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the warm season, look for the European turtle dove in open and
      semi-open places: scrub, woodland edges, hedges and near seed-rich
      farmland.

      The records-by-region table confirms distribution in Adjara and Kakheti.
      Batumi is a confirmed migration point in Adjara. The Kolkheti monitoring
      list records the species on passage, but Guria and Samegrelo–Zemo Svaneti
      are not treated as distribution regions by this table. Abkhazia has only
      isolated records.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      To identify the European turtle dove, look for the chestnut-and-black
      scaly back, black-and-white striped patches on the sides of the neck, the
      white-edged dark tail and the soft “turr-turr” purr.

      Eurasian collared dove is paler and more uniform, has a single black
      half-collar on the nape, and lacks the chestnut-and-black scaly back.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      Adult European turtle doves are about 26–28 cm long, with a wingspan of
      about 47–53 cm.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      The European turtle dove feeds mainly on plant seeds and grain taken from
      the ground. Both parents feed chicks with a nutritious secretion produced
      in the crop.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, the European turtle dove occurs in the breeding season and on
      spring and autumn passage; it usually does not remain in winter.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 checklist confirms the European turtle dove as a breeding
      species in Georgia. At the Batumi monitoring site it is listed as a
      passage bird, which does not establish breeding there. The Kolkheti
      monitoring list also records it on passage; that listing establishes
      neither breeding there nor distribution across Guria or Samegrelo–Zemo
      Svaneti.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      The European turtle dove is harmless to people and globally Vulnerable
      (VU). A national VU assessment also appears in a 2024 ministerial
      conservation order, though the species is absent from Georgia's 2014 Red
      List and official 2026 hunting-season list. Do not catch or hunt it.
