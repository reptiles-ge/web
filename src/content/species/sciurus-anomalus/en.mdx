commonName: Caucasian squirrel
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A medium tree squirrel with a chestnut-grey back, a pale eye-ring, and a rusty
  to yellowish belly that is not white. It is native and resident in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian squirrel (Sciurus anomalus) is a mammal in the squirrel family
  (Sciuridae). It is a tree squirrel and a native resident in Georgia. The type
  locality was later restricted to the fortress of Sabeka on the Sulori River,
  about 25 km southwest of Kutaisi. IUCN lists Georgia in the native range.
  Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s 2002 mammal list includes it. At species level
  total length is about 32–36 cm, the tail 13–18 cm, and mass about 250–410 g.
  It has no venom. The main human risk is a bite if handled, not poison. IUCN
  lists it as Least Concern. Georgia’s 2014 Red List lists it as Vulnerable
  (VU, criterion A1e) because of pressure from an introduced species.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in deciduous and mixed forest — beech, chestnut, oak,
  hazel — and in orchards and parks with mature trees. National Geographic
  Georgia and the Wildlife Agency describe the Georgian range as the southern
  slopes of the Greater Caucasus and the northern slopes of the Lesser Caucasus.
  Koprowski and co-authors, summarising Caucasian literature, say beech forest
  up to about 2,000 m is typical and that pine forest is often avoided; that is
  a Caucasus-level statement, not a measured Georgian elevation belt. Agency
  pages instead say it lives mainly in coniferous and mixed forest. Those two
  habitat pictures disagree; this page does not choose one as a national map.
  This page does not colour administrative regions. Named points: the restricted
  type locality near Kutaisi (Chaworth-Musters 1937); Bukhnikashvili, Kandaurov,
  Sheklashvili and Natradze 2023 compile 71 Georgian records from 1855–2022
  (mostly museum and literature, plus two unpublished field records) without
  reprinting the gazetteer here; Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park, 4 June 2015
  (photograph by ზაური ხაჩიძე on this page; the animal is on a conifer branch —
  one named locality, not a settlement of the habitat disagreement); Vake Park,
  Tbilisi, 9 February 2025 (photograph by Yuri Samoylov on this page). GBIF also aggregates recent iNaturalist
  points, including clusters around Tbilisi, Kakheti (Telavi), Mtskheta-Mtianeti
  and Kvemo Kartli; those are not treated here as a verified municipality list.
  Do not assume even density across the country.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range tree
  seeds are the main food: acorns, beechnuts, chestnuts, walnuts, and hazelnuts.
  Caucasian accounts also list apricot stones, fruit (apple, pear, peach, grape,
  plum), berries, mushrooms, and occasionally insects, eggs, or nestlings. It
  stores nuts in tree cavities, rock clefts, or buildings, and in some areas
  buries them in leaf litter. The realistic picture in Georgia is forest and
  orchard seed, but the local menu has not been measured here.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day and does not hibernate. Activity is often morning and late
  afternoon, with a midday pause in warm weather and a shift toward midday in
  winter. It forages in trees and on the ground and is more terrestrial than the
  Eurasian red squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris). Leaps between trees are uncommon in
  the Caucasian literature. Nests are usually tree hollows lined with moss and
  dry leaves, often 5–14 m up, with the entrance facing south or southeast;
  nests under rocks, roots, or buildings are also recorded. Breeding can occur
  through much of the year; in Transcaucasia litters of 2–7 young are most often
  reported in late January–early February, April, and mid-July–late August.
  Those dates are regional, not a Georgian den sample. The call is a sharp
  metallic chit-chit-chit; Stroganova compared it with the green woodpecker.
  There is no Georgian recording on this page.
conservation: >-
  Yiğit, Kryštufek, Sozen, Bukhnikashvili and Shenbrot assess Sciurus anomalus
  as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (2016 assessment; 2017 errata version).
  The global range is wide; the main named threat is habitat destruction.
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution No. 190) lists it as Vulnerable
  (VU, A1e): under pressure from an introduced species. That introduced squirrel
  is Sciurus vulgaris, released onto the northern Greater Caucasus from the
  Altai in 1937 and now sympatric with S. anomalus in parts of the Caucasus
  (Stroganova 1958; Vereshchagin 1967). Bukhnikashvili et al. 2023 also tabulate
  Georgian records of S. vulgaris. Field identification to species still matters.
  This atlas has no Eurasian red squirrel page yet. Local pressures also include
  forest loss and, historically, fur harvest; a current national trend is not
  measured on this page. Do not handle wild animals.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is not poisonous to touch. It can bite if handled or
  cornered. Any wild mammal can theoretically carry rabies; Georgian literature
  names fox, jackal, wolf, and dogs as the main sources, not this species. Do
  not feed or pick it up. In some parts of the Caucasus it enters nut and fruit
  orchards; that is a crop issue, not a medical one.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium tree squirrel with a chestnut-grey back, a pale eye-ring, and a
    rusty to yellowish belly. Native and resident in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      Total length about 32–36 cm, tail 13–18 cm, mass about 250–410 g. There is
      no obvious size or colour difference between males and females. Claws are
      relatively short for a tree squirrel. The ears may show small winter tufts
      (about 1.0–1.2 cm) in the north of the range; they are not the long tufts
      of S. vulgaris (about 2.5–3.5 cm where the two overlap). The Borjomi-Kharagauli
      photograph of 4 June 2015 on this page shows short, rounded ears without
      long tufts.
    - >-
      The back is buffy chestnut-grey in the nominate subspecies (S. a.
      anomalus), which is the form of Transcaucasia, Turkey, and Lesbos. A pale
      buff to yellow ring around the eye is usual. The belly is rust-chestnut to
      buff-yellow — not white. The tail is heavily furred and may be yellow-brown
      to deep red. Summer and winter coats are similar; the belly may look
      rustier in winter.
    - >-
      The only other Sciurus in Georgia is the introduced Eurasian red squirrel
      (S. vulgaris), tabulated in Bukhnikashvili et al. 2023. Distinguish it in
      the field by the belly: white or cream in vulgaris, rusty or yellowish in
      anomalus. Vulgaris is more strictly arboreal and, in winter in the overlap
      zone, has longer ear tufts. A red fox is much larger and dog-like. The
      edible dormouse (Glis glis) is smaller, grey, nocturnal, and tailless of
      the squirrel look. This atlas has no vulgaris or dormouse page yet.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. A daytime squirrel in Georgian woodland or a city park,
      with a rusty belly and no long ear tufts, is this species if the
      identification is to Sciurus. Do not handle it to check pads or teats.
facts:
  - >-
    Güldenstädt described it in 1785. The accepted name is Sciurus anomalus.
    Order Rodentia, family Sciuridae, genus Sciurus. The authority is sometimes
    wrongly given as J. F. Gmelin; Koprowski and co-authors treat Güldenstädt,
    1785 as correct. Sciurus persicus has been misapplied; that name traces to a
    dormouse description, not this squirrel.
  - >-
    Three subspecies are widely listed: S. a. anomalus (Transcaucasia, Turkey,
    Lesbos), S. a. syriacus (Levant), and S. a. pallescens (Iraq and Zagros
    Iran). Georgian animals belong with the nominate form. Field identification
    in Georgia is to species.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual name is კავკასიური ციყვი; სპარსული ციყვი is also used.
    In English: Caucasian squirrel or Persian squirrel; also golden squirrel or
    Transcaucasian squirrel in older literature. Generic ციყვი means squirrel.
faq:
  - question: Is the Caucasian squirrel found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is native and resident. The type locality was restricted to near
      Kutaisi. IUCN lists Georgia as native. National mammal lists include it.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In deciduous and mixed forest and in orchards and parks with large trees.
      Named points include the type locality near Kutaisi; Borjomi-Kharagauli
      National Park (photograph, 4 June 2015); and Vake Park in Tbilisi
      (photograph, 9 February 2025). A 2023 compilation has 71 Georgian records
      from 1855–2022. This page does not map every region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Medium tree squirrel, chestnut-grey back, pale eye-ring, rusty or yellowish
      belly — not white. The introduced red squirrel has a white or cream belly
      and, in winter, longer ear tufts.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 32–36 cm including the tail of 13–18 cm; mass about 250–410 g. There
      is no separate measured Georgian series on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly tree seeds and nuts. Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia
      on this page.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      By day, year-round. It does not hibernate. It is a resident and does not
      migrate like many birds.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous. It can bite if handled. Do not pick it up.
  - question: Is it protected?
