overview: |-
  The Italian scorpion (Euscorpius italicus) is a true scorpion and belongs to the arachnid group (Arachnida). It is in the Euscorpiidae family and is recognized as a distinct species in modern databases.
  Several sources confirm its presence in Georgia. Scorpion Files includes Georgia in the species’ range. Georgian Biodiversity Database lists it as native to Georgia and names several places in western Georgia: Batumi, Kobuleti, Ortabatumi, Ozurgeti, Kutaisi, Zestafoni, Zugdidi and Gagra.
  According to the “Records by region” table on this page’s map, the species’ distribution is confirmed in Abkhazia, Adjara and Imereti. In Guria, Kakheti, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti and Racha — Lechkhumi — Kvemo Svaneti, only isolated finds have been recorded so far.
  The Italian scorpion is fairly large compared with other Euscorpius species: adults grow to about 50 mm. Based on available information, it is not considered a major risk to human health, and the effects of its sting are mostly limited to the sting site.
habitat: |-
  The Italian scorpion is associated with warm places. It is found across a wide area, including in ruins, buildings, cracks in walls, under stones, and other sheltered places. It may also turn up where people live.
  The Georgian entries in the Georgian Biodiversity Database also say it is found in places changed by people. So a scorpion resembling Euscorpius found in a yard, a wall crack, or near a building could be Euscorpius italicus, but its species cannot be identified from where it was found alone.
diet: >-
  Like other scorpions, the Italian scorpion is a predator that hunts small insects and similar animals. It catches prey with its strong pincers and uses its stinger and venom to subdue it. This is based on what is known about scorpions in general; the Italian scorpion’s diet has not been studied separately in detail in Georgia.
behavior: |-
  The Italian scorpion tends to stay hidden. During the day, it often hides under stones, in cracks in walls or buildings, and in other shelters. It is more likely to be active in the evening and at night.
  Do not pick it up. If you need to move it in your home or yard, use a container and a stiff card so you do not touch it, or contact a specialist.
  Clear photos from above and from the side, along with the exact place it was found, are especially helpful for identifying it accurately.
conservation: >-
  A 2021 entry in the Georgian Biodiversity Database lists Euscorpius italicus as native to Georgia. In the same database, the national assessment field says LC, the global IUCN assessment field says NE, and the protection status field says “Not defined.” These entries should not, on their own, be taken as the species’ legal status under Georgia’s current Red List or as a complete assessment of its population in Georgia.
interaction: |-
  The Italian scorpion has venom and uses it to subdue its prey. According to available medical data, the effects of its sting are mostly limited to the sting site. It is not considered a major health risk to people.
  Do not touch a scorpion with your hands. Seek medical care promptly if the pain is severe or a child is stung. Call 112 if the person who was stung has trouble breathing, loses consciousness, or develops other severe symptoms that are not limited to the sting site. See the [guide](/scorpions/morielis-nakbeni) for the first steps to take.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This is a relatively large Euscorpius-type scorpion with a dark body, strong pincers and a relatively short, thin tail. Other scorpions also live in Georgia, so it is hard to identify the species from its general appearance alone. A good-quality close-up photo and the location where it was found help greatly with identification.
  traits:
    - >-
      According to Scorpion Files, Euscorpius italicus is one of the relatively large species in its genus. An adult scorpion can reach a total length of about 50 mm.
    - >-
      The body is usually dark brown, while the legs and tip of the tail may be lighter, yellowish brown. The pincers are strong and thick, and the tail is short and slender compared with the body.
    - >-
      Species of Euscorpius and the related Alpiscorpius often cannot be accurately told apart by color or size alone. This may require a close look at small features of their bodies and an expert’s assessment.
    - >-
      The [mottled scorpion](mesobuthus-eupeus) (Mesobuthus eupeus) belongs to the Buthidae family. In Georgia, it is more often found in dry, open places. The Italian scorpion belongs to the Euscorpiidae family. According to sources, it is often found in warm places, near buildings, in cracks in walls and in other shelters.

faq:
  - question: Is the Italian scorpion found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The Italian scorpion has been confirmed in Georgia. Scorpion Files lists Georgia among the countries where this species occurs. According to the Georgian Biodiversity Database, it is native to Georgia. The database also lists several places in the country where it occurs.
  - question: Where is it confirmed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the map's “records by region” table, the status “Distribution
      confirmed” appears for Abkhazia, Adjara, and Imereti.

      Guria, Kakheti, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, and Racha — Lechkhumi — Kvemo
      Svaneti currently appear only from individual records.
  - question: How can I identify the Italian scorpion?
    answer: >-
      Euscorpius italicus is a relatively large Euscorpius-type scorpion with a
      dark body, strong pincers, and a short, slender tail. Colour and size alone
      are not enough for exact species identification; a sharp close-up photo and
      exact discovery location are recommended.
  - question: How dangerous is it to people?
    answer: >-
      The Italian scorpion is not considered a species of high medical risk.
      Available data indicate that the sting effect is mainly local, although a
      sting can be painful.

      Do not handle the scorpion. Seek timely medical advice for severe pain or a
      child who has been stung. Call 112 in Georgia for breathing difficulty, loss of
      consciousness, or other serious symptoms beyond the sting site. See the [guide](/scorpions/morielis-nakbeni) for first steps.
  - question: What should I do if I find one indoors?
    answer: >-
      If the scorpion must be moved, place a container over it without direct
      contact, carefully slide a stiff card underneath, and move it to a safe
      place. If this cannot be done safely, contact a specialist.

      Do not let children or pets touch the scorpion.
  - question: What does native mean here?
    answer: >-
      In the Georgian Biodiversity Database record, the origin status of
      Euscorpius italicus is listed as “native”, meaning the database treats it
      as part of Georgia's local fauna.

      This does not mean the species occurs in every region of Georgia. This page
      treats a region as distributed only when the regional map table marks it as
      “Distribution confirmed”.
