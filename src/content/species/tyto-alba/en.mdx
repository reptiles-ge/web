commonName: Western barn owl
location: Year-round resident in Georgia
description: >-
  A pale barn owl with a heart-shaped face and no ear tufts. Confirmed in Georgia
  as a year-round resident and breeder on the 2024 national bird checklist.
overview: >-
  The western barn owl (Tyto alba) is a medium-sized owl of the barn-owl family
  Tytonidae, not the typical-owl family Strigidae. The 2024 annotated checklist
  of the birds of Georgia codes it YR-R: a breeder present throughout the year.
  Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality is high
  (H), with photographs, ringing data and sound recordings. The usual Georgian
  name is ბუხრინწა. Adults are about 33–39 cm long at species level, with a
  heart-shaped facial disc and dark eyes. It is not a threat to people. IUCN
  lists the species as Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns LC globally and
  nationally. Georgia’s legal Red List (Resolution No. 190, 2014) still lists it
  as EN D1 (very small population); that assessment predates the recent
  lowland expansion documented in Georgian research.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia it is tied to lowland agricultural landscapes, farm buildings and
  open country with small mammals — not closed high mountain forest. Kitel and
  Javakhishvili (2025) collected pellets in 2023–2025 at 34 roosting or breeding
  sites in five administrative regions: Adjara, Imereti, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli
  and Samegrelo–Zemo Svaneti. Sampling was densest in Kakheti and Kvemo Kartli.
  Kitel, Songulashvili and Javakhishvili (2025) treat the species as having
  colonised Georgia in recent decades and expanded across much of the lowland
  agricultural zone within about 25 years. Earlier sources already noted
  establishment from the early 2000s. This page colours those five regions on
  the atlas map from pellet-site evidence; it does not claim presence in every
  municipality or in high alpine belts. Species-level habitat is farmland,
  grassland, marsh edge and open country with nest or roost sites in barns,
  ruins, church towers, bridges and other cavities; nest boxes are used where
  provided. A separate national elevation band for Georgia is not published
  here; the diet study’s sites were lowland.
diet: >-
  Georgian diet is documented. Kitel and Javakhishvili (2025) identified 6,627
  prey items from pellets. Mammals made up 98.6% of items. Rodents were about
  53% by number and 75% by biomass; shrews about 45% by number and 23% by
  biomass. Frequent mammalian prey included Macedonian mouse (Mus macedonicus),
  social vole (Microtus socialis) and white-toothed shrews (Crocidura). Birds,
  bats, amphibians, reptiles and invertebrates were rare. Geographic variation
  occurred; some sites were shrew-dominated. Indigestible parts are coughed up
  as pellets under roosts. The authors note a potential role in agricultural
  rodent control; that is an ecological inference from diet, not a measured
  farm trial on this page.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at night. By day it roosts in a cavity or quiet building.
  It does not give the deep hoot of woodland owls; the usual contact and flight
  call is a drawn-out, harsh screech or scream. Soft hisses and begging noises
  occur at the nest. Nesting is in a building cavity, tree hollow, cliff niche
  or nest box; no stick nest is built. Clutch size varies with food supply;
  species-level literature often cites about four white eggs as a typical
  average, with higher clutches when rodents are abundant. Incubation and nest
  care are mainly by the female while the male brings food. Those breeding
  figures are from the wider range, not a tabulated Georgian nest sample on this
  page. The 2024 checklist treats the species as a breeding resident in Georgia.
  Do not handle it; birds may strike near the nest.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Tyto alba as Least Concern on the IUCN Red
  List. The 2024 Georgian checklist assigns LC globally and nationally. The
  legal Georgian Red List of 2014 (Resolution No. 190) lists ბუხრინწა as EN D1
  because of a then very small population. Recent peer-reviewed work describes
  rapid lowland expansion since colonisation in the early 2000s; the legal list
  has not been updated on this page’s sources. Report both statuses rather than
  choosing one silently. Documented pressures at species level elsewhere include
  loss of nest sites in old buildings, road mortality and secondary poisoning
  from rodenticides; that is not a ranked national threat list for Georgia. It
  is listed on CITES Appendix II, so international trade is regulated. Do not
  catch wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and is harmless unless approached. It may strike near a
  nest. Wild birds must not be taken. Seeing one near farms is normal where it
  has established; it is a nocturnal predator of rodents and shrews, not a
  threat to people.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A pale medium-sized owl with a heart-shaped face, dark eyes and no ear
    tufts. Confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident on the 2024 checklist.
    The screech call and white underparts set it apart from woodland owls.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length at species level is about 33–39 cm, wingspan about 80–95 cm, mass
      often about 250–350 g (heavier in some subspecies). Females are usually
      slightly heavier than males. The head is smoothly rounded with no ear
      tufts. The facial disc is heart-shaped and usually pale; the eyes are
      dark. The bill is short and hooked. The legs are long; the tail is short
      and rather square. In flight the bird looks pale, with dangling legs and
      an undulating path.
    - >-
      Upperparts are grey-brown to buff with fine mottling; underparts are
      white to buff, sometimes with dark speckles. Georgian birds are not
      assigned to subspecies on this page; field identification is to species.
      Belik (2024) notes several western Palearctic forms expanding in northern
      Eurasia, including T. a. guttata, T. a. alba and T. a. erlangeri;
      visual subspecies ID is unreliable. Juveniles are fluffier and may look
      darker until they moult.
    - >-
      Tawny owl (Strix aluco) is a darker woodland owl with a round head, dark
      eyes and no heart-shaped face; it hoots. Long-eared owl (Asio otus) has
      ear tufts and orange eyes. Short-eared owl (Asio flammeus) is an
      open-country bird, often active by day, with short tufts. Eurasian
      eagle-owl (Bubo bubo) is much larger, with ear tufts and orange eyes.
      Little owl (Athene noctua) is small, with yellow eyes. Eurasian scops owl
      (Otus scops) is small, tufted, and a breeding migrant in Georgia.
      Tengmalm’s owl (Aegolius funereus) is a smaller forest owl with yellow
      eyes. This atlas has pages for tawny owl, scops owl, Tengmalm’s owl and
      little owl.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Do not handle it. A pale, heart-faced owl
      screeching at night over Georgian farmland or around old buildings is
      this species, not a woodland hooting owl.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Scopoli in 1769. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows IOC and
    the OSME Region List for names: order Strigiformes, family Tytonidae, genus
    Tyto. English name on the checklist is Western Barn Owl. American barn owl
    (Tyto furcata) and eastern barn owl (Tyto javanica) are treated as separate
    species in recent taxonomy; they are not on the Georgian list.
  - >-
    Georgian research treats the species as a recent colonist of the early
    2000s that has expanded across lowland farmland (Kitel et al. 2025;
    summarised again in Kitel and Javakhishvili 2025). Older regional notes
    (Bukreev 2003; Gálvez et al. 2005) already recorded presence. Do not read
    the 2014 EN D1 listing as proof that the bird is still as rare as it was
    when that list was written, and do not treat the 2024 checklist LC code as
    a legal delisting.
  - >-
    The Georgian common name is ბუხრინწა. English names in use are Western Barn
    Owl (IOC and the 2024 checklist) and barn owl. Older literature may say
    Common Barn Owl for the broader Tyto alba complex.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photo), RD (ringing) and SR (sound
    recording). Pellet sites in five regions add named Georgian localities
    beyond the country-level checklist.
faq:
  - question: Is western barn owl found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia treats it as a
      confirmed year-round resident (YR-R). Evidence includes photographs,
      ringing and sound recordings. Recent studies describe colonisation since
      the early 2000s and expansion in lowland farmland.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Look in lowland agricultural areas and around farm buildings, ruins and
      other cavities. Pellet sampling in 2023–2025 documented roosting or
      breeding sites in Adjara, Imereti, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli and
      Samegrelo–Zemo Svaneti. Do not assume even presence in high mountains.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Heart-shaped pale face, dark eyes, no ear tufts, pale underparts, long
      legs. At night listen for a harsh screech, not a deep woodland hoot.
      Tawny owl is darker and round-faced.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 33–39 cm long at species level, wingspan about 80–95 cm. There is
      no separate measured Georgian series on this page.
  - question: What does it eat in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Mainly small mammals. In a 2023–2025 pellet study, mammals were 98.6% of
      prey items; rodents dominated biomass, with shrews also common. Birds and
      other prey were rare.
  - question: When is it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year, according to the 2024 checklist. It is easiest to detect at
      night by voice or at dusk near nest and roost buildings.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means a breeding resident. Nests are in
      cavities in buildings or similar sites. Georgian clutch dates are not
      tabulated here.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous. It may strike near a nest; do not approach. Globally
      Least Concern. The 2024 checklist codes national status LC, while the
      2014 legal Red List still lists it EN D1. Do not catch wild birds.
