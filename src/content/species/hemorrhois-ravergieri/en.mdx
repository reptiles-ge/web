overview: >-
  The spotted whip snake (Hemorrhois ravergieri) is a harmless, blotched racer of
  dry rocky country in Georgia. It belongs to the family Colubridae. The 2026
  Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. The named locality is
  Aspindza, Tmogvi, in Samtskhe — Javakheti; COI barcodes come from there too.
  The 2021 Georgian snake survey places it among dry-climate species of rocky or
  grassland habitats, mostly in eastern Georgia, and notes a substantial gain in
  knowledge for this species. Do not expect it throughout the country. The wider
  range runs from the Caucasus into Central Asia. Across the species, adults may
  reach about 1–1.3 m; a separate Georgian length series is not published here.
  It is not dangerous to people — it has no venom. IUCN lists it as Least
  Concern. Older books often used Coluber ravergieri or Zamenis ravergieri; the
  modern genus is Hemorrhois.
habitat: >-
  The 2021 Georgian snake survey treats this species as dependent on a fairly
  dry climate: rocky or grassland settings, mostly in eastern Georgia. The 2026
  checklist does not name a habitat type. The figured specimen is from Aspindza,
  Tmogvi, in the rocky Mtkvari canyon. This atlas maps only Samtskhe —
  Javakheti, because no other named Georgian locality is given on this page. The
  2021 survey notes that some eastern colubrids have also been recorded in
  suitable western habitats and that knowledge of this species improved; named
  western or Colchic points are still not listed here. Across the range it uses
  dry and semi-arid slopes, canyons, and steppe; a separate Georgian elevation
  band has not been published. Outside Georgia the range includes the Caucasus,
  Anatolia, Iran, and Central Asia. The type locality is usually given as Baku,
  not Georgia.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study. Elsewhere in the range it takes
  lizards, small mammals, birds, and other snakes. It has no venom — it catches
  prey by speed. The Georgian diet has not been measured directly.
behavior: >-
  Across the range it is mainly active by day; it often basks and moves quickly.
  Hourly activity in Georgia has not been described separately. When threatened
  it usually tries to flee. Do not pick it up — it has no venom, but a large
  snake can bite. It lays eggs; outside Georgia the literature often cites 4–10
  eggs. Mating season and clutch size for Georgian animals have not been
  published here. In the same dry south you may also meet Dahl’s whip snake
  (Platyceps najadum), the Urartian ratsnake (Elaphe urartica), and, more
  rarely, the Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetina) — pattern, head shape, and
  pupil differ. Confusion with a viper is dangerous: vipers have a vertical
  pupil.
conservation: >-
  The 2017 IUCN Red List assessment is Least Concern — the range is wide. It is
  not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically
  confirmed; distribution confidence is medium. The Georgian distribution picture
  is still uneven: the named recent point is Tmogvi, and the 2021 survey notes a
  gain in knowledge but does not provide a full map. Local threats include
  habitat conversion, road mortality, and killing after confusion with venomous
  snakes. Do not handle it.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A slender, harmless racer with a round pupil and a blotched back. In Georgia
    the key points are dry rocky ground and the absence of a vertical pupil —
    this is not a viper and not a Levantine viper.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is slender and built for speed. The head is moderately distinct
      from the neck. The pupil is round. Dorsal scales are weakly keeled; there
      are often 21 rows at mid-body. Across the species, length may reach about
      1.3 m. Do not pick the snake up to count scales.
    - >-
      The ground colour is grey, brown, or sandy. The back has dark rhomboid or
      crossbar blotches and smaller spots on the sides. Toward the tail the
      blotches often join into lengthwise stripes. A dark diagonal streak sits
      below the eye; another runs from the eye toward the mouth corner. The
      belly is whitish, often with fine dark dots.
    - >-
      Dahl’s whip snake (Platyceps najadum) is thinner, with neck ocelli rather
      than rhombs along the whole back. The Urartian ratsnake (Elaphe urartica)
      is stouter, with a dark head and larger rounded blotches. The steppe
      ratsnake (Elaphe dione) has a finer pattern and often lengthwise stripes.
      Adult red-bellied racers (Dolichophis schmidti) are often plain coppery
      with a red belly. The smooth snake is smaller and has smooth scales.
    - >-
      A viper has a vertical pupil, a more triangular head, and strongly keeled
      scales; it is venomous. The Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetina) is
      heavier, with a broad head and a vertical pupil — medically significant. If
      a blotched snake has a vertical pupil, it is a viper, not this species.
      There is no venom. A bite can still hurt. Do not pick it up.    - >-
      The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. The
      named locality is Aspindza, Tmogvi, in Samtskhe — Javakheti. The 2021
      Georgian snake survey places it among dry-climate species of rocky or

      throughout the country.

faq:
  - question: Does the spotted whip snake occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. The named
      locality is Aspindza, Tmogvi. It does not occur throughout the country.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The named recent point is in Samtskhe — Javakheti, around Tmogvi. The 2021
      survey links it to dry rocky or grassland habitats, mostly in the east, and
      notes a gain in knowledge. This atlas maps only Samtskhe — Javakheti. Named
      western or Colchic points are not listed here.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. When threatened it usually flees. A large snake can
      bite — do not pick it up. A vertical pupil points to a viper or Levantine
      viper.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Slender build, round pupil, grey or brown ground with rhomboid blotches
      that often become stripes on the tail, and a dark streak by the eye.
      Dahl’s whip snake has neck ocelli, not rhombs along the back. Vipers and
      the Levantine viper have a vertical pupil.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Across the species, adults are about 1–1.3 m. A separate measured length
      for Georgian animals is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      It lays eggs; outside Georgia the literature often cites 4–10 eggs. Diet
      has not been studied separately in Georgia; elsewhere it takes lizards,
      small mammals, birds, and other snakes.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      Across the range it is mainly diurnal. A separate hourly and seasonal
      calendar for Georgia has not been published; expect the warm season in dry
      rocky country.
  - question: How does it differ from the Urartian ratsnake?
    answer: >-
      The spotted whip snake is more slender and fast; the blotches are often
      rhomboid and turn into stripes on the tail. The Urartian ratsnake is
      stouter, with a dark head and larger rounded blotches. Both are harmless.
