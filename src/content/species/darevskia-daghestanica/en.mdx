overview: >-
  The Dagestan lizard (Darevskia daghestanica) is a rock lizard in the true lizard family (Lacertidae). It has no venom and is harmless to people. According to published measurements, its head and body are about 4.4–5.8 cm long, and its tail is often longer than the head and body combined. Its presence in Georgia has been confirmed in the northeastern mountains. It is also listed as a confirmed species in a 2026 Georgian checklist. Recent records name Tusheti and Mount Tbatana in Akhmeta Municipality. It is not expected in the lowlands of western or southern Georgia. In the past, it was often considered a close relative of the Caucasian lizard (Darevskia caucasica) or one of its subspecies. It is now considered a separate species.
habitat: >-
  Two recent locations have been reported for this species in Georgia. A 2026 list includes genetic data from Tusheti and an image of the head scales of an individual found on Mount Tbatana in Akhmeta Municipality. Both places are in the mountains of Kakheti. The Kakheti marking on this atlas map refers only to these places. It does not mean the species occurs in the Alazani Valley or on Kakheti’s dry plains. The species is found in moderately dry, rocky mountain areas: on cliffs, slopes and in river valleys. Its main range lies on the northern slopes of the Greater Caucasus, from Dagestan to the Darial Gorge in North Ossetia. Farther south, it also occurs in Tusheti and Azerbaijan. In 1963, Darevsky described a separate population in the Greater Liakhvi Valley, above Java (Dzau), on the road to the Roki Pass. Its presence was confirmed again in 2021. This is a separate population to the southwest; its record does not apply to all of Shida Kartli, so the map does not show the species across the whole region. According to a 2026 discussion, the ranges of this species and caucasica connect without a gap in the upper Terek area, where they may interbreed. This identifies an area where they meet; it does not mean the species has been confirmed separately in every local community there. Published information places the caucasica group at roughly 500–3200 m. A measured elevation range for populations in Georgia has not been published separately. The species has only exceptionally been reported in forests without cliffs. Usually, look for it on cliffs or rocks.
diet: >-
  Stomach contents from Georgia have not been published for this species.
  Caucasian rock lizards in Darevsky's account feed mainly on small
  invertebrates — spiders, beetles, flies, grasshoppers, ants. The same is
  expected here, but a Georgian diet has not been measured.
behavior: >-
  This rock lizard is active during the day. Its flattened body helps it slip into crevices. It lives mainly on rocks but is also found away from them. It has also been recorded in forest where rocks were present. No separate information has been published on when it is active during the day or across the seasons in Georgia. It lays eggs. In inland Dagestan, females with swollen bellies were caught from mid to late May. They laid eggs from late May to mid-June, 1–5 at a time. Under artificial conditions, the eggs took 36–51 days to hatch. These dates were recorded in Dagestan and should not be treated as established dates for Tusheti. The lizard can shed its tail, so a short or regrown tail does not mean it is a different species.
conservation: >-
  According to the IUCN assessment published in 2009 (based on data through 2008), the species is considered of Least Concern globally: its population is probably large, and no rapid decline is apparent. It is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Decree No. 190); the Turkish, Dahl’s and Adjara lizards are the Darevskia species listed there. The 2026 list considers its presence confirmed by biological evidence, though information about its distribution is only moderately reliable. Large genetic differences have been described between groups living in different places, but the list does not use subspecies names. The isolated group in the Liakhvi area is found in a small area, so local threats there may differ. There is no separate national assessment. Do not take it from the wild.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and is harmless to people. Do not pick it up: its tail can break off easily, and handling stresses the animal. If you see one, photograph its back, side and the scales on its head. Note where you saw it and at what elevation, then leave it where it is. Colour alone cannot confirm the species, especially in Tusheti and near Tbatana, where the Caucasian lizard is also listed.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This is a small rock lizard (Darevskia) with a flattened head. In Georgia, it is found in the mountains of the northeast. Its color does not distinguish it from the Caucasus lizard.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is slender and flattened. The head is relatively narrow and flat, and the hind legs are long. According to Roitberg, these features can help distinguish it from caucasica where both occur, though not at a glance from a photo. The scales on its back are smooth or slightly raised. The Tbatana illustrations show the scales on the head and the plates in front of the rear opening; these details are often hard to see in photos taken in the wild.
    - >-
      The back is often grayish brown, yellowish brown, or ash gray. Two parallel rows of dark spots or a netlike pattern may run along the spine. Sometimes the pattern is faint or absent. The pale stripes on the sides often do not reach the scales on top of the head. Identification guides list this as one of the species’ features, but it does not appear in every individual. Color alone cannot distinguish Darevskia species.
    - >-
      It is small. One specimen from Levashi in Dagestan measures 49 mm from head to body. In inland Dagestan, larger females measure 43.95–56.20 mm from head to body. The tail is often longer than the head and body together, but the Levashi specimen’s tail is damaged, so its full length is unknown. Data on specimens from Georgia have not been published here.
    - >-
      The Caucasian lizard (D. caucasica) has a continuous range along the upper Terek River. It has also been reported near Tbatana. Color alone cannot distinguish caucasica from daghestanica. caucasica often has more distinct stripes along its sides. Where the two lizards meet, caucasica has more scales around the middle of its body and on its belly, and more pores on its thighs. Its head is narrower and flatter. In the wild, location is the most reliable clue: daghestanica has been reported in Tusheti, while caucasica has been reported in Truso and Zakagori. The Artvin lizard (D. derjugini) belongs to the same genus, but it lives on the ground in forests rather than on steep rock faces. The Adjarian lizard (D. mixta) is associated with areas in the west and center where species’ ranges meet; it is not a substitute for the Tusheti lizard.
    - >-
      The 2026 list considers this species biologically confirmed. Recent records name Tusheti (barcode) and Mount Tbatana in Akhmeta Municipality (figure). Do not expect to find it throughout the country: it is not expected in the western and southern lowlands. Color will not distinguish it from the Caucasian lizard (D. caucasica); in the field, location is the safe way to tell them apart.
faq:
  - question: Is it found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist treats the species as biologically confirmed. That
      does not mean it occurs throughout the country.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It is currently known from Tusheti and Mount Tbatana in Akhmeta Municipality. A separate group, isolated from the others, has also been described in the Greater Liakhvi valley above Java. Do not look for it on the western and southern plains.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. Do not pick it up — the tail breaks easily.
  - question: How do I tell it from the Caucasian rock lizard?
    answer: >-
      They cannot be told apart by color alone. You need to know where they were found and, if possible, count their scales. Tusheti is named as a location for this species, and Truso for caucasica. Both may occur near Tbatana.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      It is a small lizard. Published sources give a head-and-body length of about 4.4–5.8 cm. Specimens collected in Georgia were not measured here.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It probably eats small animals without backbones. No information about its stomach contents in Georgia has been published.
  - question: Is it on the Georgian Red List?
    answer: >-
      It is not listed in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Globally, it is classified as Least Concern (LC).
