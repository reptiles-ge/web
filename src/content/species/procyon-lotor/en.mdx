commonName: Raccoon
location: Introduced in Georgia
description: >-
  A medium-sized procyonid with a black facial mask and a ringed tail. It is
  not native to Georgia; it is introduced and established.
overview: >-
  The raccoon (Procyon lotor) is a mammal in the raccoon family that has been
  introduced and established in Georgia. It is especially well documented in
  Kakheti. Its clearest features are a black mask around the eyes and a ringed
  tail. It is not venomous, but it can bite, so do not touch it. The raccoon dog
  (Nyctereutes procyonoides) is a different species.
habitat: >-
  Raccoons often live in woodland and floodplains near water, but also enter
  gardens and the edges of settlements. There is no documented release in
  Georgia itself; raccoons likely spread from Azerbaijan and were reported
  near Lagodekhi by the 1950s. In Kakheti, they are known from Lagodekhi, the
  Alazani valley and Gombori.
  Photographs and camera-trap records also exist from Kvemo Kartli and near
  Tbilisi National Park. Their presence on the Abkhazian coast has been
  described; reported tracks in Adjara remain unconfirmed.
diet: >-
  Raccoons are omnivores: they eat invertebrates, frogs, fish, birds’ eggs and
  nestlings, fruit and nuts. They also use food waste near settlements. In
  Georgia, residents report damage to crops and poultry, but the scale of that
  damage has not been assessed.
behavior: >-
  Raccoons are mainly active at night and dusk, though they may appear by day.
  They swim and climb well. Tracks made by their five-toed forefeet resemble
  small handprints. They shelter in tree hollows, burrows, rock crevices or
  building lofts. They do not truly hibernate: in cold weather they move less
  and may stay in a den for long periods. Gestation lasts about 63 days. Young
  raccoons seen at Lagodekhi and a female photographed with three kits at
  Shuamta confirm breeding in Georgia.
conservation: >-
  A 2016 assessment rates the raccoon as Least Concern globally; it is not on
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List. In Georgia it is an introduced, established species
  regarded as invasive. It can eat birds’ eggs and nestlings or amphibians,
  though the scale of its ecological impact in Georgia has not been assessed.
  Do not take a wild raccoon home or release a pet raccoon into the wild.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom, but it can bite, especially if cornered or defending its
  young. Rabies can pass to a person through the bite of an infected animal or
  when its saliva reaches broken skin or a mucous membrane. Do not touch or
  feed a raccoon. If bitten, wash the wound immediately with soap and running
  water and seek medical care promptly. In an unprotected yard it may take
  poultry, eggs or fruit.

  summary: >-
    A medium-sized, short-legged omnivore with a black facial mask, a pointed
    muzzle, and a bushy tail with several dark rings. It is introduced in
    Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      Head–body length is about 40–70 cm and tail length 20–40 cm. Weight is
      often about 4–9 kg; males are usually slightly larger than females.
    - >-
      Fur is grey-brown or yellowish-grey, often with a paler belly. A black
      “mask” around the eyes has a pale border. The tail usually has 4–7 dark
      rings and a dark tip. The forefoot has five long toes and non-retractile
      claws. The ears are relatively small and the muzzle pointed.
    - >-
      The [badger](meles-canescens) (Meles canescens) is native in Georgia:
      more massive, with long black-and-white head stripes rather than a mask,
      and a short tail without rings. The raccoon dog (Nyctereutes
      procyonoides) is also introduced; it is short-legged and masked but lacks
      a ringed tail.

      Yes. It is introduced and established in Georgia, especially in the east.

      also been described.
  - question: How can it be identified?
