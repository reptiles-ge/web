commonName: Caucasian squirrel
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A medium-sized tree squirrel with a chestnut-grey back, a pale eye-ring, and a
  rusty or yellowish belly. It is a native, year-round resident in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian squirrel (Sciurus anomalus) is a tree squirrel native to Georgia
  and belongs to the squirrel family (Sciuridae). It is characterized by a
  chestnut-grey back, a pale ring around the eye, and a rusty or yellowish belly.

  Total length is about 32–36 cm, the tail is 13–18 cm, and mass is about 250–410
  g. It lives in Georgia year-round.

  IUCN lists it globally as Least Concern, but Georgia’s 2014 Red List includes it
  as Vulnerable (VU, A1e).
habitat: >-
  In Georgia the Caucasian squirrel is mainly associated with deciduous and mixed
  forests, especially where beech, chestnut, oak, and hazel occur. It may also
  appear in orchards and parks with large trees.

  Across the Caucasus the species is often linked with beech forests and is
  described up to about 2,000 m, although this is not a separately measured
  Georgian elevation range. It is generally less associated with conifer forest,
  though individual animals can still be recorded in such habitats.

  Specific Georgian records on this page come from Borjomi-Kharagauli National
  Park and Vake Park in Tbilisi. The historical type locality is linked to the
  Kutaisi area. A 2023 paper summarizes 71 records from Georgia collected between
  1855 and 2022.

  These data indicate broad occurrence, but they do not mean the species is
  equally dense in every region or in every suitable habitat across the country.
diet: >-
  This page does not present a separate quantitative study of the Caucasian
  squirrel’s diet from Georgia. Across its range, much of its food consists of
  tree seeds and fruits, including acorns, beechnuts, chestnuts, walnuts, and
  hazelnuts.

  Fruit, berries, mushrooms, and occasional animal food such as insects, eggs, or
  nestlings are also described. It stores some food in different caches. Some
  forgotten seeds may later germinate, so the squirrel also takes part in seed
  dispersal.

  The specific local diet in Georgia has not been separately measured by the
  sources used on this page.
behavior: >-
  The Caucasian squirrel is active by day and does not hibernate. It often moves
  in the morning and late afternoon, while activity may decrease around midday in
  hot periods. Compared with the Eurasian red squirrel, it also moves on the
  ground frequently.

  It often nests in tree hollows lined with moss and dry leaves. Nests are also
  described in other sheltered places, including among roots, rocks, and
  buildings.

  Caucasian sources describe two moults per year, in spring and autumn. The
  breeding period is broad, although dates described from Transcaucasia cannot be
  applied directly to every Georgian population. It usually gives birth to
  several young, which are cared for by the female.

  Its call is described as a sharp, metallic “chit-chit-chit”. The audio used on
  this page is from Turkey and was identified with incomplete certainty by the
  recordist; no Georgian audio recording is presented here.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assesses the Caucasian squirrel globally as Least Concern. The situation in
  Georgia is different: the 2014 national Red List places the species in the
  Vulnerable category (VU, A1e), where pressure from an introduced squirrel is
  listed as one reason.

  The introduced Eurasian red squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris) also occurs in Georgia,
  and in some places the ranges of the two species overlap. Forest loss and
  historical fur harvest are also mentioned as additional pressures.

  A current quantitative trend for the Georgian population has not been
  established by the sources used on this page. Global Least Concern status does
  not mean the species has no conservation need in Georgia.
interaction: >-
  The Caucasian squirrel is not venomous and is not a toxic hazard to touch. It
  can bite if handled or cornered, so there is no need to pick it up.

  Like any wild mammal, it could theoretically carry rabies, but Georgian
  literature more often names fox, jackal, wolf, and dogs as the main rabies
  sources, not the Caucasian squirrel. Do not feed or catch it.

  In some parts of the Caucasus the species enters hazelnut and fruit orchards,
  but this is a crop-damage issue, not a medical hazard to people.

    A medium-sized tree squirrel with a chestnut-grey back, a pale eye-ring, and
    a rusty or yellowish belly. It is a native, year-round resident in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      Total length is about 32–36 cm, the tail is 13–18 cm, and mass is about
      250–410 g. Males and females do not differ sharply in size or coloration.

      Small ear tufts may appear in winter, but they are usually much shorter
      than the well-developed winter ear tufts of the Eurasian red squirrel
      (Sciurus vulgaris). The individual photographed in Borjomi-Kharagauli in
      2015 on this page has short, rounded ears without long tufts.
    - >-
      The back is usually chestnut-grey. A pale buff or yellowish ring is often
      visible around the eye. The belly is rusty, chestnut, or yellowish and,
      unlike the Eurasian red squirrel, is not white.

      The tail is bushy, and its colour can vary from yellow-brown to dark
      reddish. Summer and winter coats do not differ sharply, although the belly
      may look rustier in winter.
    - >-
      The Eurasian red squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris) also occurs in Georgia and is
      considered introduced here. One of the clearest differences is belly
      colour: the Eurasian red squirrel usually has a white or cream belly, while
      the Caucasian squirrel has a rusty or yellowish belly.

      In winter, in places where both species occur, the Eurasian red squirrel
      often has longer ear tufts.

      The edible dormouse (Glis glis) is smaller, mainly nocturnal, and looks less
      like a typical tree squirrel.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. If you see a daytime squirrel in woodland or a city
      park with a rusty or yellowish belly and no long ear tufts, the Caucasian
      squirrel is one of the main likely species. For exact identification, do
      not rely on a single feature, and do not pick the animal up.
facts:
  - >-
    Güldenstädt described it in 1785. The accepted name is Sciurus anomalus.
    Kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Rodentia, family
    Sciuridae, genus Sciurus. The authority is sometimes wrongly given as J. F.
    Gmelin; Koprowski and co-authors treat Güldenstädt, 1785 as correct. Sciurus
    persicus has been misapplied; that name traces to a dormouse description, not
    this squirrel.
  - >-
    Three subspecies are widely listed: S. a. anomalus (Transcaucasia, Turkey,
    Lesbos), S. a. syriacus (Levant), and S. a. pallescens (Iraq and Zagros
    Iran). Georgian animals belong with the nominate form. Field identification
    in Georgia is to species.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual name is კავკასიური ციყვი; სპარსული ციყვი is also used.
    In English: Caucasian squirrel or Persian squirrel; also golden squirrel,
    Transcaucasian squirrel, or Georgian squirrel in older literature. Generic
    ციყვი means squirrel.
