commonName: Southern white-breasted hedgehog
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A small spiny mammal with a short tail and a white patch on the chest. It is
  native and resident in Georgia. The accepted name is Erinaceus concolor, not
  E. europaeus.
overview: >-
  The hedgehog in Georgia is the southern white-breasted hedgehog (Erinaceus
  concolor), a mammal in the hedgehog family (Erinaceidae). In Georgian it is
  often called ევროპული ზღარბი; that name historically belonged to the western
  European hedgehog Erinaceus europaeus, which IUCN and ASM do not list for
  Georgia. Kandaurov, Bukhnikashvili, Sheklashvili and Natradze 2023 name only
  one Erinaceus among Georgia’s insectivores: E. concolor. At species level
  head–body length is about 20–26 cm, the tail 2–3 cm, and mass often about
  550–800 g; some individuals are heavier. It has no venom. The main human risk
  is spines and a bite if handled, not poison. IUCN lists it as Least Concern.
  It is not treated on this page as a threatened taxon on Georgia’s 2014 Red
  List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in gardens, parks, farmland edges, scrub, and deciduous
  woodland edge where there is ground cover and invertebrates. Species-level
  habitat is broad: deciduous forest, scrub, grassland, farms, parks, and alpine
  ground below the tree line; dense conifer forest is often avoided if litter
  cover is poor. That is a species-level picture, not a measured Georgian
  elevation belt. This page does not colour administrative regions. A named
  point is Orkhevi, Tbilisi, 5 April 2023 (photograph by David Osipov on this
  page). Kandaurov and co-authors 2023 compile 112 Georgian records from
  1864–2022 (41 literature, 37 collections, 34 field) without reprinting the
  gazetteer here; the map is in that paper. Dinesh 2017 names Mtirala National
  Park as a good place for this hedgehog; that is a trip report, not a
  peer-reviewed paper. GBIF also aggregates iNaturalist and other citizen-science
  points, including clusters in Tbilisi, Kakheti, Adjara, Guria and Imereti;
  those are not treated here as a verified municipality list. Do not assume even
  density across the country.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range the
  main food is ground invertebrates: beetles, earthworms, slugs, and snails. It
  occasionally takes frogs, eggs, carrion, or other small vertebrates. It is not
  a specialist on one prey group. The realistic picture in Georgia is garden and
  woodland-edge invertebrates, but the local menu has not been measured here.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at night. By day it hides in a nest of leaves and grass
  under a bush, roots, or other cover. Species-level accounts also describe
  burrow nests; some secondary sources say this species never digs. Those two
  pictures disagree; this page does not choose one as a national rule. In the
  colder parts of the range it hibernates in the cold season; start and end
  dates have not been measured for Georgia on this page. It is usually solitary
  except when breeding or when a female has young. When threatened it rolls into
  a ball so that the soft belly and face are covered by spines. It sometimes
  covers itself with saliva (self-anointing); the function of that behaviour is
  not fully settled. Breeding is in the warm months; litter size in the
  literature is from a few young to about seven. Those figures are regional or
  species-level, not a Georgian den sample. There is no Georgian recording on
  this page.
conservation: >-
  Amori, Hutterer, Kryštufek, Yiğit, Mitsainas and Palomo assess Erinaceus
  concolor as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (2021 publication; the
  assessment updates 2016). The global range runs from Anatolia through the
  southern Caucasus to the Levant. Eliava et al. 2007 list it as LC in Georgia.
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution No. 190) is not treated on this
  page as listing it as a threatened taxon. Named pressures at species level
  include road mortality, habitat loss, and pesticides; a national trend is not
  measured on this page. Do not handle wild animals or keep them as pets.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is not poisonous to touch. The spines are a physical
  defence, not a venomous weapon. It can bite if handled or if a dog or cat
  corners it. Any wild mammal can theoretically carry rabies; Georgian literature
  names fox, jackal, wolf, and dogs as the main sources, not this species. Do
  not feed or pick it up. It often appears in gardens and eats invertebrates;
  that is useful in a garden if strong pesticides are not used. Pet “hedgehogs”
  are usually an African species (Atelerix), not this taxon.

  traits:
    - >-
      At species level head–body is about 20–26 cm, the tail 2–3 cm, and mass
      often about 550–800 g. There is no separate measured Georgian series on
      this page. The snout is pointed, the ears short, the eyes small. The back
      and flanks are covered with smooth spines; the face, legs, and belly are
      furred. The tail is short and often hidden among the spines.
    - >-
      There is a white patch on the chest that contrasts with a darker abdomen.
      That is the main field mark against the western European hedgehog E.
      europaeus, which has a brown or more uniform chest, not a clear white
      patch. The Orkhevi photograph of 5 April 2023 shows a spiny back and
      night-time activity near housing; the chest patch is not fully readable in
      that frame, so species identity here also rests on taxonomy and range, not
      on that single photo alone.
    - >-
      Kandaurov et al. 2023 name only one hedgehog in Georgia. IUCN also lists
      the northern white-breasted hedgehog (E. roumanicus) as native in Georgia;
      that does not match the Georgian insectivore checklist. Both have a white
      chest and are hard to tell apart in the field. This page follows the 2023
      insectivore paper for Georgia (E. concolor) and does not hide the
      disagreement. Western European E. europaeus does not occur in Georgia. The
      Indian crested porcupine Hystrix indica appears on some mammal lists; it
      is much larger, with long quills, and does not roll into a ball like a
      hedgehog. This atlas has no porcupine or E. europaeus page yet.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. A small spiny animal with a short tail, active at
      night in a Georgian garden or at a road edge, is this species if the
      identification is to Erinaceus. Do not handle it to check the chest.
facts:
  - >-
    W. C. Martin described it; the name was published in 1838 (1837 is also
    cited because it appeared in the 1837 volume of Proc. Zool. Soc. Lond.). The
    type locality is near Trabzon, Turkey. The accepted name is Erinaceus
    concolor. Order Eulipotyphla (Insectivora or Erinaceomorpha in older
    systems), family Erinaceidae, genus Erinaceus.
  - >-
    It was formerly included in E. europaeus. White-breasted forms were later
    split into a southern species (concolor) and a northern one (roumanicus).
    The Caucasus is a barrier in that split (Seddon et al. 2002). Historical
    names include E. europaeus transcaucasicus Satunin and E. ponticus Satunin;
    they are now treated as synonyms of E. concolor. Field identification in
    Georgia is to species if you accept a one-species checklist.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual name is ევროპული ზღარბი; official lists also use
    აღმოსავლეთევროპული ზღარბი; თეთრმკერდა ზღარბი is also used. In English:
    southern white-breasted hedgehog; also eastern hedgehog. Generic ზღარბი
    means hedgehog.
  - >-
    IUCN includes Georgia in the range of E. concolor. It is on Bukhnikashvili
    and Kandaurov’s 2002 annotated list. The 2023 compilation has 112 Georgian
    records. That is country-level confirmation, not a full regional map.

      is historical.
