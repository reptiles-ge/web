description: >-
  A harmless treefrog of most of Georgia. In the dry far south-east it is
  replaced by the yellow-lemon treefrog.
overview: >-
  The eastern treefrog (Hyla orientalis) is a harmless treefrog found through
  most of Georgia, except the dry far south-east. Adults are usually about
  3–5 cm from snout to vent. It is not dangerous to people. Look for it in
  open woodland, shrub, gardens and near water — not in the Vashlovani
  steppe. It is common in the Colchic lowlands. Named sites include Batumi,
  Poti, Kutaisi, Borjomi, Tbilisi, Kvareli and Lagodekhi. The 2026 Georgian
  checklist treats it as a distinct species; DNA samples are from Chili Lake
  in Tbilisi and the Tskordza area, and the checklist figure is Gaghma Dvabzu
  in Guria. In the far south-east a treefrog is usually the yellow-lemon
  treefrog (Hyla savignyi).
  Taxonomic note: older books often listed it as Hyla arborea or Hyla arborea
  schelkownikowi. The checklist treats it as a candidate species — the split
  from European H. arborea rests mainly on mitochondrial DNA and still needs
  genomic checks. That does not mean it is rare in Georgia. The yellow-lemon
  treefrog is biologically confirmed in the checklist. In eastern Georgia the
  two treefrogs meet, including around Telavi; gene flow across that contact
  zone is not fully resolved.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia this is a treefrog of woodland and humid country, not of dry
  steppe. Look for it in open broadleaf or mixed forest, shrub, gardens and
  near water — ponds, pools, lake edges or slow streams. It usually avoids
  dense dark forest. The 1996 review places it in forested and farmed
  landscapes, especially orchards, across almost all of Georgia except the far
  south-east. Named sites include the Black Sea coast (Batumi, Kobuleti,
  Poti), Kutaisi, the Borjomi–Bakuriani area, the Tbilisi district (Lisi Lake,
  Kojori, Tskneti), Kvareli and Lagodekhi. The 2026 DNA samples are from Chili
  Lake (Tbilisi) and the Tskordza area; the figure locality is Gaghma Dvabzu
  in Guria. In Lower Svaneti around Lentekhi, breeding calls were recorded in
  2022 and the authors treat the frogs as H. orientalis on call and range.
  This atlas marks every region, including Kakheti, where the contact zone
  lies; in the dry Iori valley and Vashlovani a treefrog is more likely H.
  savignyi. The Telavi population shows mixed characters and is not treated
  here as pure either species. The upper elevation in Georgia is not
  established on this page. Do not expect it on the alpine belt of the Greater
  Caucasus. The range continues in the North Caucasus, Anatolia and around the
  Black Sea.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. At species level,
  adults take invertebrates, mainly insects. Tadpoles eat algae and plant
  debris.
behavior: >-
  By day it often sits on leaves or shrubs, or hides. Sticky pads on the toe
  tips let it climb vegetation. It is more active at dusk and at night. It
  breeds in small, mostly still water with dense herb cover. In the Borjomi
  canyon it belongs to the open-country breeding group. Breeding males call;
  the call differs from that of the yellow-lemon treefrog. In Lower Svaneti
  calling was recorded in spring and increased toward late May — that is one
  local observation, not a national calendar. A full seasonal calendar for
  Georgia is not given here. If you find one, leave it where it is. Do not
  pick it up or move it between water bodies. Wash your hands after any
  contact. It is not a medical risk to people.
conservation: >-
  The IUCN Red List assesses it as Least Concern. It is not on Georgia’s 2014
  Red List. Local numbers can still fall if breeding pools dry or become
  polluted. Candidate status in the checklist is a taxonomic note, not a
  conservation category. The contact zone with H. savignyi in eastern Georgia
  is not fully studied.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small, smooth-skinned treefrog with sticky pads on the toe tips. In
    Georgia it is most often confused with the yellow-lemon treefrog; place is
    often more reliable than colour.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is short and the skin is smooth. Rounded sticky pads on the
      toe tips separate a treefrog from marsh frogs and toads. Adults are
      usually about 3–5 cm from snout to vent.
    - >-
      The back is usually green, but it can turn grey or brown. Colour changes
      with temperature and background. The belly is usually pale — white or
      yellowish. A dark side stripe often forms a loop (hip sinus) at the
      groin; that stripe alone is unreliable, because some eastern treefrogs
      lack the loop and some yellow-lemon treefrogs show one.
    - >-
      The eyes are large and the pupil is horizontal. The eardrum is visible
      and smaller than the eye. Males have a vocal sac on the throat. These
      marks help, but they are not enough on their own to split the two
      Georgian treefrogs.
    - >-
      The yellow-lemon treefrog (Hyla savignyi) is the same genus. It lives in
      drier, less forested country south-east of Tbilisi. The eastern treefrog
      occupies most of the rest of Georgia — woodland and more humid ground.
      The breeding call differs. In the contact zone, including around Telavi,
      appearance can be intermediate.
    - >-
      The marsh frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) is larger, stays in water and
      lacks sticky toe pads. Toads have warty skin and do not climb like
      treefrogs.
faq:
  - question: Does the eastern treefrog occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist treats it as a species. DNA samples are from
      Tbilisi and Tskordza. The figure is Gaghma Dvabzu.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In most regions — from Colchis through Kartli to the more humid parts of
      Kakheti. Do not expect it in the Vashlovani steppe or the dry Iori
      valley; that is yellow-lemon treefrog country.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and is harmless to people. Do not pick it up. Wash
      your hands after any contact.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A small green or grey frog with smooth skin and sticky pads on the toes.
      In Georgia, place matters: this is the woodland and humid-country
      treefrog.
  - question: How does it differ from the yellow-lemon treefrog?
    answer: >-
      Both are treefrogs. Colour is not reliable. Range is more useful: this
      species occupies most of Georgia; the yellow-lemon treefrog is the dry
      country south-east of Tbilisi. In the contact zone they can look alike.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adults are usually about 3–5 cm from snout to vent. Separate Georgian
      measurements are not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-

      diet study on this page. Tadpoles eat algae.
  - question: Why is it a candidate species?
    answer: >-
      The checklist lists it as a separate species, but the split from H.
