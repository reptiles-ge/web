commonName: European turtle dove
location: Breeding migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A small migratory dove with chestnut-and-black scaly upperparts and a
  black-and-white striped neck patch. Confirmed in Georgia as a breeding
  migrant on the 2024 national bird checklist; Vulnerable globally and
  nationally on that list.
overview: >-
  The European turtle dove (Streptopelia turtur) is a small dove that occurs in
  Georgia as a breeding migrant and passage bird. It does not remain in the
  country during the non-breeding season. Adult body length is about 26–28 cm.
  It is harmless to people.

  IUCN lists the European turtle dove as Vulnerable. The 2024 Georgian bird
  checklist also gives it VU status both globally and nationally. It is not
  included on Georgia's 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, the European turtle dove occurs in the breeding season and during
  spring and autumn passage; it is not a typical winter bird for the country.

  It chooses open and semi-open places: woodland edges, scrub, tall hedges,
  gardens, farmland and bushy river valleys. The nest is usually a platform of
  thin twigs in a bush or low tree.

  Batumi is a well-documented migration point in Georgia. The species has also
  been recorded on passage in the Kolkheti area. Breeding is confirmed at
  country level, but this page does not have a full list of regions and
  municipalities. Therefore, marking only Adjara on the map does not mean the
  European turtle dove occurs only there in Georgia.
diet: >-
  The European turtle dove feeds mainly on plant food taken from the ground,
  especially small seeds of weeds and cereal plants. It also sometimes eats
  invertebrates.

  Both parents feed chicks with a nutritious crop secretion known as crop milk.
  This is general species biology; its diet in Georgia has not been studied in
  detail on this page.
behavior: >-
  The European turtle dove is active by day. Its characteristic voice is a soft,
  rhythmic “turr-turr” purr, often given from a tree or bush.

  Flight is fast and direct. In flight, the white edges of the dark tail are
  easy to see. The nest is a light platform made from thin twigs. At species
  level, females usually lay two white eggs; incubation lasts about 13–14 days
  and both parents take part. Young leave the nest after about two weeks. These
  data come from the broader species range, not from a dedicated study of
  Georgian nests.

  The European turtle dove is a long-distance migrant and winters mainly in the
  Sahel belt of Africa. Georgia is part of its broader migration corridor.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists the European turtle dove as Vulnerable. Population decline is
  especially well documented in Europe. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist also
  gives the species VU status globally and nationally, although it is not
  included on Georgia's 2014 Red List.

  Pressures named for the species include excessive harvest along migration
  routes, loss of shelterbelts and other suitable habitats, and changes in
  farmland landscapes. In other countries, reduced seed food, disease and
  hunting are also considered important problems.

  Streptopelia turtur is not included on Georgia's current published hunting
  season list; Eurasian collared dove (Streptopelia decaocto) is listed there
  as a separate species.
interaction: >-
  The European turtle dove does not pose a threat to people. Do not catch wild
  birds, take them home, or disturb nests. This species is not included on the
  current published hunting season list. Do not confuse it with the Eurasian
  collared dove.

  summary: >-
    The European turtle dove is a small migratory dove. Easy field marks are the
    chestnut-and-black scaly back and the black-and-white striped patches on the
    sides of the neck. Its voice is a soft, repeated “turr-turr” purr.

    In Georgia, the European turtle dove is a breeding migrant. One of its most
    common lookalikes is the Eurasian collared dove.
  traits:
    - >-
      Adult European turtle doves are about 26–28 cm long, with a wingspan of
      about 47–53 cm and a mass often around 100–170 g. These are general
      species data; this page does not have a separate measurement series for
      Georgian populations.

      The back is chestnut and black with a scaly pattern. The head and
      underparts are grey or pinkish-grey, and the breast often shows a light
      pink wash. Each side of the neck has a black-and-white striped patch.
      Adults show a reddish ring around the eye. The dark tail has a white tip
      and sides, especially visible in flight.
    - >-
      Young European turtle doves are duller than adults and do not yet have the
      clear black-and-white neck patch. Males and females look similar in the
      field.

      The song is a soft, repeated “turr-turr” purr. The call of the Eurasian
      collared dove is different. European turtle doves often feed on the ground
      in open places, and a sharp wing sound may be heard when they take off.
    - >-
      Eurasian collared dove (Streptopelia decaocto) occurs in Georgia year
      round. It is paler and more uniform grey-buff, with a single black
      half-collar on the nape, and it lacks the chestnut-and-black scaly back of
      the European turtle dove.

      Oriental turtle dove (Streptopelia orientalis meena) occurs in Georgia on
      migration. It is larger and darker, with a different neck pattern.
      Laughing dove (Spilopelia senegalensis) is smaller and warmer pink-brown,
      with a spotted neck pattern. Common woodpigeon (Columba palumbus) is much
      larger and has clear white wing bars.
    - >-
      The European turtle dove is harmless to people. In Georgia in late spring
      and summer, if you see a small dove with a chestnut-and-black scaly back
      and a soft purring voice, pay attention to those characters. The paler
      Eurasian collared dove common in towns is a different species.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Columba turtur. It is now placed in
    Streptopelia (Bonaparte, 1855). The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the IOC
    World Bird List and the OSME Region List for names: order Columbiformes,
    family Columbidae, genus Streptopelia. English checklist name is European
    Turtle Dove.
  - >-
    Four subspecies are widely recognised: S. t. turtur (Europe east through
    Asia Minor toward western Siberia), S. t. arenicola (North Africa and from
    Iraq–Iran east into Central Asia), plus more local Saharan and Nile forms
    (hoggara, rufescens). Handbook ranges place both turtur and arenicola in
    the broader Caucasus–Near East region. Field identification in Georgia is
    to species. A measured Georgian specimen series assigned to subspecies is
    not given on this page.
  - >-
    Georgian is გვრიტი; ჩვეულებრივი გვრიტი appears in official conservation
    documents. English names in use are European turtle dove, European
    turtle-dove, and turtle dove. Russian is обыкновенная горлица.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: BB; M, data quality A, global and national Red List
    VU, occurrence quality H, evidence PH; CH (photograph and checklist). The
    Iliauni SPA country checklist also codes BB, M. That is country-level
    confirmation, not a complete regional map. Collared dove, oriental turtle
    dove, and laughing dove are listed as separate species on the same
    checklist.
faq:
  - question: Is the European turtle dove found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The European turtle dove (Streptopelia turtur) is a confirmed
      breeding migrant and passage species in Georgia. In the non-breeding
      season, including winter, it usually does not remain in the country.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the warm season, look for the European turtle dove in open and
      semi-open places: scrub, woodland edges, hedges and near seed-rich
      farmland.

      Batumi is a confirmed migration point, and the species has also been
      recorded on passage in the Kolkheti area. Its presence and breeding in
      Georgia are confirmed at country level.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      To identify the European turtle dove, look for the chestnut-and-black
      scaly back, black-and-white striped patches on the sides of the neck, the
      white-edged dark tail and the soft “turr-turr” purr.

      Eurasian collared dove is paler and more uniform, has a single black
      half-collar on the nape, and lacks the chestnut-and-black scaly back.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      Adult European turtle doves are about 26–28 cm long, with a wingspan of
      about 47–53 cm. This page does not have a complete separate measurement
      dataset for Georgian populations.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      The European turtle dove feeds mainly on plant seeds and grain taken from
      the ground. Both parents feed chicks with a nutritious secretion produced
      in the crop. Its diet in Georgia has not been studied in detail on this
      page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, the European turtle dove occurs in the breeding season and on
      spring and autumn passage; it usually does not remain in winter. A
      September–October 2016 count in Batumi confirms autumn migration. Exact
      arrival and departure dates for Georgia are not defined on this page.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The European turtle dove is a confirmed breeding species in Georgia.
      This page does not have a full list of specific Georgian nesting sites.
      Records from the discussed Batumi and Kolkheti areas list the species
      there as a migrant, not specifically as breeding at those sites.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      The European turtle dove is harmless to people. IUCN lists it as globally
      Vulnerable, and the 2024 Georgian checklist also gives it national VU
      status. It is not included on Georgia's 2014 Red List.

      Streptopelia turtur is not on the current published hunting season list.
      Do not confuse it with the Eurasian collared dove, which is listed there
      separately. Do not catch or hunt wild turtle doves.
