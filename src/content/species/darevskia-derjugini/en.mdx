overview: >-
  Derjugin's lizard (Darevskia derjugini) is a small, non-venomous,
  ground-dwelling forest lizard in western Georgia and the forest belt of the
  east. It belongs to the true lizards (Lacertidae). It is harmless to people:
  it has no venom. Snout–vent length is usually about 4–6.5 cm; the tail is
  often nearly twice as long. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as
  biologically confirmed — it stays distinct even where other Darevskia occur.
  Named modern points include Lagodekhi, Sioni, Mulakhi, Racha, Baniskhevi and
  Kintrishi. IUCN assessed it as Near Threatened in 2009. Do not look for it
  across the whole country: dry plains and open steppe are not expected habitat.
habitat: >-
  You are likely to meet it in deciduous or coniferous forest, at edges,
  clearings, roadsides, stream or river banks, and other open patches — on the
  ground among grass and roots, not on vertical rock. Classic keys also note
  animals moving 100–150 m from forest into meadow. The 2026 checklist
  summarises the range as all of western Georgia and the forest belt of eastern
  Georgia. Named COI barcodes are from Lagodekhi, Sioni, Mulakhi (Mestia
  municipality), Racha, Baniskhevi and Kintrishi; the head-scale figure is from
  Mulakhi. This atlas map shows regional records; it does not mean the species
  is evenly present in every marked region. Literature records it from sea level
  in Adjara, from about 700 m in the Lagodekhi–Zaqatala area, and up to about
  1,800 m on the Trialeti range. The IUCN band of 600–1,700 m does not fully
  cover those low coastal records. It has been noted as sheltering in some
  Greater Caucasus caves; that does not make it a cave species.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study for this species. Classic keys treat
  it as a predator of small invertebrates — insects, spiders and similar prey.
  It has no venom; it seizes prey in the jaws.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day: it basks and moves quickly at forest edges. A
  hour-by-hour calendar for Georgia has not been published; activity is expected
  in the warm season in forest. It lays eggs. IUCN reports a typical clutch of
  4–8 eggs. In north-eastern Turkey (Artvin area) animals mature at about 2–3
  years; females there were on average larger, and lifespan was about 6–7 years.
  Do not treat those figures as Georgian averages. When threatened it usually
  flees; it may shed the tail — that is defence, not venom.
conservation: >-
  IUCN listed it as Near Threatened in 2009, mainly because of forest habitat
  loss and fragmentation. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. The 2026
  checklist treats it as biologically confirmed; distribution confidence in this
  atlas is medium. For Georgian populations the checklist names subspecies
  abchasica, barani, orlowae and nominotypical derjugini. In Turkey, barani has
  been synonymised with the nominate form; that decision is not automatically
  applied in Georgia. Local threats include logging, roads and conversion of
  forest edge. Do not collect animals.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is medically harmless to people. It may bite if handled;
  the bite can hurt but is not venomous. Photograph from a distance and leave
  it in place.

  summary: >-
    A small, ground-dwelling forest lizard with a head that is not flattened.
    Among Darevskia, colour is not enough — use habitat, head shape and range.
  traits:
    - >-
      The head is not flattened like that of rock Darevskia. Dorsal scales are
      rounded, smooth or weakly keeled toward the rear. Femoral pores are few
      (often about 6–12) and do not reach the knee. Do not handle the lizard to
      count scales.
    - >-
      The back is brown, olive or reddish-brown; pale flank stripes are common.
      Some animals have faint pale rings on the sides. Males often have a
      greenish belly with blue spots on the outer ventral plates; females are
      yellowish or whitish below. In juveniles the rear half of the tail is often
      turquoise-blue above. Colour does not separate species — it varies even
      within one population.
    - >-
      Meadow lizards (Darevskia praticola and D. pontica) are also
      ground-dwellers but different lineages: praticola in eastern and central
      Georgia, pontica in the north-western Pontic area. Rock Darevskia (for
      example D. mixta, D. brauneri, D. adjarica) may share a valley but live on
      rock and have a flatter head. The sand lizard (Lacerta agilis) is larger
      and differently patterned. The Colchic slow worm (Anguis colchica) has no
      legs.
    - >-
      The 2026 checklist’s Georgian name is კავკასიური ტყის ხვლიკი (Caucasian
      forest lizard); this atlas keeps Derjugin’s lizard / Artvin lizard — the
      type locality is Artvin in north-eastern Turkey. Described in 1898
      (Nikolsky) as Lacerta derjugini. Genus Darevskia since 1997. D. mixta was
      once thought to be a hybrid of derjugini and a rock lizard; it is now a
      separate species.    - >-
      The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. Named
      modern points include Lagodekhi, Sioni, Mulakhi, Racha, Baniskhevi and
      Kintrishi. Do not look for it across the whole country: dry plains and
      open steppe are not expected habitat. Rock Darevskia may occur in the
      same gorge but are on rock, with a flatter head.

faq:
  - question: Is Derjugin’s lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. It occurs in
      western Georgia and the forest belt of the east. It is not nationwide.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      At forest edges, clearings, roadsides and streams — in the west and in the
      eastern forest belt. Named modern points include Lagodekhi, Sioni,
      Mulakhi, Racha, Baniskhevi and Kintrishi. Do not search dry plains or open
      steppe.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and is not a medical risk. It usually flees. It may
      bite if handled — do not pick it up.
  - question: How do I tell it from a rock lizard or a meadow lizard?
    answer: >-
      This species lives on the ground in forest, with a head that is not
      flattened. Rock Darevskia are on rock and have a flatter head. Meadow
      lizards (praticola / pontica) are also terrestrial but different lineages
      and regions. Colour will not separate them.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Snout–vent length is usually about 4–6.5 cm; the tail is often nearly
      twice as long. A Georgia-specific measured maximum is not given on this
      page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia. Classic sources list small
      invertebrates — insects and spiders.
  - question: When is it active, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      It is active by day in the warm season. A precise Georgian calendar has
      not been published. It lays eggs; species-level accounts often give 4–8
      eggs.
  - question: What should I do if I find one?
    answer: >-
      Watch from a distance, take a photo and leave it. Do not handle it. If the
      lizard is on vertical rock with a flattened head, it is another Darevskia,
      not necessarily this species.
