commonName: "Marsh Frog"
location: "near water throughout Georgia"
description: >-
  A harmless water frog. It is Georgia's most widespread amphibian; look for it
  at marshes, canals, lakes and slow streams.
overview: >-
  The marsh frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) is a widespread water frog in Georgia.
  It belongs to the true frogs (Ranidae). At species level, adult body length is
  usually about 6–12 cm, and females are often larger than males. This page does
  not have a complete measured series for Georgia.

  The species is not venomous to people. Globally, IUCN lists it as Least
  Concern. In Georgia it occurs in ponds, lakes, canals, puddles, slow streams,
  and other water bodies, including in settled places.

  Older sources may use the name Rana ridibunda. The current Georgian checklist
  uses Pelophylax ridibundus.
habitat: >-
  The marsh frog is closely tied to water. It occurs in canals, streams,
  puddles, ponds, and lakes. It is especially common on open, well-warmed banks
  with vegetation cover.

  The species also occurs in village and city water bodies. In Georgia it is
  recorded from lowlands and mountain areas, though it is rare in the high
  subalpine and alpine belts of the Greater Caucasus.

  Some sources record it above 2,500 m above sea level. That is a broad-range
  datum and does not mean the species is common everywhere at that elevation.
diet: >-
  The marsh frog's diet in Georgia has not been fully studied separately. At
  species level, adults mainly catch aquatic and shoreline invertebrates,
  especially insects.

  Large individuals may sometimes eat another amphibian, a small fish, or
  another small vertebrate. Tadpoles feed mainly on algae, detritus, and small
  invertebrates.
behavior: >-
  By day it often sits at the water's edge or on the water surface. When
  threatened, it quickly jumps into the water. It is most active in the warm
  season.

  The breeding period changes with place and elevation. In the Tbilisi lowlands,
  males may already be calling in late March, and egg-laying has been recorded
  from spring into autumn. In Borjomi Canyon and the southern highlands, the
  season starts later and is shorter. These are data from individual places, not
  one national calendar for all of Georgia.

  Males have a pair of vocal sacs at the corners of the mouth, and their calls
  can be heard from far away. If you meet a frog, leave it in place and do not
  move it between water bodies.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists the marsh frog globally as Least Concern. It is not included in
  Georgia's 2014 Red List.

  It also occurs in settled places and tolerates some environmental change, but
  local populations can be harmed if a water body dries, banks are destroyed,
  or breeding sites are lost.

  There are different taxonomic approaches to Pelophylax bedriagae. In this
  atlas, Pelophylax ridibundus is used for Georgian populations.
interaction: >-
  The marsh frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) is not venomous to people and usually
  poses no danger. When a person approaches, it often jumps into the water and
  avoids contact.

  There is no need to catch a wild frog or move it elsewhere. If you touch one,
  wash your hands well with soap and water, and avoid touching your eyes or
  mouth with unwashed hands. Moving it from one water body to another is not
  recommended because it may help diseases spread between amphibian populations.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium to large water frog with smooth skin, a horizontal pupil, and long
    hind legs. It has no sticky toe pads. In Georgia it is most often confused
    with the long-legged wood frog.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is stocky, the hind legs are long, and the toes are webbed. The
      toe tips lack sticky discs, which helps separate it from treefrogs.

      At species level, adult marsh frogs are usually about 6–12 cm from snout
      to vent. Females are often larger than males. This page does not have a
      complete measured dataset for Georgia.
    - >-
      The back may be green, olive, grey, or brownish and often has dark spots.
      Many individuals also have a pale stripe down the middle of the back. The
      belly is pale and may have dark spots.

      Colour is quite variable, so identifying the species from green colour
      alone is not reliable.
    - >-
      The pupil is horizontal, the eardrum is clearly visible, and two distinct
      skin folds run along the sides of the back. Males have a pair of grey
      vocal sacs at the corners of the mouth.

      These traits help identification, but body shape, size, and habitat should
      be considered together.
    - >-
      The long-legged wood frog (Rana macrocnemis) is more connected with
      forests and land, and it often has a dark temporal patch behind the eye.
      The marsh frog is much more tied to water and often jumps straight into
      water when disturbed.

      Separating them only by leg proportions is not reliable, so use several
      characters together.
    - >-
      Eastern and yellow-lemon treefrogs are smaller and have sticky discs on
      the toe tips, which help them climb plants.

      Toads have more warty skin and large glands behind the eyes. The Caucasian
      parsley frog and eastern spadefoot have vertical pupils.
faq:
  - question: Does the marsh frog occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The marsh frog is a confirmed and widespread species in Georgia.
      Records are known from different parts of the country.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The marsh frog occurs at ponds, lakes, canals, puddles, and slow streams.
      It lives in lowlands and foothills, and may also be found in village and
      city water bodies. It is rare in high subalpine and alpine zones.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. The marsh frog has no venom and does not pose a danger to people.
      There is no need to catch it. If you touch one, wash your hands with soap
      and water.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Look for smooth skin, a horizontal pupil, long hind legs, and webbed toes.
      It has no sticky discs on the toe tips. It is often green or olive with
      dark spots on the back, but colour is variable.
  - question: How does it differ from the long-legged wood frog?
    answer: >-
      The marsh frog is more closely tied to water and is often green or olive.
      The long-legged wood frog (Rana macrocnemis) is more often found in forest
      and on land, and it often has a dark temporal patch behind the eye.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      At species level, adult marsh frogs are usually about 6–12 cm in body
      length. Females are generally larger than males. This page does not have
      a complete measured series for Georgia.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is most active in the warm season and mainly near water. Breeding time
      changes with elevation and place — the season starts earlier in lowlands
      and later, with a shorter window, at higher sites.
  - question: Is bedriagae a separate species in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Not in this atlas. Georgia's current checklist uses Pelophylax ridibundus,
      while P. bedriagae is not accepted as a separate species. Use the name
      Pelophylax ridibundus for field identification.
