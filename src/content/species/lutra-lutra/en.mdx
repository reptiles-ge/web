commonName: Eurasian otter
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A semiaquatic mammal with dark-brown fur, a broad and fairly flat head, a
  thick tail, and webbed feet. It is well adapted to swimming and is resident in
  Georgia.
overview: >-
  The Eurasian otter (Lutra lutra) is a mammal in the weasel family
  (Mustelidae) and a resident native species in Georgia. IUCN treats Georgia as
  part of its natural range.

  It is not venomous. The main human risk is a bite, mainly if the animal is
  caught or picked up.

  Globally, IUCN assesses the Eurasian otter as Near Threatened (NT). On
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List it has Vulnerable (VU) status.

  At species level, body length without the tail is about 59–90 cm, and the tail
  is about 35–47 cm long.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia the otter occurs along rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and fish ponds,
  especially where there is food and cover.

  In a 2012–2013 national survey, otter signs were recorded at 245 of 631 surveyed
  sites, or about 39%. Most positive signs came from freshwater habitats. The
  detection rate was highest at lakes and reservoirs, at about 75%; it was 36% on
  river and other freshwater sites, and about 18% among 11 sea-coast sites. Signs
  became rarer with increasing altitude.

  Georgian and South Caucasian sources indicate the otter in Georgia to about
  2,800 m, although that limit is not a separately measured belt for this page.
  Globally, IUCN lists the species to about 4,120 m above sea level.

  Specific Georgian records are known from the Alazani basin, the Bakhvistskali
  valley, Borjomi Gorge, and the Kintrishi. In the 2022 Bakhvistskali study, 55
  activity points were recorded, mostly as tracks; that information comes from a
  hydropower environmental study, not a peer-reviewed scientific paper.

  For Lagodekhi, the sources do not fully agree: one baseline survey found no
  otter signs, while a later South Caucasus map added a recent observation. The
  same earlier survey also found no signs in Vashlovani and Borjomi-Kharagauli.

  The otter is widespread in Georgia, but that does not mean it occurs at the
  same density across the whole country.
diet: >-
  In a 2006–2007 study on a section of the Alazani, 791 otter spraints were
  analysed. In the cold season, from November to March, fish were the most
  frequent food item at about 50%, while amphibians were 30%. In the warm season,
  from April to October, the pattern changed: amphibians made up about 36% and
  fish 24%.

  Reptiles were recorded in about 12–13% in both seasons. These were non-venomous
  water snakes: grass snake (Natrix natrix) and dice snake (Natrix tessellata).
  Among amphibians, marsh frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) was the main item.

  Among fish, crucian carp and common carp were especially frequent; most prey
  items were under 20 cm. Rodents, including mice and muskrat, were recorded in
  small numbers, as were birds, insects, molluscs, and crustaceans.

behavior: >-
  In freshwater, the otter is often more active at dusk and at night, although it
  can also appear by day, especially in quiet places with little human
  disturbance. It does not hibernate in winter and remains active year-round.

  Its shelter is often called a holt. It may be placed among bank roots, in a
  rock crevice, in a natural cavity, or in a tunnel the animal has dug; sometimes
  the entrance is underwater.

  Otters often mark territory with spraint, or droppings. They leave it on rocks,
  logs, bridges, and other conspicuous places. Spraint often contains fish scales
  and bone remains. Tracks are five-toed, with webbing between the toes. In the
  2022 Bakhvistskali study, otter presence was recorded mostly through tracks.

  At species level, gestation lasts about 61–74 days. Litters are usually 2–3
  young, although the number may vary from 1 to 5. The female raises the young
  alone.
conservation: >-
  In the 2022 IUCN assessment, the Eurasian otter (Lutra lutra) is considered
  globally Near Threatened (NT), with a decreasing population trend. The previous
  2015 global assessment used the same category.

  On Georgia’s 2014 Red List, the otter is listed as Vulnerable (VU). The
  assessment identifies range decline as one of the main problems. The species is
  also on CITES Appendix I, and hunting it in Georgia is prohibited.

  Older Georgian sources described the otter’s condition in more severe terms,
  but later national surveys relate those assessments to outdated data. One
  secondary summary estimates Georgia’s population at more than 300 individuals,
  although that is not the result of an independent modern national census. The
  South Caucasus biodiversity map also gives VU status for Georgia and describes
  the population as small.

  The main threats to the otter are loss of riparian forest and other bank
  habitats, water pollution, reduced fish stocks, poaching, and conflict with
  fish farms. Studies have also recorded trap use near fish ponds. Hydropower
  construction and river-bed works may also damage its habitat.

  In 2019, media reported an otter killed in Tbilisi on the bank of the Mtkvari,
  but that is a news report, not a field population study.

  Do not approach or pick up a wild otter.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous. If caught or cornered, it can bite, so do not feed it,
  touch it, or pick it up.

  Conflict around fish ponds can be real, but the species is protected and
  killing or trapping it is not allowed.

  Like any wild mammal, it could theoretically carry rabies. In Georgia, the main
  wild sources of rabies are more often named as fox, jackal, and wolf, while
  among domestic animals the dog is named; this species is not considered a main
  source.

  summary: >-
    A semiaquatic mammal with dark-brown fur, a broad and fairly flat head, a
    thick tail, and webbed feet. It is well adapted to swimming and is resident in
    Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      Males are usually larger than females. At species level, male head–body
      length is about 60–90 cm, while females are 59–70 cm. The tail reaches
      about 35–47 cm. Males weigh about 5.5–11.4 kg, and females 3.4–7.6 kg.
      Separate complete Georgian measurements are not available on this page.

      The head is broad and flat, the whiskers long, and the ears small and
      rounded. The legs are short and the feet are webbed. The tail is thick at
      the base and gradually narrows toward the tip.
    - >-
      The fur is very dense and protects it well from water. The back is dark
      brown and may look almost black when wet. The underside is paler, and the
      throat sometimes has a light patch.

      When swimming, often only the head and part of the back are visible.
    - >-
      No other otter species occurs in Georgia. Neogale vison (older name
      Mustela vison) is the American mink, not an otter. It appears in an older
      list of South Caucasian mustelids, but this atlas has no separate profile
      for it and does not map a confirmed Georgian range.

      The Eurasian otter is much larger and has a thick tail that narrows from
      the base toward the tip. The least weasel is much smaller and terrestrial,
      with a head–body length of about 13–26 cm. The Caucasian badger is heavier,
      has a black-and-white striped head, and is not adapted to water like an
      otter.

      A swimming dog may be confusing at a distance. The otter’s low profile and
      thick tail, broad at the base and narrowing toward the end, are especially
      useful clues.
    - >-
      Otter presence is often seen from its signs before the animal itself.
      Five-toed, webbed tracks may remain in bank mud; spraint, sometimes with
      fish-scale remains, may be left on rocks; and slides may be visible on the
      bank.

      It is not venomous. Do not approach or pick up a wild otter.

facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758 as Mustela lutra. The accepted name is Lutra
    lutra. Order Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Mustelidae, subfamily
    Lutrinae, genus Lutra. Synonyms include Viverra lutra and Lutra vulgaris.
  - >-
    Caucasian literature often uses the subspecies L. l. meridionalis Ognev,
    1931. Hung and Law give it from Georgia to the Persian Gulf. Gorgadze 2004
    noted that no DNA work had then been done in the Caucasus. Saveljev et al.
    2024 compare Caucasian and northern Eurasian samples from Russia and
    Armenia; a Georgian sample is not cited on this page. Field identification
    is to species.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual names are წავი and ჩვეულებრივი წავი. The Red List
    uses წავი. In English: Eurasian otter, also European otter and common
    otter.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as a native country. It is in Bukhnikashvili and
    Kandaurov’s 2002 annotated list. Gorgadze’s 2012–2013 survey is a
    country-level picture together with the named localities, not a full
    regional map.
