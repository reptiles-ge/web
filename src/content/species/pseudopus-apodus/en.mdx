overview: >-
  The European glass lizard (Pseudopus apodus) is a large legless lizard of the
  anguid family — not a snake. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat it as a
  biologically confirmed species and restrict it in Georgia to the east. Older
  literature often used Ophisaurus apodus; the Caucasus holds the nominate
  subspecies P. a. apodus. The checklist’s barcode specimen is from Kesalo
  (Meore Kesalo); the paper’s figures are from the same locality. Globally the
  species ranges from southeastern Europe to Central Asia. It is non-venomous
  to humans.
habitat: >-
  It prefers open and semi-open ground — short grassy cover, sparsely wooded or
  scrub slopes, rocky places, and river valleys; it often appears in gardens and
  near settlements. On this atlas map the species is tied to eastern and
  east-central administrative units (including Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Shida
  Kartli, Mtskheta–Mtianeti, and Tbilisi), where records appear on the region
  pages. Western Georgian administrative units are not mapped — matching the
  2026 checklist conclusion (“only in Eastern Georgia”). An exact elevation
  range for Georgia is not stated in that checklist.
diet: >-
  It feeds on invertebrates — especially snails and slugs, as well as insects
  and other arthropods; literature also notes small lizards, eggs, and small
  mammals. Strong jaws help crack snail shells. It hunts more actively in wet
  weather. The 2026 Georgian checklist does not detail diet.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. To separate it from a snake, use three field marks:
  movable eyelids, external ear openings, and a deep lateral skin fold; hind-limb
  rudiments may show near the cloaca. When threatened it may hiss, bite, or
  release a secretion — defence, not venom. Tail autotomy is possible but rarer
  than in many other “glass lizards”. It is oviparous (The Reptile Database).
  Nesting month, clutch size, and incubation length in Georgia are not given in
  the 2026 checklist. Do not handle wild animals.
conservation: >-
  The species-specific IUCN Red List account classifies it as Least Concern.
  It is not listed in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Local threats include habitat
  conversion and killing after confusion with snakes. It is useful in
  agriculture — it reduces pest insects and mollusks.

faq:
  - question: Is the European glass lizard a snake or a lizard?
    answer: >-
      It is a lizard. Unlike a snake, it has movable eyelids, external ear
      openings, and a deep lateral skin fold; hind-limb rudiments may show near
      the cloaca.
  - question: Is it dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      It is non-venomous and not medically dangerous to people. When threatened
      it may hiss or bite — leave it alone and do not catch it. It is beneficial:
      it reduces pest insects and mollusks.
  - question: Where is it found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      According to Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026, only in eastern Georgia. The atlas
      map highlights Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Shida Kartli, Mtskheta–Mtianeti, and
      Tbilisi. Barcoded record: Meore Kesalo (Kakheti).
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Literature often cites total length up to about 1–1.35 m. A separate
      measurement series for Georgian populations is not in the 2026 checklist.
  - question: Why is it called a "glass lizard"?
    answer: >-
      Because of tail autotomy — the tail can break and fragment. In this species
      that is rarer than in many other glass lizards. The tail regrows slowly and
      often stays shorter and darker.
  - question: Does it lay eggs or give live birth?
    answer: >-
      It is egg-laying. Nesting season, clutch size, and incubation length in
      Georgia are not stated in the 2026 checklist.
  - question: How does it differ from the slow worm?
    answer: >-
      The eastern slow worm (Anguis colchica) is a smaller, smoother legless
      lizard and is a checklist candidate. The glass lizard is large, has a clear
      side fold, and often a yellow belly. Both are non-venomous; neither is a
      snake.
  - question: What should I do if I find one in the yard?
    answer: >-
      Watch and leave it where it is. Do not catch or kill it — it is not a
      snake. If unsure, use the comparison guide and this profile; if you suspect
      a venomous snake, keep distance and call 112 if needed.
