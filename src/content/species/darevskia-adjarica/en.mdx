commonName: "Ajarian Rock Lizard"
location: "Adjara (Shuakhevi) and Abastumani"
description: >-
  The venomless Ajarian Rock Lizard lives on rocks and stones in the forested valleys of Adjara and Abastumani. You cannot identify it by colour alone.
overview: >-
  The Ajarian rock lizard (Darevskia adjarica) lives in Georgia, in Adjara and around Abastumani. It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. Recent records come from Shuakhevi and Abastumani, but this does not mean it occurs throughout Georgia. The species was described from a lizard found at Abastumani, at about 1,400 m. It belongs to the true lizard family (Lacertidae) and was long thought to be a subspecies of D. parvula living in a separate area. Studies in 2018 and 2022 treated it as a separate species, distinct from D. parvula and D. tuniyevi, which live in Turkey. The 2026 Georgian checklist lists it as a possible separate species, but this has not yet been confirmed. A final answer needs study of the lizards in places where the ranges of these species meet. It is still possible that these species breed freely with each other there. A DNA record from Ajaristsqali resembles this species, but a database lists it under the name D. parvula. The checklist sometimes spells the name ajarica; this is another spelling of the same name, not a different species.
habitat: |-
  It lives in wooded valleys, on rocks and stones and in their crevices, often near a stream. Treeless steppe is not its usual habitat, and neither is the forest floor, where derjugini moves about. In Turkey, lizards then known as D. p. adjarica were collected during the day, in forest near a stream. In Georgia it has been found at about 600–1,400 m.

  A 2026 review places it in southwestern Georgia, though this information is considered moderately reliable. It should not be assumed to live on the dry plains of eastern Georgia or along the whole length of the Greater Caucasus. In Adjara it has been found at Shuakhevi. An image of its head scales and DNA records come from there, and in 2019 a specimen was also collected at the same place, at about 600 m. In Samtskhe-Javakheti it is known from Abastumani. The lizard used to describe the species came from there, from about 1,400 m. Another specimen was collected in Abastumani in 2019, at about 1,300 m. A DNA record from Ajaristsqali is stored under the name D. parvula, but it matches the Shuakhevi records by 99.21% and is linked to this species in the list. These records do not mean that it lives only in Adjara and Samtskhe-Javakheti, or that every suitable valley has already been checked.

  A 1980 description gives it a wider range. That range covers the Black Sea coast from Trabzon to Batumi and the Chorokhi River below Borçka. In Georgia, it includes Adjara and the area between Meria (southeast of Poti) and Borjomi. The same description also mentions a separate group living near Bakuriani. Guria, Imereti and Bakuriani appear in older sources, but the 2026 list gives no specific DNA record for them.
diet: >-
  It most likely eats insects, spiders and similar small animals. Other rock-dwelling Darevskia species feed the same way. It has no venom and catches its prey with its jaws.
behavior: >-
  It is active during the day: it warms itself on stones and hides in rock crevices. In Turkey, specimens of lizards then known as D. p. adjarica were collected between 08:00 and 15:30, in clear and cloudy weather. In Shuakhevi and Abastumani, specimens were collected on September 8, 2019, so the lizards were still out in late summer. It lays eggs. If you catch it, it may bite you, or its tail may come off. Watch it where it is, do not pick it up, and leave it there.
conservation: |-
  The Ajarian Rock Lizard is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. It has not been assessed separately at the global level. In 2009, D. parvula was assessed as being at low risk, and at the time that assessment also covered the Ajarian Rock Lizard. Nor is it listed under CITES, the international agreement on trade in wild animals. Do not catch wild lizards.

  A 2026 study treats D. adjarica as a candidate for a separate species. To find out whether it truly differs from D. parvula, more data are needed from places where the ranges of the two lizards meet. According to one source, samples from Georgia and some genetic records of D. parvula from northeastern Turkey differ by up to 3.7% in one DNA gene. A 2018 study puts the difference between the two groups at about 14.4%. These figures cannot be compared directly: the studies used different samples, and D. tuniyevi was separated out only in 2022. So it is not yet settled whether D. adjarica in Georgia should be considered a separate species.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium-sized rock lizard with a fairly flat head and well-developed legs. It is found in southwestern Georgia, among stones and in rock crevices. You cannot identify it by color alone. To tell it apart from other Darevskia species, look at its scales, the kind of place it lives in and the part of Georgia where you saw it.
  traits:
    - >-
      This rock-dwelling Darevskia has well-developed legs and a relatively flat head. It lives on rocks and in crevices, which sets it apart from the forest-floor derjugini and the large, ground-dwelling Lacerta. Its body is stockier and more rounded than that of D. parvula. Dark stripes run past the temples, and the middle of the back often stands out clearly from them. A pale stripe or a row of whitish spots is often visible along the edges of these stripes. Adult males often have an orange or brick-red belly, and a blue ring near the armpit is seen rarely. Any greenish tint comes from a sheen, not from a separate skin color. These are only features of appearance: color alone cannot be used to tell the species apart.
    - >-
      Head and body length, meaning the lizard's length without the tail, is about 5–5.5 cm. The tail is usually longer than the body. In one study, this length was about 4.2–6.2 cm among the animals measured. Most of them came from Turkey, and the rest from Georgia, from Abastumani and Shuakhevi.
    - >-
      To tell this lizard apart, you can check the sixth scale under the jaw. According to the original description, in about 95% of cases it is triangular with outward-curving edges. In D. parvula, this scale is often flat. In about half of cases, there is one large scale in front of the scale by the rear opening; in the rest, there are two. The images of lizards from Shuakhevi show the scales on the head and in front of the rear opening, not the coloring. In the wild, these features are hard to make out without a magnifying glass.
    - >-
      Clark's rock lizard (D. clarkorum), a separate species, has been confirmed in the Charnali valley in Adjara. D. derjugini may also occur in the same valley — a lizard that lives on the ground in forests and has a more rounded head. The Mixed Rock Lizard (D. mixta) is found only in western Georgia, not in Adjara. In English it was formerly called “Ajarian lizard”, so don't let the name lead you to confuse it with the Ajarian Rock Lizard. The true D. parvula is not on Georgia's species list. D. tuniyevi occurs in Turkey. One paper says it may extend in a narrow strip as far as Ajaristsqali, but its presence in Georgia has not been confirmed. You cannot tell these lizards apart by colour alone.
    - >-
      Recent accounts report it from Shuakhevi and Abastumani in Georgia. This does not mean it occurs throughout Georgia. The species was described from a lizard found in Abastumani. For a long time it was considered a local subspecies of D. parvula, but D. parvula itself is not included in Georgia’s species list. The 2026 Georgian list includes it only as a possible species, not a confirmed one. The Mixed Rock Lizard (D. mixta) does not occur in Adjara.
faq:
  - question: Is the Ajarian rock lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 list includes it as a candidate species, which means it has not yet been confirmed as a separate species. The species was described from a lizard found in Abastumani. Its DNA records and the image of its head come from Shuakhevi.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Recent records place it in Shuakhevi, Adjara, at about 600 m, and in Abastumani, at about 1,300–1,400 m. It is found on rocks and stones in wooded valleys. These two places do not mean it lives throughout Georgia.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      It is a medium-sized rock lizard with a dark, sharp pattern on its body. Its belly is often orange or brick-red. Color alone, though, is not enough to identify it. Compare it with D. clarkorum from the Charnali valley and with D. derjugini, which lives on the forest floor. Do not confuse it with the Mixed Rock Lizard (D. mixta): that species is not found in Adjara.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No, it has no venom. If you catch it, it may bite you, and its tail may come off. So don't pick it up; leave it where it is.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Its head and body, without the tail, are often about 5–5.5 cm long. In the lizards that were measured, this length ranged from about 4.2 to 6.2 cm. The tail is not included in this figure, so the whole lizard is longer.
  - question: Why is it a candidate species?
    answer: >-
      Possibly, but it has not yet been confirmed as a separate species. Some studies treat the Ajarian Rock Lizard as a species of its own. The list once classed it as a subspecies of D. parvula and now includes it as a possible separate species. Where this lizard and D. parvula meet, their local groups have not yet been studied. That is why there is still no final answer to this question.
  - question: Is this D. mixta or D. parvula?
    answer: >-
      The Mixed Rock Lizard (D. mixta) does not occur in Adjara. D. parvula is not on Georgia's species list either: it is a species from Turkey. One older DNA record from Ajaristsqali is listed in the database under the name parvula. Yet this record almost matches the DNA records of the lizards from Shuakhevi.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is active during the day. In Shuakhevi and Abastumani, a sample was collected in early September, so it was still active at that time of year.
