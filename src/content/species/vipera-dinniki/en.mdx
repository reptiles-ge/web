overview: >-
  Dinnik’s viper (Vipera dinniki) is a snake found in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus. Its presence in Georgia has been confirmed, but it is not found throughout the country. A 2026 list treats it as a possible distinct species within the Vipera kaznakovi group. The boundary between Dinnik’s viper and the Caucasian viper as separate species is still not fully clear. One form of Dinnik’s viper is found mainly above the upper edge of the forest. According to the same list, its subspecies Vipera dinniki tuniyevi is associated with wooded and mountainous areas of central Georgia. An adult Dinnik’s viper is usually about 40–55 cm long. It is venomous, so avoid close contact; it poses a moderate risk to people. The IUCN considers the species vulnerable, meaning it faces a risk of extinction. Dinnik’s viper is also included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  Dinnik's viper lives in the high-mountain belt of the Greater Caucasus. It is
  most often found on subalpine meadows, near the upper forest line, on rocky
  ground and moraines. Colchic lowlands and the dry plains of eastern Georgia
  are not typical habitat for it. The map marks distribution only in regions
  whose status is confirmed in the field-record table; other observations do
  not count as confirmed distribution. Specific records are known from Svaneti,
  including the
  Enguri basin and the Ushguli–Shkhara area, and also from Khevsureti — Ardoti,
  the Stepantsminda area, and Truso. The species is also mentioned from Tusheti.
  According to IUCN, Dinnik's viper is found mainly at about 1,500–2,800 m above
  sea level. Other Caucasian records sometimes reach 3,000 m, and in valleys it
  may descend slightly lower. It often chooses south- and southeast-facing
  slopes. Its range is not continuous — populations are often isolated and
  fragmented.
diet: >-
  Adult Dinnik’s vipers mainly catch lizards and small mammals. They may also occasionally catch the chick of a bird that nests on the ground. Young vipers more often eat small lizards and insects, including grasshoppers. When hunting, these vipers often lie in wait and subdue their prey with venom. This information comes from observations in the western Caucasus. The diet of Dinnik’s vipers in Georgia has not been fully studied separately.
behavior: >-
  In the highlands, Dinnik's viper is usually active from mid-April to the second
  half of September. It is mainly seen by day. In hot weather it shelters in
  shade, while on cloudy days it may remain outside for long periods. It spends
  winter in rock crevices, often in the same area where it lives in summer.
  Dinnik's viper gives live birth. Mating usually takes place from late April to
  May. Caucasian observations describe young being born in August–September:
  earlier on northern slopes and later on southern slopes. Newborns are about 13
  cm long and almost do not feed before their first winter. Because of harsh
  high-mountain conditions, females do not reproduce every year. If you meet a
  Dinnik's viper, keep your distance and do not try to pick it up or catch it. It
  does not seek people and usually reacts defensively only when it feels
  threatened.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assesses Dinnik's viper as Vulnerable (VU). Its range is relatively small
  and strongly fragmented, while habitat and population decline remain concerns.
  In Georgia, the species is also included as Vulnerable (VU) in the 2014 Red
  List. Key threats include degradation of subalpine pastures and intensive
  grazing. In the 2026 checklist, Dinnik's viper is treated as a candidate
  species in the Vipera kaznakovi complex. Species boundaries in this group
  have not yet been fully confirmed with genomic data.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Dinnik’s viper is a small snake that lives high in the mountains. Its pupils are vertical, and its scales have a raised line down the middle. It often has a dark zigzag or almost straight stripe along its back. Where and in what kind of place you saw it matters when identifying it: it can be confused with the Caucasian viper or the cat-eyed snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      Its body is relatively slender and not bulky. Adults are usually about 40–55 cm long. Females are generally larger than males, while males have relatively longer tails. The head is triangular, but narrower and less clearly separated from the neck than that of the Caucasian viper.
    - >-
      The pupil looks like a vertical slit. Each scale on the back has a pronounced raised line down its center. The body’s main color may be gray, silver, brownish, or greenish gray. Some also have a yellow or orange tint. A dark zigzag usually runs along the back, though it sometimes looks almost like a straight stripe. The belly may be dark with light patches or light with dark speckles. Entirely black ones are also common among groups living in the west. In Georgia, this all-black coloring has been documented around Stepantsminda.
    - >-
      The young are usually gray-brown, unlike the reddish young of the Caucasian viper. Even those that later turn black are born with the usual pattern. They darken as they grow and become completely black around their third year.
    - >-
      The [Caucasus viper (Vipera kaznakovi)](vipera-kaznakovi) is found mainly
      in lower, humid Colchic forests, often up to about 1,000 m above sea level.
      It has a relatively broader head and often a red-orange ground colour. Even
      fully black individuals often keep red or yellow tones around the mouth.
      Darevsky's viper (Vipera darevskii) occurs in the Lesser Caucasus
      highlands — the Javakheti–Erusheti area — mostly above 2,300 m. The
      Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetinus) is a much larger snake and in
      Georgia is found mainly in dry and semi-dry eastern lowlands and foothills.
    - >-
      The cat-eyed snake (Telescopus fallax) is sometimes mistaken for a viper. It also has vertical pupils, but its body is slimmer, its eyes are large, and the scales on its back are smooth. The nose-horned viper (Vipera transcaucasiana) is easy to recognize by the horn-like growth on its nose. In Georgia, it is associated with areas of the Lesser Caucasus. Dinnik’s viper is venomous. It is considered a moderate risk to humans, and a bite requires medical attention.

    answer: >-
      Yes. The Dinnik’s viper has been confirmed in Georgia, but it is not found throughout the country. Its range is linked to the high mountain areas of the Greater Caucasus. A 2026 list treats it as a possible separate species because it is still unclear how the snakes in the Vipera kaznakovi group should be divided into distinct species.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, Dinnik's viper occurs in high-mountain zones of the Greater
      Caucasus — on subalpine meadows, near the upper forest line, and in rocky
      places. Only regions with confirmed status are marked as distribution on
      the map; other observations remain field records. Specific records are
      known from Svaneti, Khevsureti, the Stepantsminda area, Truso, and Tusheti.
      Western Georgian lowlands and dry eastern plains are not typical range for
      it.
  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      Yes, Dinnik's viper is venomous. The risk to people is moderate — lower
      than the Levantine viper, but a bite still needs medical
      care. The snake does not chase people and usually reacts defensively only
      when it feels threatened. Keep distance and do not try to pick it up or
      catch it.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
      Call [112](tel:112) immediately. Calm the person, lay them down, and limit
      movement as much as possible. Do not cut the bite site, do not suck out
      venom, and do not use a tight tourniquet. Hospital transport and medical
      treatment are needed.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Dinnik's viper is a small, relatively slim snake. It has a vertical pupil,
      keeled scales, and a dark zigzag or nearly straight stripe along the back.
      The ground colour is often grey or brown and, unlike the Caucasus viper, it
      usually lacks a strong red-orange background. The cat snake has a vertical
      pupil, but its body is slimmer, its eyes are large, and its dorsal scales
      are smooth.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adult Dinnik's vipers are usually about 40–55 cm long. Females are usually
      larger than males. Among vipers found in Georgia, it is a relatively small
      species and is much shorter than the Levantine viper.
  - question: How does it differ from the Caucasus viper?
    answer: >-
      Dinnik's viper is found mainly in high-mountain zones of the Greater
      Caucasus, at about 1,500–2,800 m, and is often grey or brownish. The
      Caucasus viper is tied to lower, humid Colchic forests, mainly below 1,000
      m. It has a relatively broader head and often a red-orange ground colour.
      In the 2026 checklist, the species limits between these two forms are still
      not fully resolved.
  - question: Why is it a candidate species and Vulnerable?
    answer: >-
      The 2026 checklist treats Dinnik's viper as a candidate species because
      its genetic separation within the Vipera kaznakovi complex is not yet
      fully confirmed. According to IUCN, the species is Vulnerable (VU): its
      range is under 20,000 km² and strongly fragmented. In Georgia, Dinnik's
      viper is also included in the Red List as VU.
