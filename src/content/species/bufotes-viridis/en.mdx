commonName: Green Toad
location: Georgia — open country
description: >-
  A harmless toad with a green-spotted back. Common in open country in Georgia,
  scarce in dense forest.
overview: >-
  The green toad (Bufotes viridis) is a harmless toad with green spots on its
  back. In Georgia it lives mainly in open country. The 2026 checklist of
  Georgia’s amphibians and reptiles lists it as a biologically confirmed
  species, which means its presence in Georgia is confirmed. The checklist keeps
  the name Bufotes viridis. It belongs to the true toad family (Bufonidae). It
  has no venomous bite and is not dangerous to people. Its length is usually
  about 5–12 cm (a figure for the species as a whole); females are generally
  larger than males. In the global IUCN assessment it is Least Concern. It is
  not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  The green toad is an animal of open country. Data from 1996 show it is
  widespread across the Caucasian Isthmus — from forest to desert, including
  settled land. It avoids dense, closed-canopy forest, though it may appear in
  forest clearings. The same 1996 data list 28 sites in eastern Georgia and 14
  in the west. It is common across the country except on the humid south-western
  Black Sea coast. Records from Batumi and Poti are old: they date from 1913 and
  were repeated in later works. The green toad is scarce in that area. As of
  2026, knowledge of its distribution in Georgia is rated as medium confidence.
  The DNA barcode and illustration published in 2026 are from Pantishara Canyon
  (Dedoplistskaro Municipality, Kakheti). Breeding in Borjomi Canyon was
  confirmed by field studies in 1993–1994. The study site lies at about 900–1200
  m — the elevation of one local site, not a country-wide elevation limit for
  the species. Public photo observations confirm the green toad in 10 regions:
  Kakheti, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Tbilisi, Kvemo Kartli, Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti,
  Samtskhe-Javakheti, Adjara, Shida Kartli, Racha-Lechkhumi and Kvemo Svaneti,
  and Imereti. These regions are shaded on the map. Abkhazia has a single record
  so far and Guria none, so these two regions are not yet shaded. Few or no
  records from a region do not mean the toad is absent there. Record counts show
  how often people look for animals in an area, not how many toads live there.
  Look for it in steppe, clearings, gardens, parks and other open settled land.
  It lays its eggs in shallow pools, ditches and ponds — usually less than 50 cm
  deep (a figure for the species as a whole).
diet: >-
  Adult green toads hunt mainly on land and eat beetles, spiders, ants and other
  crawling invertebrates. Ants are an important part of the adult diet. Young
  toads that have just changed from tadpoles catch smaller prey — springtails,
  small beetles, mites and flies. Tadpoles feed on algae and decaying plant
  matter. These diet data describe the species in general, not Georgian toads
  specifically.
behavior: >-
  The green toad is mainly active at dusk and at night and hides by day. During
  breeding it may also appear by day near shallow water. In dry weather it may
  come to water at night to drink. In Borjomi Canyon breeding is most intense
  from April to June. It is an open-country toad: in Borjomi, females may lay
  more than one clutch in a season, unlike the forest toad, which usually lays
  one. The eggs are laid in water in long strings. At the same Borjomi site,
  females typically become able to breed after three winters; the oldest females
  recorded there were 9–12 years old. It spends the winter on land — in burrows,
  holes or under stones — and sometimes in water. If you find one, leave it
  where it is and do not pick it up. Wash your hands after any contact. It is
  not dangerous to people.
conservation: >-
  In the global IUCN Red List assessment of 2023, Bufotes viridis is Least
  Concern. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List — the amphibians listed there
  are the [Caucasian salamander](mertensiella-caucasica) and the [eastern
  spadefoot](pelobates-syriacus). The green toad often lives in gardens, parks
  and other places near people. Local threats include the loss of meadows and
  the drying of wetlands. Forest clearance, on the other hand, can favour this
  open-country species. The green toad is listed in Appendix II of the Bern
  Convention.
interaction: >-
  The green toad (Bufotes viridis) is not venomous to people and usually poses
  no danger when you meet it. It has large glands behind its eyes. The defensive
  fluid (secretion) from these glands can irritate the eyes and the inside of
  the mouth. So do not pick up or disturb the toad. In Georgia the green toad is
  widespread. It is especially common in open, dry and semi-dry places,
  including gardens, the edges of settlements and farmland. In the evening or at
  night you may also meet it on roads, in yards and near lit areas. If you see
  one in nature, it is best to leave it where it is. If you touch a green toad,
  wash your hands well with soap and water. Do not touch your eyes or mouth
  until you have washed your hands. If the secretion gets into your eye, rinse
  the eye immediately with plenty of clean water. If the irritation is strong or
  lasts a long time, seek medical help. Do not try to catch the toad, take it
  home or move it elsewhere. Do not let pets touch the toad with their mouths.
  This is educational information, not a medical protocol. Leaving a wild
  amphibian in its natural habitat is the best way to protect it.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A short, warty toad with irregular green or olive spots, a horizontal pupil
    and large glands behind the eyes. In Georgia it is most often confused with
    the [Caucasian toad](bufo-verrucosissimus).
  traits:
    - >-
      The back is grey or olive, with irregular green or olive spots. The sides
      often show small red or orange warts. The belly is greyish. In the arid
      south-east of Georgia some individuals have also been described with a
      narrow mid-dorsal stripe.
    - >-
      Large glands behind the eyes — parotoid glands — produce a defensive
      secretion that can irritate the mouth or eyes. The pupil is a horizontal
      slit; a round eardrum is usually visible.
    - >-
      The skin is warty. Length is usually about 5–12 cm; females are generally
      larger than males. In the breeding season males develop rough pads on
      their fingers — first on the first finger, and at the peak of the season
      often on the second and third as well.
    - >-
      The [Caucasian toad](bufo-verrucosissimus) is brown or reddish-brown,
      without a green-spotted mosaic, and lives more in forest. The green toad
      is an open-country animal. Both occur in Borjomi Canyon, but in different
      microhabitats.
    - >-
      The [eastern spadefoot](pelobates-syriacus) has a vertical pupil and a
      hard digging “spade” on the hind foot; it lacks large glands behind the
      eyes. Treefrogs have smooth skin and sticky discs on the toes.
faq:
  - question: Does the green toad occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 Georgian checklist lists it as a biologically confirmed
      species, which means its presence in Georgia is confirmed. Data from 1996
      show it is common across the country except on the humid Black Sea coast,
      where it is scarce.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In open, dry or semi-arid country — steppe, clearings, gardens and settled
      land, especially in eastern Georgia. Known sites include, for example,
      Pantishara (Dedoplistskaro, Kakheti) and Borjomi Canyon. It is scarce on
      the humid Black Sea coast. Public photo observations confirm it in 10
      regions, which are shaded on the map. Abkhazia has a single record so far
      and Guria none; few records do not mean the toad is absent there.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venomous bite and is harmless to people. The secretion from
      the large glands behind its eyes can irritate the eyes or mouth. So do not
      pick it up, and wash your hands after any contact. If a dog takes the toad
      in its mouth, this can harm the dog.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A short, warty toad with green or olive spots, a horizontal pupil and
      large glands behind the eyes. A plain brown forest toad is a different
      species.
  - question: How does it differ from the Caucasian toad?
    answer: >-
      The green toad has a green-spotted back and lives in open country. The
      [Caucasian toad](bufo-verrucosissimus) is brown, without that spotted
      mosaic, and is found more in forest. Both occur in Borjomi, but in
      different places.
  - question: Is Bufotes sitibundus a separate species from the green toad?
    answer: >-
      Not in this atlas. The 2026 Georgian checklist keeps the name Bufotes
      viridis. The reason is that there is no evidence of geographic or genetic
      isolation between Caucasian and European green toads. Some studies call
      the Caucasian populations B. sitibundus. In the field the animal is
      identified the same way under either name.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      The green toad is usually about 5–12 cm long (a figure for the species as
      a whole). Females are generally larger than males. In Borjomi Canyon,
      young toads are about 3–5 cm long at the end of their second summer.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up or move it from a
      pool. Wash your hands after contact. It is not a medical risk to people.
