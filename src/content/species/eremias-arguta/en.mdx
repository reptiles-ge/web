commonName: "Steppe-Runner"
location: "Southeast Georgia — sporadic"
description: >-
  A harmless steppe lizard of the southeast. It is sporadic and easily confused
  with the more common rapid racerunner.
overview: >-
  The steppe-runner (Eremias arguta) is a steppe lizard found in southeast
  Georgia and belongs to the true lizards (Lacertidae). In Georgia it occurs
  sporadically and is not distributed throughout the country.

  The subspecies present in Georgia is E. a. transcaucasica. A confirmed record
  is known from Samukhi Valley in Dedoplistskaro Municipality. It has no venom.
  Globally, IUCN lists it as Least Concern, and it is not included in Georgia's
  2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  The checklist names southeast Georgia; distribution confidence is medium.
  The named genetic specimen is from Samukhi Valley, Dedoplistskaro
  Municipality. This atlas map shows Kakheti from those records; that does not
  mean the species occurs throughout Kakheti, or that every dry valley has been
  surveyed. There is no named western, Colchic, or high Javakheti locality on
  this page. Historical Georgian records rest on Nikolskii 1913 and
  Muskhelishvili 1970; further municipalities are not inferred from them here.
  At species level it uses open dry ground — steppe, semi-desert, sandy or
  clay soils with sparse grass; do not expect it in forest. A published
  elevation range for Georgia is not available. Samukhi and the dry Dedoplistskaro
  lowland are low elevation; the subspecies type locality in Armenia is about
  2,100 m — that is not a Georgian elevation. The global range is wide, from
  eastern Europe to Central Asia; the South Caucasus lies near the southern
  edge.
diet: >-
  The diet of the steppe-runner in Georgia has not been fully studied separately.
  At species level, it feeds mainly on small invertebrates, including beetles,
  other insects, and spiders. Some sources also mention small snails.
behavior: >-
  The steppe-runner is a fast, diurnal lizard. It often basks on open ground and,
  when threatened, quickly runs to a burrow, grass roots, or another shelter.

  A complete seasonal calendar for Georgia has not been described separately.
  The Georgian photograph in this atlas was taken in Dedoplistskaro in late
  September, but one observation does not represent the whole seasonal activity
  pattern.

  The species lays eggs. European populations are reported with 3–11 eggs, but
  clutch size for Georgia is not confirmed on this page. If caught, it may bite
  or shed its tail, so leave it where it is.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists the steppe-runner globally as Least Concern, and its population
  trend is assessed as stable. This is a global assessment and does not evaluate
  Georgia's sporadic population separately.

  The species is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red List. Its sporadic occurrence
  in southeast Georgia does not mean it belongs to a Red List category. A possible
  local threat is the conversion of dry steppe habitats.
interaction: >-
  The steppe-runner is not venomous and poses no medical risk to people. If
  handled, it may bite or shed its tail in defence. Do not catch it — watch it
  and leave it where it is.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A relatively large lizard of open, dry places, often with pale ocelli or dark
    spots on the back. In Georgia it may most closely resemble the rapid
    racerunner and the snake-eyed lizard. Colour alone is not enough for
    identification.
  traits:
    - >-
      Compared with the rapid racerunner, the steppe-runner has a more robust
      body and relatively shorter limbs. The head is pointed. At species level,
      snout-vent length is often about 6–8 cm. This page does not have a
      complete measured series for Georgia.

      The lower eyelid is movable, which helps separate it from the snake-eyed
      lizard.
    - >-
      The back may be grey, sandy, or olive, with pale ocelli or irregular dark
      spots. Young individuals often have a more contrasting pattern.

      In adults, stripes may be weak or absent. The rapid racerunner often has
      clearer longitudinal stripes and blue ocelli on the sides. Colour is quite
      variable, so do not rely on colour alone.
    - >-
      One distinguishing feature is the subocular scale, which in the
      steppe-runner does not reach the edge of the mouth. In the rapid racerunner
      it usually reaches the mouth edge.

      This character requires a close view, and there is no need to catch the
      lizard to check it.
    - >-
      The rapid racerunner (Eremias velox) is more common in southeast Georgia.
      It is slimmer and has a relatively longer tail.

      The snake-eyed lizard (Ophisops elegans) is small and has fused eyelids,
      giving the eye a more snake-like look. The green lizard (Lacerta strigata)
      is larger and often green. The Asian snake-eyed skink (Ablepharus
      pannonicus) is a slender skink, not a racerunner.
    - >-
      The steppe-runner is a confirmed species in Georgia, but it occurs
      sporadically in the southeast. A confirmed record is known from Samukhi
      Valley in Dedoplistskaro Municipality. Its presence should not be assumed
      throughout Georgia.

faq:
  - question: Does the steppe-runner occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The steppe-runner is a confirmed species in Georgia and occurs
      sporadically in the southeast. A confirmed record is known from Samukhi
      Valley in Dedoplistskaro Municipality.
  - question: Where might I find it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The steppe-runner occurs in dry, open steppe habitats of southeast Georgia.
      The named locality on this page is Samukhi Valley in Dedoplistskaro
      Municipality. This page has no confirmed point from western Georgia or the
      Colchic zone.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. The steppe-runner has no venom. If caught, it may bite or shed its
      tail. Do not pick it up, and leave it where it is.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Look for the relatively robust body, a back with pale ocelli or spots, and
      a movable eyelid. The rapid racerunner is slimmer and often striped, while
      the snake-eyed lizard has fused eyelids. Colour alone is not enough for
      identification.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      At species level, snout-vent length is often about 6–8 cm. This page does
      not have a complete measured series for Georgia.
  - question: How does it differ from the rapid racerunner?
    answer: >-
      Both belong to the genus Eremias and may occur in the same areas of
      southeast Georgia. The rapid racerunner is more common, slimmer, and
      longer-tailed, while the steppe-runner has a more robust body and often an
      ocellate pattern.

      One distinguishing feature is the subocular scale: in the steppe-runner it
      does not reach the mouth edge. There is no need to catch the lizard to
      check this feature.
  - question: What does it eat, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      The steppe-runner lays eggs. Clutch size has not been described separately
      for Georgia; species-level European sources report 3–11 eggs.

      Its diet in Georgia has also not been fully studied separately. At species
      level, it feeds mainly on small invertebrates.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is active by day, in the warm season, and in open places. A complete
      seasonal calendar for Georgia has not been described separately. The
      Georgian photograph in this atlas was taken in Dedoplistskaro in late
      September, but this is only one observation and does not represent the
      whole season.
