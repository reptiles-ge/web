commonName: Levantine viper
location: Eastern Georgia
description: >-
  Eastern Georgia’s high-risk venomous viper — dorsal blotches alone are not
  enough to identify the Levantine viper.
overview: |-
  The Levantine viper, or gyurza (Macrovipera lebetinus), is a large [venomous viper](/venomous-snakes). In Georgia it lives mainly in the dry and semi-dry parts of the east. You cannot [reliably identify it](/snakes/shxamiani-gvelis-amocnoba) by its back markings or size alone, so do not go near the snake. Some sources call it Macrovipera lebetina or Vipera lebetina.
habitat: |-
  The Levantine viper lives in dry, stony and rocky places. In Georgia, you will find it in dry areas along the lower Iori, Mtkvari and Alazani rivers, in Shiraki and Gareji, and in the dry foothills of [Kvemo Kartli](/regions/kvemo-kartli). It is rare around [Tbilisi](/regions/tbilisi) and Rustavi. Overall, it occurs in [Kakheti](/regions/kakheti), Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. Confirmed sites include Kvareli, the area around Nekresi Monastery, the Alazani Valley and the Colorful Desert.
  As of 2026, over recent decades the Levantine viper has spread from Georgia’s far southeast to the Alazani Valley and Tbilisi. In Tbilisi it has been seen only on isolated occasions: in the Botanical Garden, Tabori, Nadzaladevi and Saburtalo. Toward Rustavi it has been seen in Rustavi, Gardabani and at the Jandari Reservoir, mostly in dry areas of Kvemo Kartli along the Mtkvari. Such a sighting only means the snake was there in a particular place and year. It does not mean it lives in every part of the city or turns up often.
  It shelters in rock crevices, under stones and in rodent burrows, and sometimes enters structures. In intense summer heat, it may also appear near water. In Georgia, it occurs up to about 1,500 m. Its natural presence in western Georgia has not been confirmed. It does not usually live in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus or in the Colchic forest.
diet: >-
  The main prey of adult Levantine vipers is rodents. They also catch small birds, lizards and other small animals with backbones. Their usual way of hunting is to lie in wait. Sometimes they climb bushes or trees.
behavior: |-
  In the South Caucasus, the Levantine viper is active from April to the end of October, and sometimes appears as early as late March. When you might meet one depends on the weather and the daytime temperature. In spring and autumn it moves around by day, while in the summer heat it is more active at dusk and at night. On very hot days you are also more likely to see it near water. It spends the winter in rock crevices or burrows.
  The Levantine viper is the only viper in Georgia that lays eggs. It mates mainly in April and May, and in summer it lays 15–20 eggs. After about 35–45 days, young snakes 23–25 cm long hatch from them.
  The Levantine viper does not seek out people. It defends itself when it feels threatened. So if you come across one, keep a safe distance and do not try to catch it.
conservation: |-
  Worldwide, the Levantine viper faces a low risk of extinction, according to a 2021 assessment. Among vipers, Georgia’s 2014 Red List includes [Dinnik’s viper](vipera-dinniki) and the [Caucasian viper](vipera-kaznakovi), but not the Levantine viper. Older Georgian encyclopedias still sometimes link it with the Red Book.
  The Levantine viper is threatened by changes to the places where it lives, expanding settlements and overgrazing by livestock. People also kill it deliberately. In dry areas it hunts rodents and is an important link in the local food chain.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The Levantine viper is a thick-bodied viper with a broad, triangular head and vertically elongated pupils. A raised ridge runs down the middle of each scale. From a distance, when only its spotted back is visible, you may mistake it for a Urartu racer or a multicolored racer.
  traits:
    - >-
      The Levantine viper has a thick, heavy body and a broad, triangular head that stands out clearly from the neck. The nose is blunt, with no horn. It is usually 70–90 cm long, though large ones can reach 1.5 m. Reports of Levantine vipers longer than 2 m are disputed.
    - >-
      The pupil looks like a vertical slit. Each scale on the back has a prominent raised line down the middle, making the back look rough. The scales on the head, including above the eyes, are mostly small.
    - >-
      It is gray, sand-colored or brownish. The back often has dark spots or crossbands that sometimes form a zigzag, though some snakes are fairly plain. The belly is paler and often sprinkled with small dark specks.
    - >-
      The [Urartu racer](elaphe-urartica) has a thick body and large, rounded dark patches on its back. The [variegated racer](hemorrhois-ravergieri) has dark spots too. So either one can be mistaken for a Levantine viper, especially when the snake's head is out of view. The difference is in the eyes: both racers have round pupils, while a Levantine viper's are vertical, like a cat's. But if you are moving closer to see the pupils, you are already too close.
    - >-
      The [steppe viper](vipera-renardi) is also found in eastern Georgia, but it is much smaller than the Levantine viper, usually 40–60 cm long. The [nose-horned viper](vipera-transcaucasiana) has a horn on its nose, so you can recognize it easily. This snake is found in more mountainous areas. [Dinnik’s viper](vipera-dinniki) lives high in the mountains of the Greater Caucasus, so you will not meet it where the Levantine viper usually lives.
faq:
  - question: Is the Levantine viper found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. As of 2026, the Levantine viper is confirmed to live in Georgia. It is found mainly in the dry and semi-dry areas of eastern Georgia. It does not occur across the whole country.
  - question: Are “gyurza”, “giurza” and გიუზა the same snake?
    answer: >-
      Yes, it is the same snake. “Giuza” is a popular folk form of the name “giurza”. In Georgian, the Levantine viper, Macrovipera lebetinus, is called giurza. So if you hear someone say “giuza”, they mean this very snake.
  - question: Does the Levantine viper occur in Tbilisi or Rustavi?
    answer: >-
      Yes, but rarely, and mostly in dry, open or rocky places. In Tbilisi, the Levantine viper has been seen in the Botanical Garden, on Tabor, and in Nadzaladevi and Saburtalo. Toward Rustavi, it has been seen in Rustavi, Gardabani and near the Jandari Reservoir. This means it was seen in these places in specific years, not that it is common in every part of the city.
  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      Yes, the Levantine viper is a venomous snake. Among Georgia's snakes, its bite is one of the most dangerous to human health. It is a large snake and may inject a lot of venom when it bites. The venom disrupts blood clotting, causes bleeding and damages the area around the bite. It does not usually attack people. It bites when it feels threatened: if someone gets too close, steps on it or tries to catch it. If you are bitten, call 112 immediately.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
      If someone is bitten by a Levantine viper, call 112 immediately. Have the person lie down and reassure them. Try to keep them as still as possible. Do not cut the bite, suck out the venom or tie anything tightly around the bitten area. The person needs treatment in a hospital. If medicine to counter the venom is needed, only a doctor should choose and give it.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      The Levantine viper is usually 70–90 cm long, while large ones can reach 1.5 metres. It is the [largest viper](/snakes/didi-gvelebi) in Georgia. Reports of Levantine vipers longer than 2 metres are disputed.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Watch the snake from a safe distance and leave it where it is. Do not try to catch or kill it. If you give it room and do not disturb it, the Levantine viper will usually move away from you on its own.
