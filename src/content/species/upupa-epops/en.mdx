commonName: Eurasian hoopoe
location: Breeding migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A crested bird with a long bill and black-and-white wings. Confirmed in
  Georgia as a breeding migrant on the 2024 national bird checklist; it does
  not stay the winter.
overview: >-
  The Eurasian hoopoe (Upupa epops) is a medium-sized bird (family Upupidae)
  confirmed in Georgia as a breeding migrant. The 2024 annotated checklist of
  the birds of Georgia codes it BB; M: a breeder that is absent in the
  non-breeding season, and also a bird of passage. Seasonality is classed as
  high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality is high (H), with photographs.
  SABUKO lists the Georgian name ოფოფი, the family as ოფოფისებრნი, and the
  order as ყაპყაპისნაირნი (Bucerotiformes). Adults are about 25–32 cm long at
  species level, pinkish-cinnamon, with a long downcurved bill, an erectile
  crest, and black-and-white barred wings. It is not a threat to people. IUCN
  lists the species as Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns the same
  category nationally.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in the breeding season and on spring and autumn
  passage, not in winter. This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality
  map. The 2024 checklist confirms country-level breeding-migrant status but
  does not publish a regional gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly across
  Georgia, including closed high-mountain forest or treeless high plateaus.
  Species-level habitat has two requirements: bare or short-grass ground for
  foraging, and a vertical surface with a cavity for nesting — a tree, rock,
  wall, ruin, or nest box. That can be pasture, vineyard, orchard, park,
  woodland edge, farmland with trees, and village edges. A separate national
  elevation band for Georgia is not published here. The atlas map is empty
  until named Georgian localities are compiled from sources that actually list
  them. The photographs on this page were not taken in Georgia.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range it
  feeds on the ground: it probes soft soil with the long bill and takes insects
  and their larvae — often beetles, mole crickets, orthopterans, ants, and
  other soil invertebrates. Species-level literature rarely also records small
  vertebrates; that is not a measured Georgian menu. In the field a crested
  bird on a lawn, pasture, or garden soil is typical behaviour.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day and is often heard before it is seen. The song is a
  soft, low “up-up-up” or “oop-oop-oop”, usually three notes; the English name
  comes from that call. The crest is folded at rest and fanned when the bird
  is excited or landing. Flight is undulating; the black-and-white wings are
  then obvious. It does not excavate its own hole: it uses an existing cavity,
  an old woodpecker hole, a wall crevice, or a nest box. The female incubates;
  the male brings food. BTO figures at species level are a clutch of 7–8 eggs
  (observed range 4–12), incubation about 15–16 days, and fledging about 26–29
  days; those are range-wide figures, not a Georgian nest sample on this page.
  The 2024 checklist treats the species as a breeder and a migrant in Georgia,
  not as a winter visitor. Most Palearctic populations leave the breeding
  range in autumn and winter farther south, mainly in Africa. Exact arrival
  and departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated here.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Upupa epops as Least Concern on the IUCN Red
  List. The global range is large. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns
  global and national Red List category LC. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red
  List (Government Resolution No. 190). BirdLife’s European assessment lists a
  Georgian breeding estimate of 3,400–34,400 pairs (2013–2017, method partial,
  trend deficient), sourced to EBBA Georgia (SABUKO, Ilia State University,
  NGO Psovi). The range is wide and the trend is unevaluated; that figure is
  not treated here as a precise national census. Species-level pressures
  include agricultural intensification, pesticide-driven loss of soil insects,
  and loss of nest cavities; a ranked local threat list for Georgia is not
  published here. The EU Birds Directive does not apply in Georgia. Do not
  catch or keep wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people and is not venomous. Wild birds must not be
  captured. Nests can smell unpleasant; that is species-level biology, not a
  sign of disease.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A crested pinkish-cinnamon bird with a long downcurved bill and
    black-and-white barred wings. In Georgia it is a breeding migrant on the
    2024 checklist. Almost nothing else locally looks like it.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 25–32 cm, wingspan about 44–48 cm, mass often 46–89 g (BTO
      mean 67.3 g) at species level. The bill is long, thin, and downcurved,
      black with a pale base. The crest matches the body and is black-tipped;
      it is folded at rest and fanned when the bird is excited. Head, neck,
      and body are pinkish-cinnamon. Wings and tail are black with white bars;
      the tail has a broad white band. Legs are short. The sexes are almost
      alike in the field; females may be slightly duller.
    - >-
      Juveniles are paler, with a shorter bill and a less bold crest. Flight
      is undulating, and the black-and-white wings are then the best cue. The
      call is a soft “up-up-up” series. It stands on the ground and probes
      soil; it does not climb tree trunks like a woodpecker.
    - >-
      No other hoopoe occurs in Georgia. Madagascar hoopoe (Upupa marginata)
      is not on the Georgian checklist. The African form U. e. africana is
      sometimes treated as a separate species and is also absent here. Crested
      larks are small brown songbirds, not this species; this atlas does not
      yet have pages for them. Bee-eaters and rollers are colourful but have a
      different bill and body shape; they also lack pages here. Green
      woodpecker, great spotted woodpecker and wryneck excavate or use holes
      that a hoopoe may later occupy, but they look different; this atlas has
      pages for those species.
      Little owl nests in cavities in open country but is an owl, not a
      hoopoe; this atlas has a page for that species too.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Leave it alone. A crested, long-billed
      bird with black-and-white wings on a lawn or pasture in the warm months
      in Georgia is this species.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the
    OSME Region List for names: order Bucerotiformes, family Upupidae, genus
    Upupa. It was formerly often placed in Coraciiformes. The IOC World Bird
    List treats Madagascar hoopoe (Upupa marginata) as a separate species.
  - >-
    Handbooks often list eight subspecies. The nominate U. e. epops breeds
    from north-west Africa and Europe east through the Caucasus to Central
    Asia. Field identification in Georgia is to species. A measured Georgian
    specimen series assigned to subspecies is not given on this page.
  - >-
    Georgian is ოფოფი; ჩვეულებრივი ოფოფი is also used. The 2024 checklist uses
    Eurasian Hoopoe in English; SABUKO and BirdLife use Common Hoopoe. The
    same Georgian word also names a Samegrelo grape variety; this page is
    about the bird.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: BB; M, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    confirmation, not a regional map. The photographs on this page were not
    taken in Georgia.
faq:
  - question: Is the Eurasian hoopoe found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed breeding bird and migrant (BB; M), with photographs. It does
      not stay the winter.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the warmer months, in open and semi-open country with short grass or
      bare soil and nearby trees, walls, or ruins. This page has no named
      municipality list and does not colour the regional map.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Pinkish-cinnamon body, long downcurved bill, erectile crest,
      black-and-white barred wings. The song is a soft “up-up-up”. Nothing
      else in Georgia looks like this.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 25–32 cm long at species level, with a wingspan of about 44–48 cm.
      BTO gives a mean mass of 67.3 g. A separate measured series from Georgia
      is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly soil insects and larvae, probed from the ground with the bill.
      Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the breeding season and on passage, coded BB; M on the 2024
      checklist. It is not treated there as a winter visitor. Exact arrival
      and departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated on this page.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. BB on the 2024 checklist means it is a confirmed breeder. It uses
      an existing cavity and does not excavate a new one. Local nest records
      are not compiled on this page.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. It is globally Least Concern and is coded LC nationally
      on the 2024 checklist. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Do not
      catch wild birds.
