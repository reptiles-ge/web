  A medium-sized woodland owl with a round head, dark eyes and no ear tufts.
  Confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident and breeder on the 2024

overview: >-
  The tawny owl (Strix aluco) is a medium-sized woodland owl (family Strigidae)
  confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident. The 2024 annotated checklist
  of the birds of Georgia codes it YR-R: a breeder present throughout the year.
  Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality is high
  (H), with photographs and sound recordings. SABUKO lists the Georgian name
  ტყის ბუ (forest owl); ჩვეულებრივი ბუ, რუხი ბუ and ჩვეულებრივი ტყის ბუ are
  also used. Adults are about 37–46 cm long at species level, with no ear
  tufts and dark eyes. It is not a threat to people. IUCN lists the species as
  Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns the same category nationally.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look in woodland, forest edge, and parks or gardens with old
  trees — not evenly on treeless high plateaus or open steppe. This page does
  not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024 checklist confirms
  country-level resident status but does not publish a regional gazetteer.
  Species-level habitat is deciduous or mixed forest, also floodplain woodland,
  cemeteries, large gardens and city parks with mature trees; a cavity is
  needed for nesting. Closed boreal conifer forest is less typical. A separate
  national elevation band for Georgia is not published here. Elsewhere in the
  range it breeds high in mountains (species-level literature cites about
  2,350 m in Turkey); that is not a measured Georgian limit. The atlas map is
  left uncoloured until named Georgian localities are taken from sources that
  actually list them.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range the
  main prey is small woodland mammals, especially voles and mice. Birds,
  amphibians, beetles and earthworms are also taken. In built-up areas birds
  often make up a larger share. Prey is usually swallowed whole; indigestible
  parts are later coughed up as pellets — fur and bone may be found at the
  base of a roost tree. That is species-level diet, not a measured Georgian
  menu.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at night. By day it roosts in the canopy or against a
  trunk; it is often heard before it is seen. It is territorial and stays on
  site year-round; it is not a long-distance migrant. The male territorial
  call is a quavering hoot; the contact call is a short, sharp kee-wick. Both
  sexes can give both calls; the familiar “twit twoo” is often a pair duet,
  not one bird saying both parts. Nesting is usually in a tree hole, less
  often in a building cavity or an old magpie nest; nest boxes are used.
  Species-level clutches are often two or three white eggs; incubation is
  about 28–30 days (BTO figures from the United Kingdom). Young leave the
  cavity some days before they can fly and hide on nearby branches. Those
  figures are from the wider range, not a Georgian nest sample on this page.
  The 2024 checklist treats the species as a breeding resident in Georgia. Do
  not handle it; birds may strike people near the nest.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Strix aluco as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List. The 2024 Georgian checklist assigns LC globally and nationally.
  It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190); among owls that
  list includes barn owl (Tyto alba, EN D1). A Georgian population trend is
  not measured on this page. Documented pressures at species level include
  loss of old cavity trees, road mortality and secondary poisoning from
  rodenticides; that is not a ranked national threat list for Georgia. It is
  listed on CITES Appendix II, so international trade is regulated. Do not
  catch wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and is harmless unless approached. It may strike near a
  nest. Wild birds must not be taken.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A compact medium-sized owl with a round head, dark eyes and no ear tufts.
    Confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident on the 2024 checklist. The
    usual confusion is long-eared owl, which has ear tufts and orange eyes.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length at species level is about 37–46 cm, wingspan about 81–105 cm,
      mass often about 385–800 g. Females are typically larger than males
      (about 5% longer and more than 25% heavier). The head is large and
      round, with no ear tufts. The facial disc is clear; the eyes are dark
      brown or black, not yellow. The bill is short, yellowish and hooked.
      The body is stocky, the wings broad, the tail relatively short.
    - >-
      Plumage has two main types: grey-brown and rufous-brown, with
      intermediates. In both, the underparts are pale with dark lengthways
      streaks. The back is mottled for camouflage. Juveniles are fluffier and
      more barred across; the eye is still dark. NPLG notes grey and
      dark-brown types in Transcaucasia, including Georgia. Field
      identification is to species, not colour type.
    - >-
      Long-eared owl (Asio otus) is also a year-round resident on the 2024
      Georgian checklist; it has long ear tufts, orange eyes and a slimmer
      body. Short-eared owl (Asio flammeus) is an open-country bird, often
      active by day, with short ear tufts. Eurasian eagle-owl (Bubo bubo) is
      much larger, with ear tufts and orange eyes.       Little owl (Athene noctua)
      is small, with yellow eyes; this atlas has a separate page. Eurasian
      scops owl (Otus scops) is small, tufted, and a breeding migrant in
      Georgia; this atlas has a separate page. Barn owl (Tyto alba) has a
      heart-shaped face and is pale; it is on the 2014 Red List and this atlas
      has a separate page. Tengmalm's
      owl (Aegolius funereus) is a smaller forest owl with yellow eyes; this
      atlas has a separate page. It does not yet have pages for long-eared owl,
      short-eared owl or eagle-owl.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Do not handle it. A round-headed, dark-eyed
      owl without ear tufts hooting at night in Georgian woodland is this
      species, not eagle-owl.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows IOC
    and the OSME Region List for names: order Strigiformes, family Strigidae,
    genus Strix. IOC treats Maghreb owl (Strix mauritanica) as a separate
    species of north-west Africa; it is not on the Georgian list. Birds of
    the World still treats mauritanica as a subspecies in some accounts.
  - >-
    The subspecies expected in the Caucasus is S. a. willkonskii (Menzbier,
    1896). The name refers to a collector based in Batumi; that is taxonomic
    history, not a modern range map. NPLG assigns this subspecies to Georgia.
    Range accounts often place willkonskii from north-east Turkey to
    north-west Iran and Turkmenistan. Sharikov and co-authors (2015) separate
    Caucasian willkonskii from eastern European aluco by territorial calls;
    those recordings are from the Russian Black Sea coast, not a Georgian
    sample. Field identification is to species.
  - >-
    Georgian names in use are ჩვეულებრივი ბუ, ტყის ბუ (SABUKO), რუხი ბუ and
    ჩვეულებრივი ტყის ბუ. Avibase lists ბუ and ტყის ბუ. English names are
    Tawny Owl (IOC and the 2024 checklist), also brown owl and Eurasian tawny
    owl.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photo) and SR (sound recording).
    That is country-level confirmation, not a regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is tawny owl found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia treats it as
      a confirmed year-round resident (YR-R): a breeder present all year.
      Evidence is photographs and sound recordings.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In woodland, forest edge, and parks or gardens with old trees. This
      page has no municipality list and does not colour a regional map.
      Presence is confirmed at country level, not by region.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Round head, no ear tufts, dark eyes, stocky body, pale underparts with
      dark streaks. At night listen for a quavering hoot and a kee-wick.
      Long-eared owl has ear tufts and orange eyes.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 37–46 cm long at species level, wingspan about 81–105 cm.
      Females are usually larger than males. There is no separate measured
      Georgian series on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly small mammals, especially rodents; also birds and invertebrates.
      Diet in Georgia is not separately studied on this page.
  - question: When is it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year, according to the 2024 checklist. It is not treated there as a
      passage migrant or winter-only visitor. It is easiest to detect at
      night by voice.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means a breeding resident. The nest is
      usually a tree hole. Georgian clutch dates are not tabulated here.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous. It may strike near a nest; do not approach. It is
      Least Concern globally and nationally on the 2024 checklist. It is not
      on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Do not catch wild birds.
