commonName: Golden jackal
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A medium-sized dog-like predator with yellowish-grey fur and a relatively
  short tail. It is a native resident in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The golden jackal (Canis aureus) is a mammal in the dog family (Canidae). IUCN
  lists Georgia in the native range. It appears on national mammal checklists.
  At species level, head–body length is often about 70–85 cm, the tail is
  shorter than a fox’s, and mass is often about 7–15 kg. It has no venom. The
  main human risk is a bite and rabies, not poison. IUCN lists it as Least
  Concern. It is not treated on this page as a threatened taxon on Georgia’s
  2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia it is more common in the lowlands, with a milder, warmer climate,
  than in the high mountains. Shakarashvili and co-authors (2020) treat it as
  common in Georgia and note that in recent decades it is also seen at higher
  elevations than before. This page does not map administrative regions and does
  not assume even density across the country. A named modern locality is
  Lagodekhi Protected Areas (Kakheti): a photograph dated 6 March 2025,
  credited to the administration. That is a named photo record, not a mapped
  region. Another named site is Ponichala Nature Reserve near Tbilisi: in
  Kalandarishvili’s 2024 doctoral thesis, camera traps in 2020–2021 recorded
  the jackal as the most frequent mammal. The same thesis ran a 2021
  howl-playback survey in Adjara, Imereti,
  Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Racha-Lechkhumi, Samegrelo, and Samtskhe-Javakheti;
  responses differed by region, and density figures depend on assumed detection
  distance. At species level in Europe it uses mosaic farmland, scrub, woodland
  edge, marshes, and the edge of settlements. The IUCN Europe assessment
  typically places it from sea level to 800 m, occasionally to 1,800 m in the
  Italian Alps; a Georgia-specific elevation range in metres is not published
  on this page.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian stomach or scat study on this page. In Europe it
  is an opportunistic omnivore: small mammals, carrion and slaughter remains,
  hunting leftovers, birds, plants, and fruit. The realistic picture in Georgia
  is the same broad menu — including unprotected poultry or small livestock
  where conflict is reported — but local proportions have not been measured
  here.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at dusk and at night; it can also be seen by day. At
  Ponichala camera traps (2020–2021) detections were more frequent in the
  morning, about 6 a.m. to noon, than during the afternoon dog peak — that is
  one site, not a national timetable. It often lives as a pair or small family
  group. It marks territory by howling; the call sits between a fox bark and a
  wolf howl and is also used in surveys in Georgia. There is no Georgian
  recording on this page; the clip is xeno-canto XC1134910 (Stanislas Wroza). It dens in a burrow it digs
  or takes over. At species level gestation is about 63 days; litters are often
  several pups (literature 1–9, averages often about 4–6). Those figures come
  from the wider range, not a Georgian den sample. Tracks resemble a dog’s and
  are larger than a fox’s.
conservation: >-
  Hoffmann and co-authors assess Canis aureus as Least Concern on the global
  IUCN Red List (2018). The European regional assessment (Ranc et al. 2025) is
  also LC with an increasing population; that assessment does not list Georgia
  separately in its native-country table, while the Caucasus is named in the
  wider range. Eliava et al. 2007 list it as LC in Georgia. It is not treated on
  this page as a threatened taxon on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government
  Resolution No. 190). It is listed as a hunting species; the 2011 harvest
  rules close jackal hunting from 1 February to 1 September — current season
  and quota must be checked in official rules and are not copied here as
  current numbers. It is not on CITES appendices at species level. The EU
  Habitats Directive does not apply in Georgia. Local pressures can include
  hunting, road mortality, and conflict over livestock; a national trend is not
  measured on this page, though the 2020 Georgian genetic study notes an
  increase in abundance of both jackal and wolf. Do not handle wild animals.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom. It can bite. In Georgia it is named among wild carnivores
  that can carry rabies. Do not feed or handle it. Keep dogs vaccinated. In an
  unprotected yard it may take poultry or small livestock — that is opportunistic
  feeding, not hunting of people.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium-sized dog-like predator with fairly even yellowish-grey or sandy
    fur, a relatively short tail (without a white tip), and a straighter back
    line than a fox. It is resident in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level head–body is often about 70–85 cm, tail about 20–30 cm,
      shoulder height often about 40–50 cm, mass often about 7–15 kg. Males are
      usually slightly larger than females. There is no separate measured
      Georgian series on this page. The muzzle is broader than a fox’s, the
      ears are erect, and the legs are relatively long.
    - >-
      Colour is yellowish-grey, sandy, or pale brown; the back often has a
      darker “saddle” of mixed black and white hairs. The belly is paler. The
      tail is bushy, typically with a dark tip, not white. Winter fur is longer
      and darker. The African “golden jackal” is a different species (African
      golden wolf, Canis lupaster) and does not occur in Georgia.
    - >-
      The red fox (Vulpes vulpes) also occurs in Georgia: finer muzzle, longer
      bushy tail often with a white tip, black ear backs, and black lower legs.
      The wolf (Canis lupus) is much larger, longer-legged, and heavier in the
      head. A free-ranging dog can
      match size and tracks; ear shape, tail carriage, and voice do not always
      decide. The raccoon dog (Nyctereutes procyonoides) is introduced in
      Georgia: shorter-legged, with a masked face, and has no separate profile
      here yet.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. A bite or saliva can carry rabies risk. Do not handle
      it, especially if an animal approaches without fear, seems disoriented, or
      is aggressive. Daytime activity can also be normal.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758. The accepted name is Canis aureus. Order
    Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Canidae, genus Canis. The type
    locality in the literature is tied to Iran / “the East”, not Georgia.
  - >-
    Since 2015, African populations once treated as the same species are
    classified separately (Canis lupaster). Georgian animals belong to Eurasian
    Canis aureus.
  - >-
    Caucasus and European literature often cites subspecies C. a. moreoticus;
    C. a. caucasica also appears historically. Subspecies boundaries are
    disputed; do not assign a Georgian individual to a subspecies in the field
    with confidence.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual names are ტურა and ჩვეულებრივი ტურა. In English:
    golden jackal; also common jackal, Asiatic jackal, Eurasian golden jackal.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as native (2018 global assessment). It is in
    Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s 2002 annotated mammal list. Eliava et al.
    2007 list it as LC. Shakarashvili et al. 2020 treat most western Georgian
    samples as recent migrants from the east of the country.
faq:
  - question: Is the golden jackal found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. IUCN lists it as native in Georgia. It is on national mammal
      checklists and on the list of hunting species.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      More often in the lowlands and at farmland or settlement edges, at any
      time of year. Named sites are Lagodekhi Protected Areas (photo,
      2025-03-06) and Ponichala Nature Reserve. This page has no municipality
      list. The atlas map is not coloured.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Fairly even yellowish-grey fur, a relatively short tail with a dark tip,
      and a more dog-like outline. A fox has a longer tail, often white-tipped;
      a wolf is much larger.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      At species level head–body is often about 70–85 cm, tail about 20–30 cm,
      mass often about 7–15 kg. There is no separate measured Georgian series
      on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It is an omnivore: small mammals, carrion, leftovers, birds, fruit. Diet
      has not been studied separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      Mainly at dusk and at night; it can also appear by day. It is resident
      and does not migrate like many birds. Howling is often heard.
  - question: Can you hear it?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Territorial howling carries a long way. The clip on this page is
      xeno-canto XC1134910 (Stanislas Wroza); there is no Georgian recording
      here.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous. It can bite. In Georgia it is named among wild
      carnivores that can carry rabies. Do not handle it. After a bite, wash
      the wound and seek medical care.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      It is globally Least Concern and is not treated on this page as a
      threatened taxon on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. It is a hunting species;
      season and quota are set by official rules.
