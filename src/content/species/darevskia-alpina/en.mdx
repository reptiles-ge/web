overview: >-
  The alpine rock lizard (Darevskia alpina) is a harmless high-mountain rock
  lizard of the western Greater Caucasus and a candidate on Georgia’s 2026
  checklist. Darevsky described it in 1967, first as a
  subspecies of the Caucasian rock lizard. The type locality is Terskol in
  Kabardino-Balkaria, at about 2,200 m, in Russia, not in Georgia. The
  Georgian common name points to Abkhazia; the English and Russian names
  refer to high elevation. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 keep the taxon on the
  list, but the account is short: it may be a hybrid between the caucasica
  and saxicola complexes (Murphy 2000; Tarkhnishvili 2012). That paper lists
  no Georgian records, barcodes, or named locality, so this atlas does not
  colour a regional map. International literature places the species in the
  western Greater Caucasus, including Abkhazia and western Georgia — that is
  range from other sources, not a locality list from the 2026 account. It is
  not venomous and is not dangerous to people.
habitat: >-
  At species level this is a high-mountain rock lizard. Tarkhnishvili et al.
  2016, citing Darevsky 1967 and Tuniyev et al. 2009, place it above about
  1,650 m in the Greater Caucasus west of the Baksan valley. Tuniyev 2024
  treats it as a West Caucasus endemic: high
  mountains of several Russian regions, Abkhazia, and western Georgia. That
  is a species-range statement, not this atlas map. The 2026 checklist names
  no Georgian municipality, elevation band, or barcode. Doronin 2013 links
  historical 1912 specimens to Zemo Bachiani / the upper Ksani, at about
  2,296 m — a locality isolated from the known western range and not mapped
  here. Administrative regions are not inferred from habitat.
diet: >-
  Rock lizards of the genus Darevskia typically take small invertebrates —
  insects, spiders, and similar prey on rock, scree, and meadow edges. A
  Georgian stomach or faecal study of this species is not cited on this page.
behavior: >-
  It is a day-active lizard: in warm weather it is seen on rock and in
  subalpine ground. The Reptile Database lists it as oviparous. A separate
  Georgian seasonal calendar, overwintering site, or laying date is not
  published here. At high elevation the active season is short, but that is
  a general expectation, not measured Georgian phenology. Do not handle wild
  lizards.
conservation: >-
  Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat it as a candidate: a possible hybrid origin
  leaves species rank in need of further evidence. On the IUCN Red List,
  Tuniyev and co-authors assess it as Vulnerable (2009; assessment
  e.T11120A114549788). Tuniyev 2024 cites the same VU category with criterion
  B1ab(i, iii, v). Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Decree No. 190) includes Darevskia
  clarkorum, D. dahli, and D. mixta; D. alpina is not on that list. It is not
  read here as a CITES-listed species. Doronin 2013 notes habitat decline
  from human pressure and climate change — a Caucasus-level remark, not a
  measured national trend for Georgia. This atlas does not estimate a
  Georgian population size.

    species. Field identification needs scalation, range, and often genetics.

    taxon.
  traits:
    - >-
      Body and head are typical of a rock lizard: a flattened trunk, a long
      tail, and well-developed limbs. A measured Georgian size series is not
      on this page. Do not name the species from colour in a photograph
      alone.
    - >-
      Tarkhnishvili et al. 2016 describe it as morphologically intermediate
      between Brauner’s rock lizard (D. brauneri, saxicola complex) and the
      Caucasian rock lizard (D. caucasica). Other species in the saxicola
      complex also lack easy morphological diagnoses.
    - >-
      D. caucasica is biologically confirmed on the checklist; named Georgian
      points include Truso (Zakagori), Tbatana, and Lebarde. D. brauneri is a
      candidate; named points include Mestia (Manshura), Martvili, and Lake
      Ertso. Either can look like the same coloured rock lizard.
    - >-
      D. daghestanica is in the caucasica complex; ranges are continuous in
      the upper Terek. D. adjarica is named from south-western Georgia and is
      a different geographic context. Colour does not separate these pairs
      either.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. Risk to people is negligible if the animal is not
      handled. Watch it in the field; do not pick it up.
faq:
  - question: Is the alpine rock lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The 2026 Georgian checklist keeps it as a candidate. The account lists
      no Georgian locality, barcode, or records. Tuniyev 2024 and The Reptile
      Database include Abkhazia and western Georgia / the north-western
      Caucasus in the range. That is literature range, not a coloured map on
      this atlas.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The realistic setting is the high belt of the western Greater Caucasus
      — subalpine rock and meadow, at species level often above about 1,650
      m. The checklist names no village. The Georgian common name points to
      Abkhazia, but the 2026 remark does not confirm that.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Not by colour. Field identification among Darevskia uses scalation and
      range. This taxon is morphologically intermediate between D. brauneri
      and D. caucasica. A single photograph is not enough.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. It may bite if
      handled. Do not pick it up.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      This page has no measured Georgian series. It is a small rock lizard in
      Darevskia. Size is not a basis for naming the species.
  - question: What does it eat, and when is it active?
    answer: >-
      At genus level it takes small invertebrates. It is active by day in the
      warm high-mountain season. Species-specific Georgian diet and calendar
      are not measured here. It lays eggs.
  - question: Why is it a candidate species?
    answer: >-
      The checklist treats it as a candidate. The account allows a hybrid
      origin between the caucasica and saxicola complexes and does not show
      isolation. mtDNA often places it with the saxicola group; morphology is
      also linked to caucasica.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      Globally it is Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List (2009). It is not on
      Georgia’s 2014 Red List. It is a candidate on the checklist. Do not
      take wild animals.
