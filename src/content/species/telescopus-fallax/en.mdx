overview: |-
  The cat snake (Telescopus fallax) is a slender snake that is active mainly at night. In Georgia, it is found mostly in dry, rocky places in the east. It belongs to the Colubridae family. A 2026 Georgian checklist confirms the species’ presence, though the accuracy of information about its range is rated as moderate.
  According to a 2021 Georgian study, the cat snake is found in rocky or grassy places with a dry climate. This page does not provide a specific record of the snake from western Georgia or Colchis.
  The “Records by Region” table on this page’s map confirms its distribution in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti and Tbilisi. For Shida Kartli, only isolated sightings have been recorded so far; these are not considered confirmation of its distribution there.
  Cat snakes living in the Caucasus are often assigned to the subspecies T. f. iberus. Tbilisi (“Tiflis”) is listed as the location of the specimen on which the description of this subspecies is based. The cat snake has venom fangs at the back of its mouth and uses its venom to subdue prey. It is not considered a significant threat to human health, but there is no need to pick it up.
  It is usually about 60–100 cm long. According to the IUCN, the species is of Least Concern.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, the cat snake is mainly associated with dry, rocky, or grassy
  places. It may occur on rocky slopes, around crevices, in scrub, along open
  woodland edges, in ruins, and near dry-stone walls. It climbs rocks and walls
  well.

  By day it often hides under stones, in crevices, or under bark. Only regions
  whose status is “distribution confirmed” on the records-by-region table count
  as distribution. At present those are Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta —
  Mtianeti, and Tbilisi. Specific photographs are from Mtatsminda and Iagluji
  Mountain near Rustavi.

  This page presents no specific record from Samtskhe-Javakheti. Across the
  wider species range it has been described up to about 2,000 m elevation, but a
  separate elevation range for Georgia has not been established here.
diet: >-
  The cat snake’s diet has not been studied separately in Georgia. In other
  parts of the species range it mainly hunts lizards. It also takes small
  snakes, nestling birds, and small mammals.

  Geckos are often included in its diet around the Mediterranean, but that
  should not be treated as a directly confirmed diet for the Georgian
  population.

  It restrains prey with the body and uses rear fangs at the back of the mouth
  to subdue it.
behavior: >-
  The cat snake is nocturnal. It hides by day and hunts in the evening and at
  night. Its large eyes and vertical pupil are linked to activity in low light.

  When threatened, it mostly tries to flee or hide. It may sometimes coil its
  body, hiss loudly, or bite defensively, so do not handle it.

  It is egg-laying. Species-level literature often cites 5–9 eggs, but mating
  season and egg number for the Georgian population have not been published
  separately. Overwintering details in Georgia are also not defined by the
  sources used on this page.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists the cat snake as Least Concern. It is not included in Georgia’s
  2014 Red List.

  In Europe, the species is included in Bern Convention Annex II and EU Habitats
  Directive Annex IV, but the EU legal regime does not automatically apply in
  Georgia.

  In the 2026 Georgian checklist, the cat snake is listed as a biologically
  confirmed species.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It has a slender body and large eyes. It is active mainly at night. Its pupils are vertical, so it may be mistaken for a viper. To identify it, don’t rely on the pupils alone: look at its body shape, smooth scales and the large scales on its head.
  traits:
    -  |-
      The body is slender and almost cylindrical. The head is flat and oval. The boundary between the head and neck is visible but not sharp. The eyes are large. The pupils are vertical and become narrow slits in bright light.
      The scales on the back are smooth. Around the middle of the body, they are often arranged in about 19 rows. Large scales are arranged symmetrically on top of the head. Vipers often have smaller scales on top of their heads.
      Do not pick up a snake to count its scales.
    -  |-
      Its body may be gray, sand colored, brownish or olive colored. It has dark spots or bands across its back, often becoming less distinct toward the rear of the body.
      There are often dark markings on its head. A dark, slanting stripe runs from the eye toward the corner of the mouth. Its belly is pale and may have small dark dots. These markings are usually more distinct in young ones.
      This species is usually about 60–100 cm long. The greatest length measured in Georgia is not listed separately on this page.
    -  |-
      The nose-horned viper (Vipera transcaucasiana) and other vipers have thicker bodies than the cat-eyed snake. Their back scales have a raised line down the middle, and their heads are broader and triangular. The gyurza (Macrovipera lebetina) is an even thicker venomous snake, and its bite poses a serious risk to human health.
      The cat-eyed snake has a slender body, large eyes and smooth back scales. The copper snake (Coronella austriaca), patterned racer (Elaphe dione) and olive-colored racer (Platyceps najadum) have round pupils.
      When identifying an unfamiliar snake, do not rely on just one feature.

facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1831 (Fleischmann), originally as Tarbophis fallax, from the
    eastern Adriatic. The Caucasian form iberus (Eichwald, 1831) has its type
    locality at Tbilisi (“Tiflis”) — iberus refers to ancient Caucasian Iberia,
    not the Iberian Peninsula. The 2026 checklist uses Telescopus fallax at
    species rank.
  - >-
    A vertical pupil is unusual among colubrids. Georgian vipers also have
    vertical pupils — the eye alone is not enough for identification.
  - >-
    A 2019 phylogenetic study notes that the T. fallax group needs revision;
    in practice Georgia is still treated as Telescopus fallax.
  - >-
    On the map’s records-by-region table, distribution is confirmed in Kakheti,
    Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, and Tbilisi; Shida Kartli currently shows
    only individual records. Named photographs are from Mtatsminda and Iagluji
    Mountain near Rustavi.
faq:
  - question: Is the European cat snake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The cat snake is a confirmed species in Georgia. In the 2026
      Georgian checklist it is listed as biologically confirmed. This page
      presents no specific record from western Georgia or Colchis.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      On the records-by-region map table, status “distribution confirmed”
      applies to Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, and Tbilisi. Shida
      Kartli shows only isolated records for now and is not treated as

      Specific photographs are from Mtatsminda and Iagluji Mountain near
      Rustavi. This page has no named record from Samtskhe-Javakheti.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      The cat snake has venom and rear fangs at the back of the mouth, but it
      is not considered a medically significant venomous snake for people. When
      threatened, it mostly tries to flee or hide.

      Do not handle it. Vipers also have vertical pupils, so if you are not
      sure of the species, keep a safe distance.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Look for a slim body, large eyes, vertical pupil, smooth dorsal scales,
      large shields on the top of the head, and dark cross-blotches on the
      back. It is mainly active in the evening and at night.

      Vipers are usually more heavily built and have keeled scales. Smooth
      snakes and many day-active racers have round pupils.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Species length is usually about 60–100 cm. A separate measured size
      series for the Georgian population is not presented in the sources used on
      this page.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is mainly active at dusk and at night. The 2021 Georgian study lists
      it as nocturnal. By day it often hides under stones, in crevices, or in
      other shelters, so it is sometimes found by chance when lifting stones or
      on walls.

      If you find a snake under a stone, do not touch it by hand.
  - question: What does it eat, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      The cat snake is egg-laying. Species-level literature often describes
      5–9 eggs.

      It feeds mainly on lizards, but the Georgian population’s diet and egg
      number have not been fully studied separately.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Do not approach the cat snake or pick it up. It does not seek people and
      usually avoids conflict.

      If you are not sure about the species of a vertical-pupilled snake, keep
      your distance for safety and let the snake move away.
