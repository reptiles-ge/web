commonName: Dahl's whip snake
location: Eastern and southern Georgia; local western records
description: >-
  Dahl's whip snake is a non-venomous snake, thin as a whip, and not a viper. It is found mainly in dry, stony places in eastern and southern Georgia.
overview: |-
  Dahl's whip snake (Platyceps najadum) is a non-venomous, very slender snake and is not dangerous to people. It is linked to dry climates. In Georgia it is found mostly in dry, rocky places in the east and south. In eastern Georgia it is mainly known from rocky and grassy places. Adults are usually about 70–100 cm long and rarely exceed 1 metre. According to 2026 data, its presence in Georgia is confirmed, and a modern specimen comes from Tmogvi in Aspindza Municipality. It belongs to the Colubridae family and is not considered to be at high risk of extinction.
habitat: |-
  Dahl's whip snake prefers drier places, especially the rocky and grassy areas of eastern Georgia. In Georgia, it may occur on dry, rocky slopes, near cliffs, in scrub, at forest edges, on dry meadows and near farmland. It shelters in cracks between stones and in rock hollows, and in winter it has also been seen in buildings made by people. Across the wide area where this species lives, it has been seen at elevations of up to about 2,000 m.

  Its [distribution](/snakes/gavrtseleba) is confirmed in Kakheti, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Adjara, Tbilisi, Shida Kartli and Imereti. In modern times, it has been seen near Tmogvi and in Imereti. Isolated cases are known from Racha-Lechkhumi and Kvemo Svaneti, as well as from Abkhazia, but its presence there has not yet been confirmed. Old museum data show it was also found in Tbilisi and Borjomi. These old data, however, do not show precisely where it lives today.
diet: >-
  Elsewhere in its range, Dahl's whip snake mainly hunts lizards. It also catches small mammals, birds and other snakes. It does not use venom when hunting: its main weapon is speed.
behavior: |-
  In other parts of its range, Dahl's whip snake is active during the day. It moves quickly and often warms itself in the sun. It lays eggs: this species is often described as laying 3–16 eggs.
  In 2026, one Dahl's whip snake in Georgia was found in a shared winter shelter inside a reinforced-concrete septic tank. It was wintering there together with 133 [red-bellied racers](dolichophis-schmidti) and one [dice snake](natrix-tessellata). This was only one case and does not mean that this species usually spends the winter with other snakes.
  When threatened, Dahl's whip snake usually tries to escape. If it has no way out, it may raise the front part of its body and open its mouth. So if you come across one, leave it a way to get away.
conservation: |-
  According to the available assessment, Dahl's whip snake faces a low risk of extinction. It is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List. In Europe, it is protected under Appendix II of the Bern Convention and Annex IV of the EU Habitats Directive. However, these EU rules do not automatically apply in Georgia. The 2026 Georgian list confirms its presence, while the accuracy of data about its distribution is rated as moderate.
interaction: |-
  Dahl's whip snake has no venom and is not dangerous to people. Still, do not pick it up: a large snake may [bite you](/snakes/gvelis-nakbeni) in self-defense, and the bite may be painful. If a snake's pupil is vertically elongated, it is not Dahl's whip snake. Watch an unfamiliar snake from a safe distance, take photos from afar and leave it a way to get away.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Dahl's whip snake is not venomous and has a very slender, whip-like body. It is easiest to recognize by the dark spots on its neck and the front part of its body, which have pale edges. Unlike [vipers](/venomous-snakes) and the [Levantine viper](macrovipera-lebetina), it has a round pupil. But when identifying an unknown snake, do not rely on this feature alone.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is very slender and looks like a whip. The head is narrow, the eyes are relatively large, and the pupils are round. The scales on the back are smooth, and the tail is long. Around the middle of the body, the scales usually lie in 19 rows, rarely in 17. Do not pick up a snake to count its scales.
    -  |-
      The body is usually olive, gray or rusty brown. On the neck and the front part of the body it has dark spots with light edges. Toward the rear the spots get smaller and often fade out completely, leaving the back end almost plain.
      Some snakes have a light stripe in front of and behind the eye, running from top to bottom. The belly is light-colored. In many young snakes the spots on the neck are clearer.
    -  |-
      Dahl's whip snake has its clearest spots mainly on the neck and the front of the body. The [Spotted Whip Snake](hemorrhois-ravergieri) (Hemorrhois ravergieri), by contrast, has dark, diamond-shaped spots along much of its body. The [Red-bellied racer](dolichophis-schmidti) (Dolichophis schmidti) is larger, and adults are often almost entirely reddish.
      The [Urartian ratsnake](elaphe-urartica) (Elaphe urartica) is stouter, with a dark head and large spots. The [Steppe ratsnake](elaphe-dione) (Elaphe dione) has a finer pattern and often has stripes running along the body as well. The [European cat snake](telescopus-fallax) (Telescopus fallax) has a vertical pupil, like a narrow line, and is mostly active at night. So don't rely on a single feature: from a distance, look at where the spots are, how thick the body is and what colour it is, all together.
    - >-
      Dahl’s whip snake has round pupils and a very slender body. Vipers, including the Levantine viper, have vertical pupils, stretched from top to bottom, and thicker bodies. Still, [identifying an unknown snake](/snakes/shxamiani-gvelis-amocnoba) by its pupils or head shape alone is not reliable. If a snake has vertical pupils, it is not Dahl’s whip snake. But that does not mean it must be a viper or a Levantine viper: in Georgia, the European cat snake has pupils like this too. So do not approach an unknown snake, not even to look at its pupils.
    -  |-
      Yes. Dahl's whip snake is a confirmed species in Georgia and is listed that way in the 2026 Georgian list as well. A modern specimen with a known location comes from Tmogvi in Aspindza Municipality. The snake is mainly known from dry, rocky places in eastern and southern Georgia, but this does not mean it occurs evenly across the whole country.
facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1831 (Eichwald), originally as Tyria najadum; the type
    locality is Baku, not Georgia. Older literature often used Coluber najadum
    or Zamenis dahlii. The modern genus Platyceps became standard after Schätti
    & Monsch 2004. Caucasian animals belong to the nominotypical subspecies
    (P. n. najadum).
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist specimen and COI barcode are from Tmogvi (Aspindza;
    CaBOL-ID 1027410). The same paper notes about 6.65% COI difference between
    Georgian and Armenian sequences — a reason to revisit taxonomy, not a
    completed species split.
  - >-
    The Georgian name reflects the olive–buckthorn dorsal tone. The English
    name whip snake refers to the extremely slender, whip-like body. Light-edged
    dark neck spots are the main field mark.
faq:
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to humans?
    answer: |-
      No. Dahl's whip snake has no venom and is not dangerous to human health. When it feels threatened, it usually tries to escape, but if you pick it up, it may bite you.
      If a snake's pupil is vertical, like a thin line, it is not a Dahl's whip snake. Do not go near a snake like that, and leave it a way to get away.
  - question: Is Dahl’s whip snake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes, Dahl's whip snake lives in Georgia: Georgia's 2026 species list includes it as a confirmed species. In recent times it has been seen around Tmogvi. Still, it is not found equally everywhere in the country.
  - question: Where might I encounter it in Georgia?
    answer: |-
      Dahl’s whip snake is found mainly in eastern and southern Georgia, in dry, rocky or grassy places. Its presence has been confirmed in Kakheti, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Adjara, Tbilisi, Shida Kartli and Imereti. One modern sighting comes from the area around Tmogvi in Samtskhe-Javakheti. In Racha-Lechkhumi and Kvemo Svaneti, as well as in Abkhazia, only isolated cases are known. So its presence in these regions is not yet confirmed.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: |-
      Dahl's whip snake has a very thin, whip-like body, smooth scales and a round pupil. Dark spots with pale edges mark its neck and the front part of its body. The rear part is often almost plain.
      In the Spotted Whip Snake, the spots run along a much larger part of the back. If a snake has a vertical pupil, it is not Dahl's whip snake. Still, when identifying an unfamiliar snake, do not rely on just one feature.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: |-
      An adult Dahl's whip snake is usually 70–100 cm long and rarely exceeds 1 metre. Across the wider area where this snake occurs, larger snakes are also known, about 1.35–1.40 m long.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      In other parts of its range, Dahl's whip snake feeds mainly on lizards. It also hunts small mammals, birds and other snakes. It has no venom and catches its prey with speed.
  - question: When is it active, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: |-
      Elsewhere in its range, Dahl's whip snake is active during the day. It reproduces by laying eggs, often 3–16 of them. In Georgia, one Dahl's whip snake was found in winter in a human-built structure. It was sheltering there together with other non-venomous snakes.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: |-
      Watch the snake from a safe distance, take a photo and let it go on its way. Do not pick it up. If the snake has a vertical pupil, it is not a Dahl's whip snake. Venomous vipers and other snakes may also have this kind of pupil. So pupil shape alone will not tell you which snake it is.
