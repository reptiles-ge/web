commonName: Golden eagle
location: Year-round resident in Georgia
description: >-
  A large dark-brown eagle with a golden nape. Confirmed in Georgia as a
  year-round resident and breeder on the 2024 national bird checklist.
overview: >-
  The golden eagle (Aquila chrysaetos) is a large bird of prey that hunts by day. It belongs to the family Accipitridae. The subspecies Aquila chrysaetos homeyeri has been recorded in Georgia. It nests there and remains year-round; its presence in Georgia has been confirmed by a photograph. Adults are dark brown, with lighter, golden-rust feathers on the back of the head and neck. It poses no direct threat to people during an ordinary encounter, but people should not approach its nest. Globally, it is considered of least concern. Georgia’s 2014 Red List assessed it as vulnerable (VU), while a 2024 bird checklist lists it as of least concern (LC) in Georgia. The two assessments differ.
habitat: >-
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level resident status but does not publish a
  regional gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly in lowland towns or
  treeless Colchis plains. Abuladze and Shergalin (2002) link breeding to the
  southern slopes and spurs of the Greater Caucasus Main Ridge (including
  Gagra, Bzipi, Chkhaltha, Kodori, Svaneti, Egrisi, Racha, Kharuli, Alevi,
  Mtiuleti, Gudamakari, Kartli, and Kakheti) and, in the Lesser Caucasus, the
  Ajara–Imereti Ridge. In the nesting season it uses the upper forest belt and
  alpine meadows where open ground is available for hunting. In winter in
  Caucasia it is more nomadic and may appear in lowlands, plateaus, semi-desert,
  and floodplains of large rivers; coastal wetlands of the Black Sea are noted
  there as vagrant records. Caucasian nests they measured were at about
  920–2400 m, most often 1400–1800 m; hunting grounds are often above the nest,
  toward the treeline. Named historical nest or egg records in Georgia:
  Lagodekhi Reserve, Matzimi Gorge, 1840 m (1978); Dariali Gorge on the Terek,
  1480 m (1982); Bzipi Ridge (1984); Dusheti district, Lomisi Ridge, 1910 m
  (1988); Kazbegi district near Sno, 1870 m (1990); Borjomi Nature Reserve,
  Kvabiskhevi Gorge (1991). The Aragvi Protected Landscape website names the
  species among important local raptors; that is a park list, not a survey table
  published on this page.
diet: >-
  Of 189 identifiable prey items studied in the Greater Caucasus of Georgia and northwestern Azerbaijan between 1977 and 1999, 88 (46.6%) were mammals and 101 (53.4%) were birds. The most common were Caucasian grouse (Tetraogallus caucasicus, 16.4%), European hare (Lepus europaeus, 12.7%), Caucasian snowcock (now Lyrurus mlokosiewiczi; called Tetrao mlokosiewiczi in the study, 12.7%), and chukar (Alectoris chukar, 8.5%). Other prey included rodents, young wild goats and domestic goats, martens, quail, domestic chickens, and other birds living in forests or rocky places. What it ate varied from year to year and from place to place. It also feeds on carrion, especially in winter, including remains of animals killed by wolves. These records describe prey studied in those places in the past, not what the species eats across Georgia in the 2020s.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. Territorial pairs occupy nest areas for years; a pair
  may keep several nests (up to four in that study) and use them in rotation.
  In Caucasia Abuladze and Shergalin recorded no tree nests: sites were on
  inaccessible cliffs, niches, and canyon walls in the forest belt. Pairs
  appear at nest sites from late February to early March. Eggs are laid mainly
  in the second half of March; incubation is about 44–45 days. Of 41 monitored
  clutches most had two eggs (mean 1.85). Hatching was in the first two weeks
  of May; young left the nest from late July to early August. In all successful
  nests only one young fledged (n = 43). The 2024 checklist treats the species
  as a breeding resident in Georgia and does not code it as a passage migrant.
  In winter in Caucasia it is more nomadic than in the breeding season. It is
  often silent; NPLG gives a yelping “kek-kek-kek”. Do not handle wild birds or
  approach nests.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Aquila chrysaetos as Least Concern on the
  IUCN Red List (2021). The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global and
  national Red List category LC. Government Decree No. 190 of 2014 lists the
  species on Georgia’s Red List as მთის არწივი, Aquila chrysaetus (spelling in
  the decree), status VU, criterion D1 (small population). That is the legal
  list; the 2024 ornithological checklist codes it LC nationally. The European
  Red List of Birds annex reports 25–35 breeding pairs in Georgia (2018–2019,
  partial method), a stable short-term trend (2005–2018), and an unknown
  long-term trend. Abuladze and Shergalin (2002) estimated not more than 30
  breeding pairs in Georgia, about 20 on the southern macroslope of Greater
  Caucasia in the east, and discussed a possible rise toward 35–40 pairs in the
  1990s. A complete 2020s national census is not on this page. The same paper
  links the historic decline mainly to 1940–1970, food shortage, traps,
  poisoned baits, nest disturbance, and the former campaign against birds of
  prey; by 2002 illegal shooting, traps, and poison remained the main threats.
  Do not catch or kill wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It poses no threat to people during an ordinary encounter. It has large talons, and a pair behaves aggressively near the nest. Do not pick it up or approach the nest. Catching or killing this Red List species is prohibited by law.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a large, dark brown eagle with a golden nape and a relatively long tail. According to 2024 information, it lives in Georgia year-round. Young birds have a white area at the base of the tail and often white patches on their wings. Adults no longer have these markings.
  traits:
    - >-
      This species is about 66–102 cm long, with a wingspan of about 1.8–2.34 m. The wing measures 600–685 mm, the tail 330–360 mm, and the bird weighs 3.5–4.5 kg. Females are larger than males, but they have the same colouring. In A. c. homeyeri, the wing measures about 55–64 cm in males and 60–70 cm in females; weight is about 2.9–6 kg. This page gives no separate measurements for Georgia. Adults have dark brown backs and wings. The feathers on the back of the head and neck are lighter, golden or rust-coloured. Feathers cover the legs down to the toes. The soft area at the base of the beak and the feet are yellow; the claws are black. In flight, the long, broad wings show feathers spread like fingers at their tips. The tail is longer than that of other large eagles.
    - >-
      In its first few years, a young bird has a tail that is white at the base, with a broad black band at the tip. White patches are often visible under its wings, too. Adults no longer show such a sharp contrast between the colors. It takes several years to develop full adult coloring. When observing the bird in the wild, size is a more useful clue to its sex than color.
    - >-
      The 2024 list records the eastern imperial eagle (Aquila heliaca) in Georgia as a year-round resident, a migrant and a winter visitor. Adults have a pale head and white patches on their shoulders. It is more often found in lowlands and open areas and looks less like an eagle that nests on high mountain cliffs. The steppe eagle (Aquila nipalensis) is listed as a summer visitor and a migrant, but not as a year-round resident. The greater spotted eagle (Clanga clanga) and lesser spotted eagle (Clanga pomarina) have a more compact build and different patterns on their wings. The white-tailed eagle (Haliaeetus albicilla) is larger and more heavily built. Its tail is wedge-shaped and is white in adults. The common buzzard (Buteo buteo) is much smaller. The griffon vulture, black vulture and bearded vulture have different shapes and fly differently. This atlas does not yet have separate pages for these eagles.
    - >-
      It poses no direct threat to people on an ordinary hike. Do not pick it up. A large, dark eagle seen in the mountains with a golden nape is more likely to be this species than a buzzard. Young birds also have a white base to the tail.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Falco chrysaetos; the accepted name is
    Aquila chrysaetos. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the OSME Region List
    and names the Georgian form Aquila chrysaetos homeyeri (Mediterranean
    Golden Eagle). IOC and ITIS treat homeyeri as a subspecies. Some genetic
    work brings homeyeri close to nominate chrysaetos; field identification is
    to species.
  - >-
    The official Georgian name is მთის არწივი. ოქროსფერი არწივი is a calque of
    English Golden eagle and is used in search. The Red List decree spells the
    scientific name chrysaetus; the accepted spelling is chrysaetos.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R, data quality A, global and checklist-national
    Red List LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). The legal Red
    List (2014) is VU D1. That is country-level confirmation plus the named
    historical nest records, not a complete regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the golden eagle found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident (YR-R): a breeder present throughout the
      year.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the upper mountain-forest belt, rocky gorges, and nearby alpine
      meadows, mainly in the Greater and Lesser Caucasus. Named historical nest
      sites include Lagodekhi, Dariali, Lomisi, Sno, Borjomi’s Kvabiskhevi, and
      the Bzipi Ridge. This page does not map every region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A large dark-brown eagle with a golden nape and legs feathered to the
      toes. Juveniles show a white tail base and often white wing patches. It is
      much larger than a buzzard.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 66–102 cm long at species level, with a wingspan of about 1.8–2.3
      m. NPLG gives mass as 3.5–4.5 kg; females are larger than males. A
      separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      In a 1977–1999 prey sample from Greater Caucasia in Georgia and adjacent
      Azerbaijan the main items were Caucasian snowcock, hare, Caucasian black
      grouse, and chukar; it also takes carrion. That sample is historical.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year, according to the 2024 checklist. It is in the mountains in the
      breeding season; in winter in Caucasia it is more nomadic and may appear
      in the lowlands.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means resident breeder. In the Caucasian
      study eggs were laid mainly in the second half of March, and young left
      the nest in late July or early August.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless on ordinary encounters; do not approach nests. It is
      globally Least Concern. The 2014 Red List lists it as VU D1; the 2024
      checklist codes it LC nationally. Do not catch wild birds.
