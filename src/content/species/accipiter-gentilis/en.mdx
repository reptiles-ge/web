commonName: Eurasian goshawk
location: Year-round resident, migrant and winter visitor in Georgia
description: >-
  A large woodland hawk with short broad wings, a rounded tail and a white
  eyebrow. Confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident, a passage migrant and
  a winter visitor on the 2024 national bird checklist.
overview: >-
  The Eurasian goshawk (Accipiter gentilis) is a large woodland raptor of the
  family Accipitridae. The usual Georgian name is ქორი; დიდი ქორი is sometimes
  used to separate it from the sparrowhawk. The 2024 annotated checklist of the
  birds of Georgia codes it YR-R; M; WV: a breeder present throughout the year,
  and also a bird of passage and a winter visitor. The English checklist name is
  Eurasian Northern Goshawk. Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A).
  Occurrence quality is high (H), with photographic evidence. Adults are about
  49–64 cm long at species level; females are clearly larger than males. It is
  not a threat to people. IUCN lists the species as Least Concern. The 2024
  checklist assigns the same category nationally.
habitat: >-
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level resident, migrant and winter-visitor status
  but does not publish a regional gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly
  across Georgia, including treeless high plateaus. Abuladze (2011) treats it in
  Georgia as an uncommon or locally rare year-round resident with local vertical
  movements. For breeding it prefers mature or middle-aged dense forest —
  deciduous, coniferous, or mixed. Passage is described as more frequent in the
  lowlands of western Georgia and in the valleys of large rivers in the east;
  winter visitors are called widespread, but that is not a municipality list.
  The Ilia State University SPA checklist gives YR-R, M; the 2024 Sandgrouse
  checklist adds WV. In autumn the eastern Black Sea bottleneck near Batumi
  (Sakhalvasho, Shuamta) is a watchpoint, but the species is scarce there, passes
  low and solitarily, and complete counts are difficult. A separate national
  elevation band for Georgia is not published here.
diet: >-
  A tabulated Georgian diet study is not given on this page. Abuladze (2011)
  collected prey remains and pellets at nests, but his prey list is not
  reproduced here. At species level it takes medium-sized birds (pigeons,
  corvids, thrushes, gamebirds) and mammals (squirrels, rabbits). It hunts by
  surprise in woodland or at the forest edge. In western Georgia it was
  traditionally flown by bazieri falconers at common quail; that is cultural use,
  not a diet sample for this page. It is not food for people.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. In woodland it is fast and agile: short broad wings and a
  relatively long tail with a rounded tip. Flight is often flap-flap-glide, but
  the silhouette is bulkier than a sparrowhawk — deeper chest, broader wing
  bases, a comparatively shorter tail. The call is a loud, resonant kek-kek-kek
  or similar series, slower and deeper than the sparrowhawk, mostly near the
  nest. The recording on this page is xeno-canto XC396773 (Alvaro Ortiz
  Troncoso): an adult female alarm call at an urban cemetery in Germany, not a
  Georgian recording. Do not handle it; birds may be aggressive at the nest. The
  nest is a stick platform in a tree. At species level clutches are often two to
  four eggs and incubation about five weeks; NPLG gives 2–4 eggs in April and
  notes that incubation length for the Caucasian form is not established. That
  is not a Georgian nest sample on this page. The 2024 checklist treats the
  species as a breeding resident in Georgia. Northern populations migrate; local
  birds stay more often, sometimes shifting altitude. Georgia has all three
  components: local breeders, passage birds, and winter visitors.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Accipiter gentilis as Least Concern on the
  IUCN Red List. The 2024 Georgian checklist assigns LC globally and nationally.
  Abuladze’s 2011 summary speaks of about 240–250 breeding pairs, after a decline
  in the 1950s–1970s and a moderate increase in the 1980s–1990s; that is a
  then-current estimate, not a 2024 national census. A Kartli-area paper in the
  same 2011 volume gives 20–25 pairs locally — a regional figure, not a national
  one. The 2014 Georgian Red List (Decree No. 190) includes Levant sparrowhawk
  (Accipiter brevipes, VU), not this species. Accipitridae are on CITES Appendix
  II. Local pressure is documented in the Batumi bottleneck: illegal shooting
  and traditional trapping for falconry in Adjara. Do not take wild birds
  without a permit. Species-level historical pressures include persecution,
  felling of mature forest, and pesticides; a measured recent Georgian population
  trend is not given on this page.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people and is not venomous. The talons are strong; do
  not handle it and do not approach nests. A bulky hawk appearing suddenly in
  deep woodland in Georgia is more likely this species than a sparrowhawk. It is
  not food.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large woodland hawk with short broad wings, a rounded tail, a dark cap and
    a white eyebrow. Adults are slate-grey above with fine barring below;
    juveniles are brown with lengthwise spots. In Georgia the usual confusion is
    with Eurasian sparrowhawk.
  traits:
    - >-
      The silhouette is bulkier than a sparrowhawk: deep chest, broad wing
      bases, a comparatively short tail with a rounded tip. Flight is
      flap-flap-glide, not the pointed wings of a falcon. Adults have an orange
      to reddish eye and yellow legs. At species level length is about 49–64 cm
      and wingspan often about 90–125 cm. Females are clearly larger than males.
      A separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this page.
    - >-
      Adults have a dark cap and ear-coverts with a bold white supercilium. The
      back is slate-grey; the breast and belly are pale with fine dark
      cross-barring. Juveniles are brown above and cream below with lengthwise
      teardrop spots — not the adult’s fine bars. The eye is yellower in
      juveniles.
    - >-
      Eurasian sparrowhawk (Accipiter nisus) is much smaller, with thinner legs,
      a longer almost square-tipped tail and a weak eyebrow; this atlas has a
      separate page for it. Levant sparrowhawk (Accipiter brevipes; IOC
      Tachyspiza brevipes) is smaller still, with more pointed wings and a dark
      eye; on the 2024 checklist BB; M and VU on the national Red List. Common
      buzzard (Buteo buteo) is a broad-winged soaring raptor, not a woodland
      hawk. Peregrine is a pointed-winged falcon. Shikra (Accipiter badius) is
      casual (Cas) on the checklist. This atlas does not yet have a separate
      page for Levant sparrowhawk.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Leave it alone. A small hawk in a Georgian
      garden is more likely a sparrowhawk; a bulky silhouette and white eyebrow
      in deep forest point to this species.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Falco gentilis; later placed in Accipiter.
    The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the OSME Region List for names: order
    Accipitriformes, family Accipitridae, genus Accipiter. IOC World Bird List
    now treats the species as Astur gentilis. Field identification is to
    species; the checklist still uses Accipiter gentilis. Since 2023 the North
    American form is a separate species (Astur atricapillus); Georgian birds are
    Eurasian.
  - >-
    Abuladze (2011) and NPLG assign Caucasus birds to Accipiter gentilis
    caucasicus Kleinschmidt, 1923. Other current references often synonymize
    caucasicus with A. g. marginatus, or include the Caucasus in nominate A. g.
    gentilis. That disagreement is reported here; field identification stays at
    species. Eastern and Japanese subspecies are not split for Georgia on this
    page.
  - >-
    Georgian is ქორი. დიდი ქორი is sometimes used to distinguish it from
    sparrowhawk. English on the 2024 checklist is Eurasian Northern Goshawk; IOC
    uses Eurasian Goshawk. The older combined name northern goshawk also covered
    the North American species. Russian is ястреб-тетеревятник. Turkish is çakır
    kuşu.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes are YR-R; M; WV, data quality A, global and national
    Red List LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is
    country-level confirmation with the named literature, not a full regional
    map.
faq:
  - question: Is the Eurasian goshawk found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia confirms it as a
      year-round resident, a passage migrant and a winter visitor (YR-R; M; WV).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In mature or middle-aged dense forest. Passage is described as more
      frequent in western Georgian lowlands and large river valleys in the east,
      following Abuladze 2011. It is scarce in the Batumi bottleneck in autumn.
      This page does not map every region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A bulky woodland hawk with a white eyebrow, dark cap, short broad wings
      and a rounded tail. Adults are grey with fine-barred underparts; juveniles
      are brown with lengthwise spots. Sparrowhawk is much smaller.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 49–64 cm long at species level, with a wingspan often about 90–125
      cm. Females are clearly larger than males. A separate measured series from
      Georgia is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly medium-sized birds and mammals. Diet in Georgia is not tabulated on
      this page.
  - question: When is it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year as a resident, and also as a passage migrant and winter visitor,
      according to the 2024 checklist.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means a breeding resident. Named nest
      sites in Georgia are not listed on this page.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. Globally and nationally LC on the 2024 checklist. It is
      not on the 2014 Georgian Red List (Levant sparrowhawk is VU there). Do not
      take wild birds.
