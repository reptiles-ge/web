commonName: "Meadow Lizard"
location: "Eastern and central Georgia — Sioni, Tushurebi"
description: >-
  The Meadow Lizard is a small, non-venomous lizard of eastern and central Georgia that lives on the ground rather than on rocks. You cannot tell it apart from similar lizards by colour alone.
overview: >-
  The meadow lizard (Darevskia praticola) is a small lizard that lives on the ground in eastern and central Georgia. Most lizards in the genus Darevskia live on rocks, but this species is an exception. It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. Its head and body are usually less than 6.5 cm long. It belongs to the true lizard family (Lacertidae), and the subspecies living in Georgia is D. p. praticola. Georgia’s 2026 species list treats it as a confirmed species, not merely a possible one. Globally, a 2009 assessment rated it as near threatened, but it is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Lizards in the northwest have a separate name: the Pontic meadow lizard (Darevskia pontica), which is still regarded only as a possible species. In damp Colchis in the west and along the Black Sea coast, the Pontic meadow lizard is found instead. So do not look for the meadow lizard there.
habitat: |-
  The Meadow Lizard lives at the edges of deciduous forests, in cleared or grassy openings within forests, and in forest meadows. Look for it on the ground, among fallen leaves, rather than on steep rock faces. Most lizards in the genus Darevskia live on rocks, but this species and Derjugin's Lizard (D. derjugini) are exceptions.

  In Georgia, it is known from the eastern and central parts of the country. Recently it has been seen at the Sioni Reservoir and at Sioni–Tushurebi. Both places are in Mtskheta-Mtianeti, and Tushurebi lies in Tianeti Municipality. This does not mean the lizard lives only in these two places. Earlier it was also reported from Lagodekhi, the Tsiv-Gombori Range, the Surami Pass in Shida Kartli and the Kartli ranges. The museum record from Lagodekhi dates from 1980, and the one from the Surami Pass from 1963. Information about its distribution is moderately reliable.

  It is still unclear whether loriensis, which lives in Armenia, also occurs in eastern Georgia near the Armenian border. So do not treat its presence there as confirmed. In Armenia, the main group of this species is recorded in forested areas at about 600–2,000 m above sea level. This figure applies to Armenia and should not be carried over to Georgia. Do not look for the Meadow Lizard in the Colchis Lowland or along the Black Sea coast.

  On the atlas map, no region has a confirmed status in the records-by-region table. iNaturalist obscures every public coordinate for this species by about 28 km, so these records cannot confirm a region and the map does not count any region as range. iNaturalist treats the Pontic Meadow Lizard (D. pontica) as a subspecies of the Meadow Lizard, so records from western Georgia (Adjara, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Imereti) may belong to it.
diet: >-
  In Bulgaria, the Meadow Lizard mainly eats insects, spiders and similar animals. There it often eats spiders, beetles and leafhoppers, small insects that resemble cicadas, but it usually avoids ants. It catches prey with its mouth and has no venom.
behavior: |-
  It is active during the day: it moves on the ground and warms itself at forest edges or in grass. Observations in Serbia show that on especially hot days it may stop moving and seek shade. It lays eggs. In Serbia, it mates in April–May and lays eggs once a season in June, often 4–8 of them. In Azerbaijan, the Hyrcanian subspecies lays 1–6 eggs at a time.

  When it senses danger, it runs away. If you catch it, its tail may come off, or it may bite you. Do not pick it up: watch it and leave it where it is.
conservation: |-
  Globally, the Meadow Lizard is close to being threatened: it was assessed this way in 2009 because its range is split into separate parts far from each other. This older assessment covers the species' whole wide range, not just the lizards living in Georgia. It is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red List (Decree No. 190). Nor is it listed under the agreement that regulates international trade in wild animals. The 2026 list, however, confirms its presence with biological evidence. Locally, clearing forest edges and breaking them into fragments are named as threats. Do not catch wild lizards.

  According to 2016 data, the species splits genetically into three groups: in the Balkans, the western Greater Caucasus and Transcaucasia. These data do not fully support the subspecies distinguished by appearance. It is also disputed whether the Pontic Meadow Lizard (D. pontica) is a separate species or a subspecies. The same list still keeps it as a candidate species, because it has not been shown to differ genetically enough from the other groups.

  traits:
    - >-
      It is found on the ground, not on sheer rock faces. Its head is less flattened than in rock-dwelling Darevskia lizards. The back is often brown or olive-brown, and a pale stripe often runs along the side. This look is common, but colour alone cannot tell the species apart. So note where you saw it and the shape of its head.
    - >-
      Head and body length, not counting the tail, is usually less than 6.5 cm. Females are often slightly larger than males. Only a few adults have been measured in eastern Georgia. Do not pick up a lizard to count its scales.
    - >-
      You cannot tell the Pontic Meadow Lizard (D. pontica) from the Meadow Lizard by colour alone. Where you saw the lizard is a more reliable clue than the pattern on its back. The name refers to the northwestern Pontic groups, and the specimen used to describe it came from Gagra. Derjugin's Lizard (D. derjugini) also lives on the ground, but it belongs to a different forest-dwelling group and is more widespread in the west. DNA data also exist for one of its specimens collected in Sioni, so it and the Meadow Lizard may occur in the same place. Colour does not tell them apart. The Sand Lizard (Lacerta agilis) is larger, has a different pattern and belongs to another lizard genus. Rock-dwelling Darevskia lizards may also live in the same valley, but their heads are flatter. So pay most attention to where you saw the lizard and what shape its head is.
    - >-
      The Meadow Lizard was first described by Eversmann in 1834, who named it Lacerta praticola. That description is linked to a place in the North Caucasus (Kislovodsk / Narzan), not Georgia. Since 1997, the lizard has been placed in the genus Darevskia. Figures in one paper show its head scales and the plates in front of the anus. These figures are linked to the Sioni Reservoir.
    - >-
      You cannot tell the Meadow Lizard from the Pontic Meadow Lizard (D. pontica) by color. A more reliable clue is the place where you saw the lizard. In Georgia, the Meadow Lizard lives in the eastern and central parts of the country. In damp Colchis in the west and along the Black Sea coast, the Pontic Meadow Lizard occurs instead. The 2026 list treats the Meadow Lizard as a confirmed species, not just a possible one.
faq:
  - question: Is the meadow lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 list confirms the Meadow Lizard's presence in Georgia with biological evidence. It lives in the eastern and central parts of the country, but it is not found throughout Georgia.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It lives in east-central Georgia, at forest edges and in grassy clearings. Recently it has been seen at Sioni and at Tushurebi in Tianeti. It is also reported from Lagodekhi, Tsiv-Gombori and the Surami Pass. There is little point looking for it on the Colchian coast. On the atlas map, no region has a confirmed status: the coordinates of every iNaturalist record are obscured.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. The Meadow Lizard has no venom. If you catch it, it may bite you, and its tail may come off. So don't pick it up: leave it where you found it.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      It is a small ground lizard of forest edges, and its head is not flat. You cannot identify it by colour alone. A flat-headed lizard seen on a rock is a different Darevskia species. In the northwest, a similar lizard may be the Pontic Meadow Lizard (D. pontica), and in the same forest it may be Derjugin's Lizard (D. derjugini). So pay most attention to where you saw the lizard and the shape of its head.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Usually less than 6.5 cm. That is the length of the head and body; the tail is not included.
  - question: Is it the same as the Pontic meadow lizard?
    answer: >-
      No. The northwestern Pontic lizards are known as the Pontic Meadow Lizard (D. pontica), and they may be a separate species. However, it has not been fully established that they are genetically distinct from other groups. Scientific publications classify this lizard in different ways. You cannot tell it apart from the Meadow Lizard by color.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It eats mainly spiders, beetles and other small animals that have no backbone. This was shown by a study in the Balkans.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is active during the day in the warm season. In the Balkans, this lizard is active from spring until the end of summer. That timing comes from the Balkans, so do not apply it directly to Georgia.
