overview: |-
  The mottled scorpion (Mesobuthus eupeus) belongs to the Buthidae family. In 2022, the species was reexamined and its range was limited to the Caucasus region. The original specimen has been lost, so a female collected in Tbilisi was chosen in its place to define the species.
  In Georgia, the mottled scorpion is found in Tbilisi, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Shida Kartli and Samtskhe-Javakheti. It prefers dry, warm places and often hides under stones, in crevices or in other sheltered spots.
  It also uses its stinger and venom to subdue prey. A scorpion sting can be painful and sometimes cause other symptoms. Avoid picking up a scorpion or touching one carelessly.
habitat: |-
  In the Caucasus and neighboring areas, Mesobuthus eupeus is mostly found in warm, dry, rocky places or open grassy areas. During the day, it often hides in a sheltered place.
  A region marked on the map does not mean the species is found equally in every kind of environment there. A single record of it being seen in the wild is not enough to confirm its distribution in a region.
diet: |-
  Like other scorpions, the mottled scorpion is a predator. It mainly hunts insects and other small animals that have no backbone and have jointed legs.
  Scorpions catch prey with their pincers; they do not always use their stinger and venom.
behavior: |-
  Scorpions spend most of their time hidden. During the day, they often hide under stones, in crevices or in other sheltered places. They become more active in the evening and at night.
  Do not catch or move a scorpion with your hands. If you need to move one safely in your home or yard, use a suitable container and a stiff card without touching it, or ask a specialist for help.
conservation: >-
  The presence of Mesobuthus eupeus in Georgia has been confirmed.
interaction: |-
  The mottled scorpion is venomous, but it does not seek out people or try to attack them. Most stings happen by accident when someone touches or presses the scorpion or disturbs it in its hiding place.
  Do not touch a scorpion with your hands. If the pain is severe or a child has been stung, seek medical care promptly. If you have trouble breathing, lose consciousness, or have other serious symptoms beyond the sting site, call 112. Read about what to do first in the [guide](/scorpions/morielis-nakbeni).

identification:
  summary: >-
    An adult scorpion measures about 4–6 cm in total length. The yellowish-brown scorpion has two pincers. Its tail has several sections and ends in a venomous stinger. A photo alone does not always allow reliable identification of its species. An expert’s assessment is often needed.
  traits:
    - >-
      A scorpion has two pincers. Its tail is made up of several parts, curves upward and has a stinger at the end. This is one of the main ways to tell a scorpion from a pseudoscorpion: a pseudoscorpion also has pincers, but no tail or stinger.
    - >-
      The body of Mesobuthus eupeus is often yellowish or brownish yellow. It may also have dark spots or stripes on its back. Color alone is not enough to identify this species with certainty.
    - >-
      Many scorpions in the Buthidae family have relatively slender pincers and well-developed tails. These features alone, however, cannot identify the species with certainty. A clear close-up photo or an expert’s assessment is more reliable.
    - >-
      An adult [Caucasian scorpion](olivierus-caucasicus) (Olivierus caucasicus), like this species, has a dark, brownish back and relatively slender pincers. The two species can be confused in a photo taken from above. Color and the overall shape of the pincers alone are not enough to tell them apart reliably. A specialist also checks other features, including the number of teeth on their comb-like organs.
    - >-
      In older sources, the names Buthus eupeus and Mesobuthus eupeus may have been used more broadly. The way these species were divided was revised in 2022. So older records do not necessarily refer to the species now called Mesobuthus eupeus in the narrower sense.
facts:
  - >-
    The accepted name is Mesobuthus eupeus (C. L. Koch, 1839). The protonym is
    Androctonus eupeus C. L. Koch, 1839; older sources may also use Buthus
    eupeus.
  - >-
    Kovařík et al. 2022 restrict Mesobuthus eupeus sensu stricto to the Caucasus
    region and recognize no subspecies.
  - >-
    The map marks distribution as confirmed in Tbilisi, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli,
    Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Shida Kartli, and Samtskhe — Javakheti. Individual field
    records elsewhere do not carry the same status.
  - >-
    The Georgian name used here is ჭრელი მორიელი. English sources often use
    mottled scorpion; older broad usage also used lesser Asian scorpion.
faq:
  - question: Is the mottled scorpion found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The presence of Mesobuthus eupeus in Georgia has been confirmed. The map marks Tbilisi, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Shida Kartli and Samtskhe-Javakheti as places where its presence has been confirmed.
  - question: Is it a scorpion, spider, or insect?
    answer: >-
      The mottled scorpion is an arachnid (Arachnida). Spiders belong to this group too, but scorpions are not spiders. Insects belong to an entirely different group.
  - question: How risky is it to people?
    answer: |-
      A scorpion sting can be painful. Sometimes symptoms appear beyond the site of the sting. Do not touch or pick up a scorpion with your hands.
      If the pain is severe or a child has been stung, seek medical care promptly. Call 112 if breathing becomes difficult, the person loses consciousness, or other serious symptoms occur. Read the [guide](/scorpions/morielis-nakbeni) for what to do first.
  - question: In which regions is distribution confirmed?
    answer: >-
      The map shows confirmed presence in Tbilisi, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Shida Kartli and Samtskhe-Javakheti. It also shows an isolated record of a sighting in the wild in Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti, but presence there is not marked as confirmed.
  - question: Why do older sources say Buthus eupeus?
    answer: >-
      Buthus eupeus is an older scientific name for this species. This page calls it Mesobuthus eupeus and uses the narrower definition of the species established in a 2022 review.
