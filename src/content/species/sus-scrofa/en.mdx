commonName: Wild boar
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A large even-toed ungulate with coarse bristles, a long snout, and, in
  males, upward-curving tusks. It is a resident species in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The wild boar (Sus scrofa) is a year-round mammal in Georgia, in the pig
  family (Suidae). It is the wild ancestor of the domestic pig and the same
  species. IUCN lists Georgia in the native range. It appears on national
  mammal checklists and on the list of hunting species. At species level,
  head–body length is about 90–200 cm, shoulder height 55–110 cm, and mass
  often about 44–320 kg, depending on subspecies and sex. It has no venom.
  The main human risk is a charge or a tusk wound, not poison. IUCN lists it
  as Least Concern. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in deciduous and mixed forest, floodplain woodland,
  scrub, ravines, and woodland edges where cover and food occur together. At
  species level the highest densities are often in oak forest; it also enters
  crops when the plants give cover. A named Georgian locality is the Iori
  floodplain hunting farm (2020 management plan): suitable habitat there is
  listed as oak, poplar, floodplain scrub, gullies and ravines; fields,
  arable land, gardens and marshes only occasionally. The same plan says it
  enters the farm episodically in summer when livestock are on summer
  pastures, and that it moves through the year. The Georgian encyclopaedia
  names floodplain and mountain forest to about 2,500 m; that is a secondary
  source and is not treated here as a measured national elevation series.
  This page does not map administrative regions. Country occurrence is
  confirmed, but a municipality-by-municipality list is not compiled here.
  Do not assume even density across the whole country.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range it
  is an opportunistic omnivore: roots, tubers, grass, oak and beech mast,
  berries, insects, snails, carrion, refuse, and sometimes small vertebrates.
  In Europe it often damages crops and grassland. The realistic picture in
  Georgia is the same — forest mast, floodplain cover, and sometimes fields —
  but the local menu has not been measured here.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at dusk and at night; a long rest by day and a shorter
  rest in the early afternoon are typical. Where hunting pressure is high it
  shifts further toward night activity. Sows with piglets travel in a sounder;
  adult males are often solitary outside the breeding season. It wallows in
  mud and then rubs on trees — also a territorial mark. At species level
  gestation is about 112–130 days, often with 5–7 piglets; larger or smaller
  litters are less common. Before farrowing the sow leaves the group and
  builds a nest. In the temperate zone piglets are born mainly in spring. The
  Georgian encyclopaedia places the rut in November–December; that is a
  secondary source and is not treated here as a measured Georgian calendar.
  Piglets have longitudinal pale stripes; that pattern fades at about 2–6
  months. The track is a cloven hoof; on soft ground the dew claws often
  print as well. Rooted soil and wallows are common field signs. Calls are
  common in a sounder; no Georgian recording is on this page. The clip is
  xeno-canto XC1104344 (Marc Anderson, Mango Lagoon, Queensland, Australia,
  30 August 2018).
conservation: >-
  Keuling and Leus assess Sus scrofa as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List
  (2019). There is no major global threat; local pressures can include habitat
  loss, hunting, killing after crop damage, and hybridisation with domestic
  pigs. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution No. 190).
  It is listed as a hunting species (Order No. 175); quotas and seasons are
  set by agency documents and are not copied here as current numbers. The
  Georgian encyclopaedia states that numbers in the country do not exceed
  2,000; year and method are not given here, so that figure is not treated as
  a current census. In 2007 African swine fever was confirmed in Georgia;
  the first clinical cases are linked to Poti, most likely from discarded
  ship meat. The disease affects domestic and wild pigs and does not infect
  people. The FAO summary at the time had no confirmed wild-boar cases, but
  contact with free-ranging village pigs was considered possible. A national
  population trend is not measured on this page. Do not handle wild animals.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom. It can charge or cut with its tusks, especially a sow with
  piglets or a cornered male. Do not feed it or approach it. Keep dogs back.
  African swine fever is not a human disease. If meat is taken legally,
  undercooked pork can carry Trichinella at species level; there is no
  separate Georgian survey on this page.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large even-toed ungulate with a short neck, a long snout, coarse
    bristles, and a straight back line. Males often show upward-curving
    tusks. It is resident in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level head–body is about 90–200 cm, tail 15–40 cm, shoulder
      height 55–110 cm, mass often 44–320 kg. Females are usually about 80%
      the size of males. There is no separate measured Georgian series on
      this page. The Georgian encyclopaedia gives males to 2 m body length,
      130 cm at the shoulder, and 50–300 kg; those are maxima in a secondary
      source, not a sample measured here. The snout is long, the ears erect,
      the eyes small.
    - >-
      The coat is coarse bristle, often with a softer underwool. Colour runs
      from brown to almost black and often greyer with age. In males the
      upper canines curve out and up; the lower canines stay sharp by
      rubbing. In females the tusks are smaller and often hidden. Piglets
      have longitudinal pale stripes on a brown ground — a reliable age mark.
    - >-
      Georgia has no other wild suid. The domestic pig is the same species
      and hybridises freely; a “pure wild” animal and an escaped domestic
      pig cannot always be separated in the field. Roe deer (Capreolus
      capreolus) is slimmer, longer-legged, and has antlers in males, not
      tusks. Brown bear (Ursus arctos) is much larger and leaves a
      plantigrade print, not a cloven hoof.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. The risk is physical: a charge and tusks. Do not
      approach a sow with piglets. Do not handle it.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758. The accepted name is Sus scrofa. Order
    Artiodactyla (even-toed ungulates), family Suidae, genus Sus. It is not a
    ruminant.
  - >-
    Subspecies counts are disputed (roughly 4 to 16 or more, depending on the
    author). The Georgian encyclopaedia assigns Georgia to Sus scrofa attila.
    The IUCN Wild Pig Specialist Group profile limits attila to the northern
    flank of the Caucasus and excludes Transcaucasia (Georgia, Armenia,
    Azerbaijan); Groves notes that pigs north and south of the Caucasus
    differ sharply and that Transcaucasian boars are not attila. Do not
    assign a Georgian individual to a subspecies in the field with
    confidence.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual name is გარეული ღორი; the male is often ტახი, the
    female ნეზვი, the young გოჭი. In English: wild boar, Eurasian wild pig,
    wild pig. The domestic pig is often written Sus scrofa domesticus or Sus
    domesticus — a domesticated form of the same species.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as native. It is in Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s
    2002 annotated mammal list. It is on the hunting-species list. That is
    country-level confirmation, not a regional map.
