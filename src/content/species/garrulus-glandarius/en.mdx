commonName: Eurasian jay
location: Year-round resident in Georgia
description: >-
  A medium-sized woodland corvid with a black crown in the Caucasus form and a
  bright blue wing panel. Confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident and
  breeder on the 2024 national bird checklist.
overview: >-
  The Eurasian jay (Garrulus glandarius) is a medium-sized crow-family bird
  (Corvidae) confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident. The 2024 annotated
  checklist of the birds of Georgia codes it YR-R: a breeder present throughout
  the year. Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality
  is high (H), with photographic evidence. The usual Georgian name is ჩხიკვი;
  ჯაფარა is also used. Adults are about 32–35 cm long at species level, with a
  pinkish-brown body, black moustache, white rump, and a bright blue black-barred
  wing panel. The Caucasus form has a largely black crown. It is not a threat to
  people. IUCN lists the species as Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns
  the same category nationally.
habitat: >-
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level resident status but does not publish a
  regional gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly across Georgia, including
  treeless high plateaus. NPLG treats it as a resident throughout Georgia, with
  only minor local wandering. Named locality: the Agency of Protected Areas
  lists ჩხიკვი among birds of Algeti National Park (Algeti gorge). A Commons
  photograph from 26 May 2024 documents the species in Tbilisi. Species-level
  habitat is deciduous and mixed woodland, especially with oaks, plus forest
  edge, parks, large gardens, and wooded suburbs. Closed treeless steppe and
  alpine grassland above the tree line are not typical. A separate national
  elevation band for Georgia is not published here.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range the
  diet is mixed: acorns and other large seeds, nuts, fruits and berries,
  insects and other invertebrates, and occasionally eggs, nestlings, or small
  vertebrates. In autumn it caches acorns in soil and leaf litter and retrieves
  many of them later; unrecovered caches help oaks regenerate. That scatter-
  hoarding role is documented at species level and is biologically relevant
  wherever oaks and jays co-occur; it is not a measured Georgian food study.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. The usual call is a loud, harsh screech; birds also
  chatter and are well known for mimicking other sounds, including buzzard-like
  mews. Flight is often short and undulating between trees; the white rump and
  blue wing panel show clearly then. Nesting is in a tree or tall shrub. Species-
  level clutches are commonly about four to seven eggs; those figures are from
  the wider range, not a Georgian nest sample on this page. The 2024 checklist
  treats the species as a breeder in Georgia. It is resident rather than a
  long-distance migrant; NPLG notes only slight wandering within the country.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Garrulus glandarius as Least Concern on the
  IUCN Red List. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global and national
  Red List category LC. A Georgia-specific population trend is not quantified on
  this page. Do not catch or keep wild birds. Loss of mature oak woodland and
  persecution of corvids would be biologically relevant pressures at species
  level; local threat ranking for Georgia is not published here.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people; wild birds must not be captured. It may take
  eggs or nestlings of smaller birds, which is natural predation, not a human-
  safety issue.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A pinkish-brown woodland corvid with a black moustache, white rump, and a
    bright blue black-barred wing panel. In Georgia the Caucasus form usually
    shows a largely black crown. Magpies and crows lack that blue wing panel.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 32–35 cm and wingspan about 52–58 cm at species level; mass
      is often about 140–190 g in handbook ranges. Body plumage is warm pinkish-
      brown to rufous-grey. The throat is pale with a bold black moustache
      stripe. The rump is white and conspicuous in flight. The outer wing shows
      a striking blue panel finely barred with black — the most reliable field
      mark at any distance.
    - >-
      Georgian birds belong to the black-capped atricapillus group. NPLG names
      the form in the country as Caucasian jay, G. g. krynicki (Kaleniczenko,
      1839), distinguished by a largely black crown (not the pale, black-
      streaked crown of many north-west European birds). Sexes are alike in the
      field. Juveniles are duller, with a less neat crown pattern, but still
      show the blue wing panel.
    - >-
      Eurasian magpie (Pica pica) is also a year-round resident in Georgia on
      the 2024 checklist: black-and-white with a long graduated tail, without a
      blue barred wing panel; this atlas has a page for that species. Northern
      raven (Corvus corax) is a much larger all-dark corvid; this atlas has a
      page for that species too. Rook, hooded crow, and jackdaw are larger or
      greyer/blacker corvids without the jay’s pink-brown body and blue wing
      patch. Northern nutcracker is irregular/scarce (Cas; WV on the checklist)
      and is heavily spotted white on brown. Alpine and red-billed choughs are
      mountain birds with red or yellow bills. This atlas does not yet have
      separate pages for rook, crow, jackdaw, nutcracker, or choughs.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Leave it alone. A pinkish woodland bird with
      a harsh screech and a flash of blue on the wing in Georgian parks or oak
      forest is this species.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Corvus glandarius; now placed in Garrulus.
    The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the OSME Region List for names: order
    Passeriformes, family Corvidae, genus Garrulus. IOC World Bird List treats
    Garrulus glandarius as a polytypic species; some authorities split white-
    faced and plain-crowned Asian forms as separate species, which does not
    change the Caucasus resident treated here.
  - >-
    Handbooks place G. g. krynicki in north-east Turkey and the Caucasus within
    the atricapillus (black-capped) group. Field identification in Georgia is to
    species; subspecies is inferred from geography and the black crown. Recent
    mitochondrial work finds a well-differentiated Caucasus lineage centred on
    krynicki — useful taxonomic context, not a field character.
  - >-
    Georgian is ჩხიკვი; ჯაფარა is an alternate common name. English names in use
    are Eurasian jay (2024 checklist) and jay. The specific epithet glandarius
    means “of acorns”.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R, data quality A, global and national Red List LC,
    occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    confirmation plus the named Algeti and Tbilisi records cited above, not a
    complete regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the Eurasian jay found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident (YR-R): a breeder present throughout the
      year.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In deciduous and mixed woodland, forest edge, parks, and wooded towns.
      Named sources place it in Algeti National Park and document a May 2024
      photograph in Tbilisi. NPLG describes it as a resident across the country.
      This page does not map every region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Pinkish-brown body, black moustache, white rump, and a bright blue black-
      barred wing panel. In Georgia the crown is usually largely black
      (Caucasian form). Magpies and crows lack that blue panel.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 32–35 cm long at species level, with a wingspan of about 52–58 cm.
      Handbook mass is often about 140–190 g. A separate measured series from
      Georgia is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Acorns and other seeds, fruits, insects, and occasionally small vertebrates
      or nest contents. It caches acorns in autumn. Diet has not been studied
      separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year, according to the 2024 checklist. It is not treated there as a
      passage migrant or winter-only visitor.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means resident breeder.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. It is globally Least Concern and is coded LC nationally on
      the 2024 checklist. Do not catch wild birds.
