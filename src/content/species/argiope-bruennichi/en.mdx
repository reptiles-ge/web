commonName: Wasp spider
location: Western and eastern lowlands
description: >-
  A yellow-and-black banded orb-weaver. It is native in Georgia and is not a
  medical risk to people.
overview: >-
  The wasp spider (Argiope bruennichi), also known in Georgian as Brunich's argiope, is an
  orb-weaving spider. It is especially easy to recognize in females: the abdomen has bold
  yellow and black bands, which is why the English name is “wasp spider”.

  The species is widely distributed in Georgia and is considered native to the local
  fauna. Caucasus spider data also describe it as a very common species in Georgia. This
  does not mean that it occurs in equal numbers in every region of the country — local
  density depends on environment and habitat.

  The female's body length is about 10–22 mm, while the male is much smaller, about 7–8
  mm. These measurements do not include the legs.

  Argiope has venom and uses it mainly to subdue prey, especially insects. For people, its
  bite is usually not considered a medically significant danger. Handling the spider is
  still not recommended.

  Argiope bruennichi has not been assessed by the IUCN. The species is not included in
  Georgia's 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  Argiope is often found in low grass and meadows, where the female builds an orb web
  close to the ground. The species' habitat is especially described as humid meadow
  vegetation. This is a general species description, not an elevation belt defined for
  Georgia.

  In Georgia, records of Argiope bruennichi are known from Tbilisi and Kvemo Kartli,
  Kakheti, Adjara, Guria, Imereti, Mtskheta–Mtianeti, Samegrelo, and Abkhazia. Individual
  records also exist from Samtskhe–Javakheti, Shida Kartli, and Racha–Lechkhumi–Kvemo
  Svaneti, but on this atlas map a region is treated as distribution only when the
  records-by-region table gives it confirmed status.

  This does not mean that the species definitely does not occur in other regions.
  Likewise, “widely distributed in Georgia” does not mean that Argiope occurs equally in
  every habitat and every elevation belt in the country.
diet: >-
  Argiope catches prey in an orb web. Its food is mainly different insects, including
  wasps, flies, grasshoppers, and other relatively large invertebrates that enter the web.
  The spider usually wraps captured prey in silk first and then bites it.

  This is the species' general feeding behaviour. The diet of the Georgian population has
  not been studied separately, so listing a specific “Georgian menu” would not be correct.
  Argiope feeds on insects, but it should not be treated as a deliberate “biological
  control” tool in a garden.
behavior: >-
  By day the female often sits in the centre of the web, head down. A white zigzag band,
  the stabilimentum, is often visible in the middle of the web. Its exact function is not
  fully settled; it may be involved in attracting prey, making the web more visible, or
  making it noticeable to other animals.

  Adult females become especially conspicuous in the second half of summer. From Georgia,
  for example, there is a record from 20 July 2006 near David Gareja, although a complete
  countrywide seasonal activity calendar is not presented on this page.

  The eggs are placed in a brown, papery-looking sac. The species overwinters in the egg
  stage, and young spiders emerge in spring.

  The male is much smaller than the female and often appears near her web during the
  mating period. The female may eat the male during mating, although this is general
  species behaviour and not a separately confirmed Georgian observation.

  There is no need to handle Argiope — it is better to observe it calmly near the web.
conservation: >-
  Argiope bruennichi has not been assessed globally by the IUCN, and its status is NE —
  Not Evaluated. The Ilia State University biodiversity database assesses the species as
  LC at national level, although this is not an official state Red List status.

  Argiope bruennichi is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red List. Among arachnids, that
  list includes the fen raft spider (Dolomedes plantarius), which has VU — Vulnerable
  status.

  In northern Europe, Argiope has expanded its range in recent decades. Genetic studies
  also treat the Caucasus as one of the important regions of diversity for the species,
  including on the basis of samples from Georgia.

  Do not catch wild spiders; leave them in their natural environment.
interaction: >-
  The spider has venom for prey. It is not a medical risk to people: bites are
  rare and usually only local pain or redness. This is not a viper bite. An
  allergic reaction to any bite is possible; if breathing, the face, or general
  condition worsens, call 112. Do not handle it. A web in garden grass in summer
  is typical; destroy it only if it blocks a path — the spider is not a pest.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large orb-weaving spider with bold yellow-and-black cross-bands on the abdomen. A
    white zigzag band is often visible in the middle of the web. The female is much larger
    than the male.
  traits:
    - >-
      The female's body length is about 10–22 mm, while the male is about 7–8 mm. Legs are not
      included in this measurement. These are general species data.
    - >-
      The female has a silvery, shiny cephalothorax. On the upper side of the abdomen, bold
      white and yellow cross-bands are visible on a black or dark background, giving the
      species its characteristic “wasp-like” appearance. The underside of the abdomen is dark,
      with yellow stripes and spots. The legs are yellowish and have brown rings.

      The male is much less colourful — mostly silvery-grey or yellowish, with the abdominal
      bands less clearly visible.
    - >-
      Argiope builds a large orb web, often in grass and fairly close to the ground. A white
      zigzag band, the stabilimentum, is often visible in the middle of the web. An adult
      female usually sits in the centre of the web, head down. This is one of the easiest ways
      to recognize Argiope in the field.
    - >-
      Georgia has another species in the same genus — Argiope lobata. It has pronounced side
      projections, or lobes, on the abdomen, and its colour is more silvery and spotted. It
      does not have the bold yellow-and-black “wasp-like” bands typical of Argiope bruennichi.

      Other orb-weaving spiders, including species of the genus Araneus, often have a
      cross-shaped or different pattern on the abdomen.

      Argiope is not a wasp: a spider has eight legs, no wings, and usually sits in its own
      web.
    - >-
      A separate profile for the lobed argiope (Argiope lobata) is available in this atlas.

    The accepted name is Argiope bruennichi (Scopoli, 1772). The World Spider
    Catalog treats the species as accepted. Range: Europe, North Africa, Turkey,
    the Middle East, Russia (Europe to the Far East), the Caucasus, Iran,
    Central Asia to China, Korea, Japan.

    A. lobata. Seropian et al. 2023 repeat that Araneidae count (47 species in
    Georgia) and do not list A. bruennichi among the specimens in that paper.

faq:
  - question: Is the wasp spider found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Argiope (Argiope bruennichi) is confirmed in Georgia and is considered a native
      species in the local fauna. This is a country-level assessment and does not mean that it
      is equally distributed in every region or habitat.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Confirmed distribution regions are Tbilisi, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti, Adjara, Guria,
      Imereti, Mtskheta–Mtianeti, Samegrelo–Zemo Svaneti, and Abkhazia. Individual records
      also appear from Samtskhe–Javakheti, Shida Kartli, and Racha–Lechkhumi–Kvemo Svaneti,
      but on this page those remain records only and are not counted as distribution until
      the table status is confirmed.

      It is especially likely to be seen in grassy places and meadows in summer, when adult
      females become more conspicuous.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      The female is easy to recognize by the bold yellow-and-black cross-bands on the abdomen.
      Its orb web often also has a white zigzag band. The male is much smaller and plainer in
      colour.

      In Georgia it can be confused with the lobed argiope (Argiope lobata), but that species
      has pronounced lobes on the sides of the abdomen and lacks the characteristic
      yellow-and-black bands.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      The female's body length is about 10–22 mm, while the male is about 7–8 mm. Legs are not
      included in this measurement. These are general species data.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      Yes, Argiope has venom, which it uses to subdue prey. For people, its bite is not
      considered a medically significant danger. Bites are rare and usually cause only a local
      reaction.

      Do not handle the spider. If strong or unusual symptoms occur, call 112.
