commonName: European roe deer
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A small deer. Summer coat rusty-brown, winter greyer; a white rump patch
  shows when it flees. It is a resident species in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The European roe deer (Capreolus capreolus), in Georgian შველი, is a mammal in
  the deer family (Cervidae). It is the western species of the genus Capreolus.
  Georgia’s Agency of Wildlife states it is the only roe deer in the country.
  The ASM Mammal Diversity Database lists Georgia in the native range. At
  species level, body length is often about 107–126 cm, shoulder height 66–83
  cm, and mass 22–32 kg; the Agency’s Georgian and English pages give different
  figures (see Identification). It has no venom. The main human risks are road
  collision, free-ranging dogs, and picking up a hidden fawn — not a bite. It
  is an important forest herbivore and prey for large carnivores. IUCN lists it
  as Least Concern. The Agency states it is not on the Red List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in deciduous and mixed woodland, at forest edges, in
  open woodland with undergrowth, and on farmland next to cover. The Agency
  names sparse deciduous or mixed forest with well-developed undergrowth as
  preferred habitat. At species level it also uses more open ground, especially
  at dusk. This page does not map administrative regions. Country occurrence is
  confirmed, but a municipality-by-municipality list is not compiled here. Do
  not assume even density across the whole country.

  Camera-trap monitoring by NACRES (2020–2021) recorded it in all three Adjara
  study areas — Mtirala National Park, Kintrishi Protected Areas, and
  Machakhela National Park — with 2,188 photos in total. Independent-photo
  trapping rates were highest in Mtirala (6.68 per 100 trap-days), then
  Machakhela (3.14) and Kintrishi (1.31). In Machakhela every camera unit
  recorded roe deer; distribution there was more even than for chamois. Most
  photos were taken in spring and summer, with a clear summer peak in Mtirala
  and lower rates in autumn and winter. Early winter placement in the programme
  favoured lower trails when snow was deep around 1,400 m; that is a survey
  choice, not a published national elevation limit for the species.

  It is named as an indicator species for Algeti National Park (NACRES
  biomonitoring shortlist). The Agency of Protected Areas lists roe deer among
  the main mammals of Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park. It is listed in the
  Tusheti ungulate community and as present in Kazbegi National Park, without
  a separate census on this page. A Georgia-specific elevation range in metres
  is not published here.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range it is
  a selective herbivore: shoots, leaves, forbs, buds, and sometimes crops. In
  winter it takes more woody browse. It is not a predator. Georgia’s Forest
  Biodiversity situation analysis notes that forests supply leaves, nuts and
  roots used by roe deer and wild boar. The realistic picture in Georgia is
  woodland edge and open forest, but the local menu has not been measured here.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at dusk and dawn; it can also be seen by day where
  disturbance is low. In the Adjara camera data, photo frequency was highest in
  spring–summer and lower in autumn–winter. Males shed antlers in
  October–November; new antlers grow in velvet. At species level the rut is in
  July–August. After mating the embryo stays dormant for several months
  (embryonic diapause) and active development resumes in late winter; fawns are
  born in May–June, often one or two. Newborns are spotted and lie hidden in
  vegetation for the first days — that is not abandonment. Those figures come
  from the wider range and Agency text, not a Georgian birth sample.

  Tracks are cloven and elongated; at a run the hind print covers the front.
  When fleeing, the white rump patch opens. It is heard in the field,
  especially when alarmed; no Georgian recording is on this page. The clip is
  xeno-canto XC1059462 (Maxime Raux, Castellane, Alpes-de-Haute-Provence,
  France, 18 July 2025).

  Ecologically it is a browser and a prey animal. In Georgia, Eurasian lynx,
  grey wolf, and (where present) Caucasian leopard are documented or routinely
  listed as taking medium ungulates; lynx profiles on this atlas treat roe deer
  as a typical forest prey. Exact diet shares for Georgian carnivores are not
  measured on this page.
conservation: >-
  Lovari and co-authors assess Capreolus capreolus as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List (2016). Georgia’s Agency of Wildlife states it is not on the Red
  List; Government Resolution No. 190 (2014) is not read here as listing this
  species. It appears on the Georgian hunting-mammal list as შველი; quotas and
  seasons are set by agency documents and are not copied here as current
  numbers. EUNIS places Cervidae on Appendix III of the Bern Convention; that
  is not a national hunting ban.

  In Adjara, camera traps recorded it in all three protected areas in
  2020–2021, with the most photos in Mtirala. The Algeti indicator list names
  poaching, disturbance, and disease as pressures. Georgia’s Forest Biodiversity
  situation analysis treats illegal hunting of forest ungulates — including roe
  deer — as a serious, long-standing problem, especially in mountain regions. In
  Mtirala, camera traps also recorded dogs without people, some resembling
  hunting breeds — a local disturbance and chase risk. A national population
  trend is not measured on this page. Do not handle wild animals.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom. It does not normally attack people. A male in the rut may
  strike with antlers if you approach too closely. A fawn lying in grass is
  normal — do not pick it up. Keep dogs under control in woodland. Night-time
  road collision is a real risk. Do not feed it.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small deer with a short tail and a white rump patch. Summer coat is
    rusty-brown, winter greyer. Only males grow antlers. It is resident in
    Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level (Sempéré et al. 1996, European samples) body length is
      about 107–126 cm, shoulder height 66–83 cm, mass 22–32 kg. The tail is
      short. The Agency’s Georgian page gives length about 150 cm and mass
      10–25 kg; the English page gives 95–135 cm (excluding the tail) and
      15–30 kg. The figures do not match. This page uses the Mammalian Species
      range for field size until a measured Georgian series is published. Males
      are usually slightly larger than females. Antlers are short, often with
      three tines on each side.
    - >-
      Summer coat is rusty-brown; winter coat is greyer and thicker. The belly
      is paler. A white rump patch is a useful mark when the animal flees.
      Fawns have white spots on the back that fade after the first months.
      Females have no antlers.
    - >-
      Red deer (Cervus elaphus) also occur in Georgia: much larger, longer-
      legged, with more complex antlers; they are listed as Endangered on the
      national Red List and remain mainly in protected areas. A roe deer is not
      a red-deer calf — they are separate species. Siberian roe deer
      (Capreolus pygargus) is larger and is not accepted here as part of
      Georgia’s fauna; the Agency names only C. capreolus. Bezoar goat
      (Capra aegagrus) has permanent curved horns, a beard in adult males, and
      a different outline on steep slopes. Wild boar (Sus scrofa) is heavier,
      with tusks not antlers, and a rounder print. Tur and chamois also differ
      in silhouette and habitat; this atlas does not yet have separate pages for
      red deer, tur, or chamois.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. Do not handle it, especially a fawn. Watch from a
      distance.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758, originally as Cervus capreolus. The accepted
    name is Capreolus capreolus. Order Artiodactyla (even-toed ungulates),
    family Cervidae, subfamily Capreolinae, genus Capreolus.
  - >-
    The genus has two living species: western C. capreolus and eastern
    C. pygargus. Historical subspecies include C. c. caucasicus (Dinnik, 1910).
    Lister and others treat caucasicus provisionally as the large form north of
    the Caucasus. Do not assign a Georgian individual to a subspecies in the
    field with confidence.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual names are შველი and ევროპული შველი. In English:
    European roe deer, western roe deer, roe deer. A fawn is a young of this
    species, not another deer.
  - >-
    ASM lists Georgia as native. It is on national mammal checklists. That is
    country-level confirmation, not a regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the European roe deer found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is resident. The Agency of Wildlife names it as the only roe deer
      in Georgia. It is on national mammal checklists and on the hunting-species
      list.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      At woodland edges, in open deciduous or mixed forest, and in undergrowth.
      Camera traps confirm Mtirala, Kintrishi, and Machakhela, with the highest
      trapping rate in Mtirala. It is also named for Algeti, Borjomi-Kharagauli,
      Tusheti, and Kazbegi National Park. This page has no municipality list.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A small deer with a short tail, rusty in summer and greyer in winter, and
      a white rump patch when it runs. Only males have short antlers. Red deer
      are much larger; bezoar goat has curved permanent horns.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      At species level shoulder height is about 66–83 cm, body length 107–126
      cm, mass often 22–32 kg. Agency figures differ. There is no separate
      measured Georgian series on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Plants — shoots, leaves, forbs, buds, and in winter more woody browse.
      Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      Mainly at dusk and dawn. It can also appear by day. Adjara camera traps
      recorded more photos in spring and summer than in autumn and winter. It is
      resident and does not migrate like many birds. Fawns are born in late
      spring.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous. It does not normally attack. Do not pick up a fawn.
      Control dogs. Drive carefully at night on forest roads.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      It is globally Least Concern. The Agency states it is not on Georgia’s
      Red List. It is listed as a hunting species; season and quota are set by
      official rules. Hunting in protected areas is prohibited unless a
      separate rule says otherwise. Poaching of forest ungulates remains a
      documented pressure in Georgia.
