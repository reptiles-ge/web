commonName: Mediterranean black widow
location: Eastern steppe and Kartli
description: >-
  The Mediterranean black widow. It is recorded in Georgia; a bite is medically
  significant.
overview: >-
  The Mediterranean black widow (Latrodectus tredecimguttatus) is a spider in the
  family Theridiidae. Members of this family usually spin irregular, tangled webs,
  not classic round orb webs. The current scientific name is Latrodectus
  tredecimguttatus. Georgian names include ყარაყურთი and შავი ქვრივი; English
  names include Mediterranean black widow and European black widow. The southern
  black widow (Latrodectus mactans) is a different species found in North America.
  In older literature, some Caucasus records appear under the name Latrodectus
  mactans or as one of its subspecies. Under modern classification, the species
  confirmed in Georgia is Latrodectus tredecimguttatus. Karakurt occurs in Georgia.
  It is widespread across the Caucasus, but that does not mean it is equally common
  in every region of the country. Female body length is about 7–15 mm; males are
  about 4–7 mm. Females are usually larger and easier to notice. The venom is
  neurotoxic. A bite can cause latrodectism, a set of symptoms that may require
  medical care. IUCN has not assessed the species, and it is not on Georgia’s 2014
  Red List.
habitat: >-
  Karakurt is mainly found in dry, open places: steppe, semi-desert shrubland,
  under stones and other objects, and in low vegetation. Species-level habitat
  descriptions include dunes, sandy and shingle areas, and dry low vegetation. This
  is a general species-level description, not a Georgian elevation belt measured
  separately. Confirmed Georgian records are known from Tbilisi, Gori, Aspindza,
  the Shiraki Valley, and the Eldari Valley. The atlas map therefore marks only
  regions where we have a specific record. For western Georgia, Adjara, Racha,
  Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, and other regions, this page does not cite a
  specific record. That does not prove absence; it only means we do not add
  unconfirmed distribution to the map. A broad or abundant Caucasus occurrence does
  not mean the species occurs in every region or every elevation belt.
diet: >-
  Karakurt mainly hunts insects and other arthropods. It catches prey in an
  irregular web, wraps it in silk, and then bites it. The diet of Georgian
  populations has not been studied separately, so this is the species’ general
  feeding pattern, not a measured Georgian menu. Karakurt should not be treated as
  a garden pest-control tool.
behavior: >-
  The female usually hides in a web retreat rather than sitting in the centre of
  the web. Its web is irregular and often built near the ground, among stones and
  other objects or in low vegetation. Karakurt does not seek people out. Bites are
  mostly accidental, for example when a hand, clothing, or a shoe touches the
  spider. In Europe, such cases have been recorded very rarely in recent decades.
  Do not handle the spider. Eggs are kept in silk sacs. One photo on this page
  shows two such sacs, but that photo was taken in Turkey and does not represent a
  Georgian population. A full Georgian seasonal activity calendar is not established
  from the data available on this page.
conservation: >-
  Karakurt has not been assessed by IUCN. It is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red
  List; among arachnids, that list names the fen raft spider (Dolomedes
  plantarius) as vulnerable (VU). The size and trend of the Georgian population
  have not been assessed from the data available on this page. Do not catch wild
  spiders or touch individuals found in nature.
interaction: >-
  The spider has venom for prey; it contains latrotoxins. A bite can cause
  latrodectism — severe, lasting pain and systemic symptoms. At species level
  Nentwig et al. write: pain is severe and long-lasting in about two-thirds of
  cases; in more than half it increases in the first hour and often radiates into
  the limbs or abdomen; sweating is common; nausea, raised temperature,
  neuromuscular signs, and hypertension occur in a smaller share. Pain usually
  lasts 1–2 days, other symptoms 1–4 days. This is not a viper bite and not an
  Argiope bite. Death is rare in modern European material; that does not make a
  bite “mild”. If bitten, call 112. Do not handle it, cut, suck, or burn the
  bite. This is educational text, not a medical protocol. An allergic reaction to
  any bite is possible.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Karakurt is a small, glossy black spider with a rounded, almost globose
    abdomen. The female often has red dorsal spots, although some individuals may
    be almost entirely black. The web is irregular and tangled, not a round orb.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level female body length is about 7–15 mm (Nentwig et al.); male
      about 4–7 mm. Legs are not included. There is no separate Georgian
      measurement series on this page.
    - >-
      The female has a black, glossy cephalothorax and black legs. The abdomen is
      rounded, almost globose. Red spots often appear on the back, usually arranged
      in three longitudinal rows. The scientific name tredecimguttatus means
      “thirteen-spotted”. The spots are not always bright red; they may be orange
      or yellowish. In some individuals they are reduced or absent, and the abdomen
      can look entirely black. A red mark may also be present on the underside, but
      it does not always resemble the hourglass mark known from the American black
      widow. The male is much smaller than the female and often has whitish spots.
    - >-
      In Georgia, adult females often have reduced or completely lost red spots.
      In that case, only a thin white margin may remain where the spots were. A
      completely black female therefore does not rule out karakurt.
    - >-
      Karakurt spins an irregular, tangled web, usually near the ground. Its web
      does not resemble the round Argiope orb with a white zigzag. An adult female
      often hides in a retreat rather than sitting in the centre of the web.
    - >-
      Another species of Latrodectus, Latrodectus pallidus, was confirmed in
      Georgia in 2024. It was recorded in Chachuna, Dedoplistskaro Municipality,
      at about 648 m. This is the first confirmed record of the species in the
      Caucasus. L. pallidus is easy to separate from karakurt: its abdomen is
      cream-coloured or whitish, not black. Latrodectus dahli is known in the
      Caucasus from Azerbaijan, but this source does not give a confirmed Georgian
      record. Karakurt can also be confused with the false black widow (Steatoda
      paykulliana), which also occurs in Georgia. It is usually brown and often
      has a red or yellow band at the front of the abdomen. That differs from
      karakurt’s glossy black, rounded abdomen and red spots. This atlas has a
      separate profile for the false black widow, while Latrodectus pallidus does
      not yet have one.
    - >-
      This atlas has separate profiles for the wasp spider (Argiope bruennichi)
      and the lobed argiope (Argiope lobata). Both spin orb webs and differ
      clearly from karakurt in appearance: they are larger, with banded or strongly
      lobed abdomens. Argiope and karakurt should not be confused.
facts:
  - >-
    The accepted name is Latrodectus tredecimguttatus (Rossi, 1790). The World
    Spider Catalog treats the species as accepted and as the type species of the
    genus. The original combination was Aranea 13-guttata. Range: Mediterranean,
    Ukraine, the Caucasus, Russia (Europe to southern Siberia), Kazakhstan, the
    Middle East, Iran, Central Asia, China.
  - >-
    Latrodectus mactans (Fabricius, 1775) is a separate species. WSC: probably
    native to North America only; introduced elsewhere. The sources on this page
    do not record L. mactans in Georgia’s fauna.
  - >-
    Georgian names: ყარაყურთი and შავი ქვრივი. English: Mediterranean black
    widow / European black widow. Russian: каракурт. Turkish: karakurt.
  - >-
    Otto 2022 lists 30 records in the Caucasus ecoregion. The Georgian map on
    this page is coloured only from the named points above. Seropian et al. 2024
    do not list L. tredecimguttatus among that paper’s specimens; the new record
    there is L. pallidus.
faq:
  - question: Is the Mediterranean black widow found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Karakurt (Latrodectus tredecimguttatus) is a confirmed species in
      Georgia. That does not mean it is equally distributed across every region of
      the country.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Named Georgian records are known from Tbilisi, Gori, Aspindza, the Shiraki
      Valley, and the Eldari Valley. It mainly occurs in dry, open places: steppe,
      under stones, and in low vegetation. For western Georgia and high mountain
      regions, this page does not cite specific records, so they are not marked on
      the map.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Karakurt is a small black spider with a rounded, almost globose abdomen. The
      back often has red spots, although some adult females may be entirely black.
      Its web is irregular and tangled. A cream-coloured or whitish abdomen points
      more toward Latrodectus pallidus. A yellow-and-black spider with an orb web
      is an Argiope, not a karakurt.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Female body length is about 7–15 mm, while males are about 4–7 mm. These
      measurements do not include the legs. A separate measurement series for
      Georgian populations is not available on this page.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Karakurt venom is medically significant, and a bite can cause severe
      pain and other systemic symptoms. Do not handle the spider. If bitten, call
      112 and seek medical care. A karakurt bite is a spider bite, and
      its effects differ from a viper bite.
  - question: Is this Latrodectus mactans?
    answer: >-
      No. Latrodectus mactans is a North American species, while the species
      confirmed in Georgia is Latrodectus tredecimguttatus. Older literature
      sometimes confused these names.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      Karakurt has not been assessed by IUCN and is not included in Georgia’s 2014
      Red List.
