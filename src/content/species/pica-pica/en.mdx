overview: >-
  The Eurasian magpie (Pica pica) is a medium-sized crow confirmed in Georgia
  as a year-round resident. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of
  Georgia codes it YR-R: a breeder present throughout the year. Seasonality is
  classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality is high (H), with
  photographic evidence. The usual Georgian name is კაჭკაჭი. Adults are about
  44–46 cm long at species level, roughly half of that length being the tail;
  the plumage is black and white with blue-green iridescence on the wings and
  tail. It is not a threat to people. IUCN lists the species as Least Concern.
  The 2024 checklist assigns the same category nationally.
habitat: >-
  This page does not shade municipalities on the map. The 2024 list confirms that magpies live in Georgia year-round, but it gives no records by region. We should not assume they occur equally across the country, including in dense mountain forests and treeless high-altitude plateaus. Magpies are listed for the Chachuna Managed Reserve (Dedoplistskaro Municipality). A regional list also names them alongside Tbilisi National Park, but that entry does not show how many live there. An older Georgian encyclopedia describes magpies in scrub, at forest edges and in riverside forests. It says they live in eastern Georgia, while in some parts of the west they mainly spend the winter. The nationwide 2024 list does not confirm this difference between east and west. In general, magpies use open or partly open places with trees, including farmland, gardens, parks, river floodplains and green spaces in settlements. They usually avoid dense forests and large treeless marshes. This page does not specify the elevations at which they occur in Georgia.
diet: >-
  This page does not include a separate study of what it eats in Georgia or how much of each food it consumes. Across its range, it eats whatever food is available, including animals without backbones, small animals with backbones, other birds’ eggs and chicks, dead animals, fruit, seeds and waste near people. It often feeds on the ground, moving in short hops or walking. During the nesting season, it may also take eggs or chicks from other birds’ nests. It is not considered food for people.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day and is often heard before it is seen. The usual call is
  a harsh, rapid metallic chatter; alarm calls are loud and repetitive. The
  clip on this page is xeno-canto XC707401 (Sonothèque ADVL / Corentin
  Rivière, Vains, Manche, France; CC0). Flight is a flap-and-glide with the
  long tail trailing; birds often fly fairly low. On the ground they walk and
  hop with the tail often cocked. Pairs hold territories; loose groups may
  gather where food is concentrated. The nest is typically a bulky stick
  structure, often with a roofed dome and side entrances, in a tall tree or,
  where trees are scarce, on a pylon or similar support. Species-level
  clutches are commonly about five to seven eggs, with incubation of roughly
  two and a half to three weeks; those figures are from the wider range, not
  a Georgian nest sample on this page. The 2024 checklist treats the species
  as a breeding resident in Georgia. Long-distance migration is not typical;
  movements, where they occur, are usually short. Old magpie nests may later
  be used by other birds; tawny owl (Strix aluco) sometimes uses them — see
  that page in this atlas. Do not handle wild birds.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Pica pica as Least Concern on the IUCN Red
  List. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global and national Red List
  category LC. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution No.
  190). European Red List of Birds supplementary data report a Georgian
  breeding estimate of about 9,700–97,200 pairs for 2013–2017 (partial
  method; short- and long-term trends listed as deficient), prepared for EBBA
  by SABUKO, Ilia State University and NGO Psovi. That is a wide interval, not
  a precise census. A current national trend series is not given on this page.
  At species level, local persecution and nest predation of other birds can
  cause conflict with people; that is not ranked here as a measured Georgian
  population driver. Do not catch wild birds without a permit.
interaction: >-
  It is not dangerous to people. It may take eggs or chicks of garden birds. Wild birds must not be caught.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a black-and-white, crow-like bird with a long tail whose feathers are different lengths. It has white patches on its shoulders and a white belly, while its wings and tail shimmer blue-green. Its call is a sharp chirp. No other bird that lives in Georgia year-round has this combination of features.
  traits:
    - >-
      Birds of this species are about 44–46 cm long, with the tail making up much of that length. Their wingspan is often about 52–60 cm, and they usually weigh about 180–270 g. This page does not provide separate measurements for birds measured in Georgia. Males and females look alike, though males are slightly larger on average.
    - >-
      Adults are strikingly black and white: the head, chest and back are black, with white patches on the shoulders and a white belly. In good light, the wings and long, stepped tail show a blue, green or purple sheen. The bill and legs are black. Young birds are duller, less glossy and have shorter tails until they replace all their feathers for the first time.
    - >-
      The grey crow (Corvus cornix) has a greyer body. It lacks the long black-and-white tail with feathers of different lengths and the white patches on the shoulders. Its call is deeper. The common jay (Garrulus glandarius) is mostly reddish-pink. It has a blue patch on its wings and a short tuft of white feathers, but no long black-and-white tail. The black crow and the raven are mostly black; unlike the magpie, they have no white belly or shoulders. The common raven (Corvus corax) is much larger, with a thick beak and a wedge-shaped tail. It has its own page in this atlas. The North American black-billed magpie (Pica hudsonia) and the eastern magpie (Pica serica) are not found in Georgia. This atlas does not yet have separate pages for the grey crow and the jay.
    - >-
      It is harmless to people. If you see a noisy black-and-white bird with a long tail in a park, at the edge of a field, or on a tree in a village in Georgia, it is usually this species.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Corvus pica. The 2024 Georgian checklist
    follows the OSME Region List for names: order Passeriformes, family
    Corvidae, genus Pica. Recent treatments split Maghreb, Asir, Oriental and
    black-rumped magpies as separate species; Georgian birds remain Pica pica.
  - >-
    Subspecies limits are debated. Many modern handbooks assign Caucasus birds
    to P. p. bactriana. The Georgian encyclopaedia names P. p. fennorum for
    Georgia. Field identification is to species; this page does not treat
    subspecies as a field split.
  - >-
    The usual Georgian name is კაჭკაჭი. English names in use are Eurasian
    Magpie (2024 checklist) and common magpie. Russian is сорока.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    confirmation plus the named literature and localities above, not a complete
    regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the Eurasian magpie found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident (YR-R).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In open or semi-open places with trees: farmland, parks, gardens,
      riparian belts and settlements. It is named for Chachuna Managed
      Reserve. This page does not map every region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Black-and-white plumage, long graduated iridescent tail, white scapular
      patches and belly, and a harsh chatter. Hooded crow and jay lack this
      combination.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 44–46 cm at species level, with a long tail making up much of that
      length. A separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this
      page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It eats animals without backbones, small animals with backbones, eggs and chicks, carrion, fruit, seeds and scraps. This page does not say how much each type of food contributes to its diet in Georgia.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      According to the 2024 list, it lives here year-round and breeds here.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means resident breeder. Nests are bulky
      stick structures, often domed, in trees or similar supports.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless to people. Globally and on the 2024 national list, it is classified as LC (Least Concern), meaning its risk of extinction is low. It is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Do not catch wild birds.
