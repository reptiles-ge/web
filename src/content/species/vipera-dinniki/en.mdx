commonName: Dinnik's viper
location: Greater Caucasus
description: >-
  Dinnik's viper (Vipera dinniki) is a small venomous snake that lives high in the Greater Caucasus mountains of Georgia. A bite needs medical attention.
overview: >-
  Dinnik’s viper (Vipera dinniki) is a small venomous snake that lives in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus. Adults are usually about 40–55 cm long. It does occur in Georgia, but not across the whole country. One form lives mainly above the upper edge of the forest. Globally, it is considered vulnerable, meaning it faces a risk of extinction. It is also listed in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. A 2026 list treats it as a possible separate species within the [Vipera kaznakovi](vipera-kaznakovi) group. It is still not fully clear where the line between Dinnik’s viper and the Caucasus viper lies. According to the same list, the subspecies Vipera dinniki tuniyevi is associated with wooded and mountainous areas of central Georgia. This snake poses a moderate risk to people, so do not go near it.
habitat: >-
  Dinnik’s viper lives high in the mountains of the Greater Caucasus, mainly at 1,500–2,800 m above sea level. Its main places are the upper edge of the forest, the mountain meadows above it, rocky slopes and the stones and gravel left behind by glaciers. It prefers slopes facing south and southeast. In the Caucasus it has sometimes been seen as high as 3,000 m, and in valleys it may come down a little lower. It has been seen in Svaneti, including the Enguri basin and the Ushguli–Shkhara area, as well as at Ardoti in Khevsureti, around Stepantsminda and in Truso. It has also been mentioned in Tusheti. The Colchian lowlands and the dry plains of eastern Georgia are not its usual habitat. Its range is broken up: many groups live cut off from one another.
diet: >-
  Adult Dinnik’s vipers mainly catch lizards and small mammals. Occasionally they may also take the chick of a ground-nesting bird. Young vipers more often eat small lizards and insects, including grasshoppers. Dinnik’s viper often hunts from ambush: it waits for its prey and subdues it with venom. All of this is known from observations in the western Caucasus.
behavior: |-
  In the high mountains, Dinnik’s viper is usually active from mid-April until the second half of September, and it is seen more by day than at other times. In hot weather it retreats into the shade, while on cloudy days it may stay out for a long time. It spends the winter in rock crevices, often in the same place where it lives in summer.

  It gives birth to live young. Its main mating time is late April and May. Records from the Caucasus show that the young are born in August–September: earlier on northern slopes and a little later on southern ones. A newborn is about 13 cm long and eats almost nothing before its first winter. Because conditions high in the mountains are harsh, females do not breed every year.

  Dinnik’s viper does not seek out people. Above all, it defends itself when it feels threatened. If you come across one, keep your distance and do not try to pick it up or catch it.
conservation: >-
  Dinnik’s viper is at risk of extinction. Globally, it is classed as Vulnerable. It is also listed as Vulnerable in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. It lives in a relatively small range that is highly fragmented. The loss of its habitat and the decline in its numbers are still considered a threat. The biggest threats are the deterioration of mountain pastures near the upper tree line and overgrazing by livestock. A 2026 list treats Dinnik’s viper as a possible separate species within the Vipera kaznakovi group. Studies of the full genetic material have not yet fully confirmed where the boundaries between species in this group lie.

  summary: >-
    Dinnik’s viper is a small venomous snake that lives high in the mountains. It often has a dark zigzag or an almost straight stripe along its back. Its pupils are vertical, and each scale has a raised line down the middle. It can be confused with the [Caucasus viper](vipera-kaznakovi) or the [European cat snake](telescopus-fallax), so pay attention to where and in what kind of place you saw it.
  traits:
    - >-
      Adults are usually about 40–55 cm long, with a relatively slender body. Females are generally larger than males, while males have relatively longer tails. The head is triangular, but narrower than that of the Caucasus viper and less clearly set off from the neck.
    - >-
      A dark zigzag usually runs along the back, though on some snakes it looks almost like a straight stripe. Body color varies from snake to snake: gray, silver, brownish or greenish gray, and some also have a yellow or orange tint. The belly varies too: dark with light patches, or light with dark speckles. Among snakes living in the west, entirely black ones are also common. In Georgia, such black snakes have been seen around Stepantsminda. The pupil looks like a vertical slit, and each scale on the back has a pronounced raised line down its center.
    - >-
      Young Vipera dinniki are usually gray-brown, while young Caucasus vipers are reddish. Even the snakes that later turn black are born patterned, like the rest. They darken with age and become completely black around their third year.
    - >-
      Georgia has other vipers too, but they differ in colour, size or where they live. [The Caucasus viper (Vipera kaznakovi)](vipera-kaznakovi) is often reddish orange and has a relatively broad head. Even entirely black snakes of this species often show a red or yellow tint around the mouth. This viper lives mainly lower down, in damp Colchian forests, often up to about 1,000 m above sea level. [Darevsky's viper (Vipera darevskii)](vipera-darevskii), by contrast, is found in the high mountains of the Lesser Caucasus, in the Javakheti–Erusheti area, mostly above 2,300 m. The [Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetinus)](macrovipera-lebetina) is a much larger snake. In Georgia, it lives mainly in the dry and semi-dry lowlands and foothills of the east. So besides colour, pay attention to where and at what altitude you saw the snake: you don't need to get close to work that out.
    - >-
      The [European cat snake (Telescopus fallax)](telescopus-fallax) is sometimes mistaken for a viper because it also has vertical pupils. Its other features give it away: its body is slimmer, its eyes are large, and the scales on its back are smooth. The [nose-horned viper (Vipera ammodytes transcaucasiana)](vipera-transcaucasiana) is easy to recognize by the horn-like growth on its nose. In Georgia, it is associated with areas of the Lesser Caucasus. Dinnik’s viper is venomous: its risk to humans is assessed as moderate, and a bite requires medical attention.

  - question: Is Dinnik's viper found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes, Dinnik’s viper is confirmed to live in Georgia, but not across the whole country. Its range is tied to the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus. A 2026 list treats it as a possible separate species. This is because it is still not fully clear how the snakes of the Vipera kaznakovi group should be divided into distinct species.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, Dinnik's viper lives high in the Greater Caucasus mountains: in mountain meadows, near the upper edge of the forest and in rocky places. It has been seen in Svaneti, Khevsureti, around Stepantsminda, in Truso and in Tusheti. It is not usually found in the lowlands of western Georgia or on the dry plains of the east.
  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      Yes, Dinnik’s viper is venomous. Its risk to people is rated as moderate, lower than that of the [Levantine viper](macrovipera-lebetina). A bite still needs medical attention, though. It does not chase people and usually defends itself when it feels threatened. So keep your distance, and do not try to pick it up or catch it.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
      Call 112 immediately. Reassure the person who was bitten, have them lie down, and try to keep them from moving as much as possible. Do not cut the bite, do not suck out the venom, and do not apply a tight tourniquet. They need to be taken to a hospital for treatment. Read more in the [snakebite guide](/snakes/gvelis-nakbeni).
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Dinnik’s viper is a small, relatively slender snake with a dark zigzag or almost straight stripe along its back. Its main colour is often grey or brownish. Its pupils are vertical, and its scales have a raised line down the middle. Unlike the [Caucasus viper](vipera-kaznakovi), it usually lacks a bright red-orange background colour. The [European cat snake](telescopus-fallax) also has vertical pupils, but it has a slimmer body, larger eyes and smooth scales on its back. Do not go closer to look at the pupils or scales: this snake is venomous.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      An adult Dinnik’s viper is usually about 40–55 cm long. Females are generally larger than males. Among Georgia’s vipers, Dinnik’s viper is relatively small and much shorter than the Levantine viper.
  - question: How does it differ from the Caucasus viper?
    answer: >-
      Dinnik’s viper and the Caucasus viper differ in elevation, colour and head shape. They mostly live at different heights: Dinnik’s viper in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus, at 1,500–2,800 m, and the [Caucasus viper](vipera-kaznakovi) lower down, in moist Colchic forests, up to 1,000 m. Their colours often differ too: Dinnik’s viper is grey or brownish, the Caucasus viper red-orange. The Caucasus viper also has a relatively broad head. The 2026 list has not yet fully settled how these two forms should be split into separate species.
  - question: Why is it a candidate species and Vulnerable?
    answer: >-
      In the 2026 list, Dinnik’s viper is treated as a possible separate species. This is because it has not yet been fully confirmed that it is genetically distinct from other vipers in the Vipera kaznakovi group. Globally, the species is considered vulnerable, which means it faces a risk of extinction. Its range covers less than 20,000 km² and is highly fragmented. It is also listed as vulnerable on Georgia’s Red List.
