description: >-
  A brown frog with a horizontal pupil. Widespread in Georgia; harmless to
  people.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian brown frog (Rana macrocnemis) is a brown frog of the family
  Ranidae, biologically confirmed in Georgia and harmless to people. It is a
  largely terrestrial Rana, not the marsh frog (Pelophylax ridibundus), which
  stays at open water. Boulenger described it in 1885; the type locality is
  Bursa, Türkiye. Georgian also uses კავკასიური მურა ბაყაყი; English names
  include Caucasian brown frog and long-legged wood frog. Tarkhnishvili et al.
  2026 confirm the species and name two neighbouring forms in Georgia — R. m.
  macrocnemis and R. m. camerani; the formal rank of camerani still needs
  genomic work and is not treated as a separate species here. COI barcodes are
  from Tabatskuri (camerani) and Bjolieti, Guria (macrocnemis); the checklist
  figure is Guria, Bjolieti. IUCN Least Concern.
habitat: >-
  It is widespread in Georgia and is not tied to a single region. Tarkhnishvili
  (1996) lists 42 Georgian localities of typical macrocnemis — including Sokhumi,
  Gagra, Batumi, Kobuleti, Lomis Mt. in the Borjomi district, Kazbegi,
  Lagodekhi, Svaneti, Turtle Lake near Tbilisi, the Nedzura valley, Omalo and
  the Telavi area — and 11 of typical camerani, mainly on the Javakheti plateau:
  Tabatskuri, Akhalkalaki, Paravani, Khanchali, Tsalka, Tskhratskaro. This
  atlas map marks every administrative region from that literature set; the 2026
  checklist rates distribution confidence as medium. Type localities of camerani
  are in Georgia: Lake Tabatskuri at about 2,500 m and Akhalkalaki. In the
  Nedzura canyon (Borjomi district) breeding sites sit at about 900–1,200 m.
  AmphibiaWeb places the species in forest, subalpine and alpine belts; in dry
  country it stays near permanent water, and adults usually remain within a few
  hundred metres of it. Photos on this page were taken on 1 June 2015 in
  Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park. CBG also lists cave records — a trogloxene,
  not a cave resident. Do not assume it on dry eastern plains away from water.
diet: >-
  This page has no Georgian stomach-content study. At species level adults are
  predators and take whatever invertebrates are available. In a Turkish series
  (Çiçek 2011, Uludağ) about 96% of prey items were insects, mainly beetles,
  flies and hymenopterans; that is a Turkish sample, not a Georgian diet. Tadpoles
  feed in water. Do not treat it as garden pest control.
behavior: >-
  It is more terrestrial than the marsh frog, but it returns to puddles, ponds
  or stream pools to breed. AmphibiaWeb records hibernation on land or in water,
  from about September–November to February–May depending on altitude — a
  species-level window, not a calendar for every Georgian site. Breeding in that
  source runs from February to late August, usually April–May; it is later at
  high elevation. Pairing is axillary amplexus (the male clasps behind the
  female’s forelimbs). Each female lays one clutch per season; AmphibiaWeb gives
  about 580–3,500 eggs in a single mucous clump. In the Borjomi Canyon the
  brown-frog guild, including this species, spawns once a year (Tarkhnishvili
  1993). In the Nedzura canyon eggs are laid in many temporary pools along the
  river; most froglets come from a few source sites, and some pools dry out
  (Tarkhnishvili and Gokhelashvili 1999). Metamorphosis is usually in June–July;
  tadpoles may overwinter in cold highland ponds. Sexual maturity is usually at
  2–3 years.
conservation: >-
  IUCN Red List Least Concern (IUCN SSC Amphibian Specialist Group 2023). CBG
  also lists LC on the Georgian Red List. Not on CITES. AmphibiaWeb treats it as
  one of the most common amphibians in the Caucasus; that does not mean every
  local population is secure. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 accept it as
  biologically confirmed; camerani is not a separate species here — formal rank
  still needs genomic work. Nikolskii’s Rana dalmatina / R. agilis records in
  Georgia are treated as confusion with this species and have not been
  confirmed later in the Caucasus. Do not collect wild amphibians.
interaction: >-
  Not venomous and not a medical threat to people. Do not handle it; the skin
  produces mucus, as in other amphibians.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium brown frog with a horizontal pupil, dorsolateral folds and a dark
    mask behind the eye. It is the only Rana in Georgia. The usual confusion is
    the marsh frog.
  traits:
    - >-
      The pupil is horizontal. A large dark temporal patch sits behind the eye —
      the usual mark of brown frogs. The back is olive, grey, brown, reddish or
      dark violet; the number of dark spots varies. A pale mid-dorsal stripe may
      be present or absent. The belly is usually unmarked, pinkish, orange-red
      or whitish. The hind legs are long (macrocnemis means a long shank); the
      heels overlap when the shins are set at right angles to the body. The
      inner metatarsal tubercle is oval and relatively small. AmphibiaWeb: males
      have internal vocal sacs and nuptial pads on the first finger. Adult
      length at species level is often about 4–8 cm: 40–79 mm in a Turkish
      series; breeding frogs in the Nedzura canyon exceed 50 mm before the
      second hibernation. This page has no separate Georgian size series.
    - >-
      Two neighbouring forms are described in Georgia. Forest macrocnemis is
      smoother-skinned, with a pale spot pattern, often no mid-dorsal stripe,
      and a small metatarsal tubercle. Plateau camerani is more rugose, with
      a stronger dorsal spot pattern and a contrasting mid-dorsal stripe. Where
      the forms meet, for example on the northern Trialeti slopes, intermediate
      animals occur. That is a field difference, not evidence for two species
      in this atlas.
    - >-
      The marsh frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) stays at water, is often green or
      olive and calls loudly; the brown frog is in forest or meadow away from
      the breeding season. The Caucasian parsley frog (Pelodytes caucasicus) is
      smaller, with a vertical pupil and an oblique cross on the back. The
      eastern spadefoot (Pelobates syriacus) is stocky, with a large digging
      spade, in the dry east. Toads are warty, with large glands behind the
      eyes. Tree frogs have toe pads and climb. The agile frog (Rana dalmatina)
      does not occur in Georgia — old records are treated as confusion with this
      species.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. A brown frog with a horizontal pupil and a mask behind
      the eye at a forest-road puddle or a mountain pond matches this species
      more than a marsh frog. Do not handle it.
    - >-
      Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 confirm the species and name two neighbouring
      forms in Georgia — R. m. macrocnemis and R. m. camerani; the formal rank
      of camerani still needs genomic work and is not treated as a separate species
      here. It is Georgia’s only Rana. COI barcodes are from Tabatskuri
      (camerani) and Bjolieti, Guria (macrocnemis). The agile frog (Rana
      dalmatina) does not occur in Georgia.

faq:
  - question: Is the Caucasian brown frog found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 list it as biologically confirmed. Barcodes
      are from Tabatskuri and Bjolieti (Guria).
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In forest, subalpine meadow or beside mountain ponds, close to water.
      Named localities include the Colchis coast and western forests,
      Borjomi-Kharagauli and the Nedzura canyon, the southern Greater Caucasus
      (Kazbegi, Svaneti, Lagodekhi, Omalo) and the Javakheti plateau
      (Tabatskuri, Paravani, Akhalkalaki). Do not assume it on dry plains away
      from water.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A brown or olive frog with a horizontal pupil, a dark mask behind the eye,
      dorsolateral folds and relatively long hind legs. It is not tied to open
      water like a marsh frog and does not have the parsley frog’s vertical
      pupil.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adults are often about 4–8 cm at species level. Breeding animals in the
      Nedzura canyon exceed 50 mm. This page has no separate Georgian size
      series.
  - question: Is camerani a separate species?
    answer: >-
      Not in this atlas. The checklist names two neighbouring subspecies;
      camerani rank may be revised, but the listed species remains R.
      macrocnemis.
  - question: How does it differ from the marsh frog?
    answer: >-
      This is Rana — a browner, more terrestrial frog with a mask behind the
      eye. The marsh frog (Pelophylax) stays at water and is often green.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: It is not venomous. Do not handle it.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      Globally Least Concern (2023). CBG also lists LC on the Georgian Red List.
      Do not collect wild amphibians.
