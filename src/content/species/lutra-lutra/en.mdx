commonName: Eurasian otter
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A semiaquatic mustelid with a long dark-brown body, a flat head, a thick
  tapering tail, and webbed feet. It is a resident native species in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The Eurasian otter (Lutra lutra) is a mammal in the weasel family
  (Mustelidae) and a resident native species in Georgia. IUCN lists Georgia in
  the native range. It has no venom. The main human risk is a bite if it is
  handled, not poison. Globally it is Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List. On
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List it is Vulnerable. At species level, head–body length
  is about 59–90 cm and the tail 35–47 cm.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it on the banks of rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and fish
  ponds where there is cover and prey. In Gorgadze’s 2012–2013 national survey,
  signs were found at 245 of 631 sites (39%). Sites were 90% freshwater, 8%
  lake/reservoir, and 2% sea coast. Positive rate was highest at lakes and
  reservoirs (75%), 36% in freshwater, and 18% of 11 coastal sites. Occurrence
  declined with altitude. Historical literature and the South Caucasus
  biodiversity map give about 2,800 m in Georgia; that figure is not a separate
  measured belt on this page. Globally IUCN gives 0–4,120 m. This page does not
  map administrative regions. Named Georgian localities include the Alazani in
  eastern Georgia, 2006–2007, Gorgadze spraint study (about 41.65°N, 46.12°E);
  the Bakhvistskali in Ozurgeti Municipality near Ukanava, 530–1,400 m, August–
  September 2022, 55 activity points, mostly tracks (Kerdikoshvili, a hydropower
  study, not a peer-reviewed paper); and Borjomi Gorge and the Kintrishi as
  recent sightings on the South Caucasus map. Sources disagree on Lagodekhi:
  Gorgadze’s protected-area baseline found no signs, while the later map lists a
  recent record. The same baseline also found no signs in Vashlovani and
  Borjomi-Kharagauli. Do not assume even density across Georgia.
diet: >-
  On the Alazani, Gorgadze analysed 791 spraints in 2006–2007. In the cold
  season (November–March) fish occurred in 50% of spraints and amphibians in
  30%; in the warm season (April–October) amphibians were 36% and fish 24%.
  Reptiles were about 12–13% in both seasons — only grass snake (Natrix natrix)
  and dice snake (Natrix tessellata). Amphibians were frogs only, mainly marsh
  frog (then Rana ridibunda; in this atlas Pelophylax ridibundus). Among fish,
  crucian carp and common carp dominated; most individuals were under 20 cm.
  Rodents (mice, muskrat), birds, insects, molluscs, and crayfish were minor.
  That is one Alazani stretch with fish ponds, not a country-wide menu. On the
  Bakhvistskali the same hydropower study describes a poor prey base; brown
  trout is named.
behavior: >-
  In freshwater it is often more active at night and at dusk than by day; it can
  still be seen in daylight where disturbance is low. It does not hibernate. The
  den is a holt: bank roots, a cavity, rocks, or a dug tunnel, sometimes with an
  underwater entrance. It marks with spraint on rocks, bridges, or logs; spraint
  often contains fish scales and bone. At species level, gestation is about
  61–74 days; litters are usually 2–3 young, rarely 1–5; the female raises them
  alone. A Georgian breeding calendar is not measured on this page. Tracks are
  five-toed and webbed; the 2022 Bakhvistskali work recorded mostly footprints.
  Photographs on this page are not from Georgia.
conservation: >-
  Loy and co-authors assess Lutra lutra as Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List
  (2022); the trend is decreasing. The previous global assessment (Roos et al.
  2015) used the same category. On Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government
  Resolution No. 190) the otter is VU, criterion B1(bI), for range decline. It
  is on CITES Appendix I. Hunting is prohibited because it is on the Red List.
  Gorgadze 2004 cites the old Red Data Book as critically endangered or nearly
  extinct; the 2012–2013 survey treats that as outdated. Yoxon and Yoxon
  summarise Gorgadze’s survey as not abundant but apparently stable, population
  >300 — that is a secondary summary, not a census on this page. The South
  Caucasus map gives national status VU and calls the population “small”.
  Documented pressures include loss of riparian forest, water pollution,
  reduced fish stocks, poaching, and conflict at fish farms; Gorgadze often saw
  traps at ponds during the survey.   Hydropower and river-bed works are named as habitat threats in the
  literature. In 2019 Radio Tavisupleba reported a killed individual on the
  Mtkvari bank in Tbilisi; that is a news report, not a field survey. Do not
  handle wild animals.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom. It can bite if handled. Do not feed it or pick it up.
  Conflict at fish ponds is real; the species is protected and killing or
  trapping it is illegal. Any wild mammal can theoretically carry rabies; in
  Georgian literature the main sources named are fox, jackal, wolf, and dog,
  not this species.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A semiaquatic dark-brown mustelid with a flat head, a thick tapering tail,
    and webbed feet. It is resident in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level, head–body length is about 60–90 cm in males and 59–70 cm
      in females; tail 35–47 cm; mass 5.5–11.4 kg in males and 3.4–7.6 kg in
      females (Hung and Law, citing Conroy). Males are usually larger. There is
      no separate measured Georgian series on this page. The head is broad and
      flat, the whiskers long, the ears small and rounded, the legs short, the
      feet webbed, and the tail thick at the base and cone-shaped.
    - >-
      The coat is dense and water-resistant. The back is dark brown, almost
      black when wet; the underside is paler. The throat may show a light
      patch. In water, often only the head and a low back line are visible.
    - >-
      There is no other otter in Georgia. American mink (Neogale vison / Mustela
      vison) is listed among South Caucasian mustelids by Gorgadze 2004; this
      atlas has no mink page, and a confirmed Georgian range is not mapped
      here. The otter is much larger, with a thick tapering tail. The least
      weasel is terrestrial, with head–body about 13–26 cm. The Caucasian
      badger is heavy, with a striped head, and does not swim like an otter. A
      dog in the water can be confused at a distance; an otter’s tail is thick
      at the base and tapers evenly.
    - >-
      Signs often appear before the animal: five-toed webbed tracks in bank
      mud; spraint on rocks with fish scales; slides into the water. It is not
      venomous. Do not handle it.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758 as Mustela lutra. The accepted name is Lutra
    lutra. Order Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Mustelidae, subfamily
    Lutrinae, genus Lutra. Synonyms include Viverra lutra and Lutra vulgaris.
  - >-
    Caucasian literature often uses the subspecies L. l. meridionalis Ognev,
    1931. Hung and Law give it from Georgia to the Persian Gulf. Gorgadze 2004
    noted that no DNA work had then been done in the Caucasus. Saveljev et al.
    2024 compare Caucasian and northern Eurasian samples from Russia and
    Armenia; a Georgian sample is not cited on this page. Field identification
    is to species.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual names are წავი and ჩვეულებრივი წავი. The Red List
    uses წავი. In English: Eurasian otter, also European otter and common
    otter.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as a native country. It is in Bukhnikashvili and
    Kandaurov’s 2002 annotated list. Gorgadze’s 2012–2013 survey is a
    country-level picture together with the named localities, not a full
    regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the Eurasian otter found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. IUCN lists it as native in Georgia. It is on national mammal lists
      and on the 2014 Red List.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      On river, lake, reservoir, and fish-pond banks. In 2012–2013, 39% of 631
      sites had signs. Named places include the Alazani, the Bakhvistskali
      (Ozurgeti Municipality), Borjomi Gorge, and the Kintrishi. Sources
      disagree on Lagodekhi. This page does not map every region.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A long dark-brown body, a flat head, a thick tapering tail, and webbed
      feet. Banks often show five-toed tracks and spraint with fish scales.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      At species level, head–body is about 59–90 cm, the tail 35–47 cm, and mass
      often 3–12 kg. There is no separate measured Georgian series on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      On the Alazani mainly fish and frogs, also water snakes. The menu can
      differ on other rivers.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      In freshwater often at night and at dusk. It is resident and does not
      migrate like many birds. It does not hibernate.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous. It can bite if handled. Do not pick it up.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      Globally it is Near Threatened. In Georgia it is Vulnerable on the Red
      List. It is on CITES Appendix I. Hunting is prohibited.
