overview: >-
  The Asian snake-eyed skink (Ablepharus pannonicus) is a tiny harmless skink
  with fused eyelids, known in Georgia only from a 1973 Vashlovani record. No
  later confirmation is available. It belongs to the skink family (Scincidae). Georgia’s
  2014 Red List names it აზიური შიშველთვალა and lists it as Critically
  Endangered (CR D2) because the national range is a point locality. The 2026
  Georgian checklist treats the species as biologically confirmed but has no
  barcoded specimen. Globally IUCN lists it as Least Concern; that does not
  replace the national CR status. The species ranges across western and Central
  Asia. Do not expect it across Georgia. It is harmless to people.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, this skink is known from only one place: Vashlovani Nature Reserve in eastern Georgia, in today's Kakheti. The record dates from 1973 and has not been confirmed since. No other region is named. This single record is the only link between the species and Kakheti. It does not mean the skink lives widely or permanently across Kakheti. Some descriptions of protected areas mention it as a Caucasian rarity of Vashlovani. They repeat the same old record, though, and do not report a new find in the wild. Elsewhere in its range, for example in Iran, it often lives in semi-desert and steppe, on mountain slopes, in leaf litter and on stony ground. This habitat has not been established for Georgia. If you see a small “snake-eyed” skink in Vashlovani today, that alone does not confirm the old record. Such a sighting needs to be properly documented.
diet: >-
  Elsewhere in its range, this small skink eats small beetles, grasshoppers and ants. In Georgia, too, it probably feeds on small invertebrates.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day but easy to miss: with its slender body and short legs, it often slips under fallen leaves or stones. It lays eggs: glands that form the eggshell have been described in its body. In western Iran, its ovaries are active in spring, while no signs of breeding are seen in autumn. There, it is thought to store fat before winter. The tail may break off. If you see one with a short or regrown tail, that does not change the identification. The snake-eyed lizard (Ophisops elegans) also has fused eyelids, but it is larger and runs fast on open ground. Schneider’s skink (Eumeces schneiderii) is much larger and has a visible ring around the eye, while the slow worm (Anguis colchica) has no legs at all. The two-streaked snake-eyed skink (Ablepharus bivittatus) is not on Georgia’s 2026 species checklist; it is known from Armenia, Azerbaijan and northern Iran.
conservation: >-
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190) lists the species as Critically
  Endangered (CR D2), based on a very restricted (point) range. That national
  status follows from a single, narrow locality. The IUCN Red List places the
  species globally as Least Concern because the Asian range is wide. A 2024
  Caucasian ecoregion review also uses LC and names south-east Georgia
  (Vashlovani). That does not cancel the 2014 Georgian CR D2 listing. The 2026
  checklist treats it as biologically confirmed; distribution confidence is
  medium; there is no barcode. If a Georgian population still exists, it is
  likely very small or local — an inference, not a new census. Do not handle
  it.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is harmless to people. Still, do not pick it up: this small skink has thin skin, is easily injured and may drop its tail. If you see one, photograph its back and side up close, note where it was and leave it where you found it. Confirming the species in Vashlovani again requires a scientific record.

  summary: >-
    The Asian snake-eyed skink is a very small lizard with a slender body, short legs and smooth scales. Its eyelids are fused and it cannot blink, so its eye looks like a snake's. In Georgia it is known only from a 1973 record in Vashlovani. The snake-eyed lizard (Ophisops elegans) also has fused eyelids, so watch the size and movement: the snake-eyed lizard is larger and runs fast.
  traits:
    - >-
      The eyelids are fused and cover the eye like a transparent spectacle. Because of this, the skink cannot blink, and its eye looks like a snake's. The genus name Ablepharus refers to exactly this lack of eyelids.
    - >-
      The body is slender and elongated, with smooth, shiny scales. The legs are short but have five toes, so this is not a legless lizard. The ear opening is very small. The back is often bronze-olive, sometimes with three dark lines running along its length. A dark band with light edges runs along the side, passing through the eye. The belly is greenish-white. These colours come from a general description of the species, not from an animal found in Georgia.
    - >-
      It is a small skink. Species accounts give its length as about 3.5 cm without the tail and about 9.5 cm with it. Skinks in this genus usually grow up to 6 cm without the tail. These figures come from general descriptions, not from an animal measured in Georgia.
    - >-
      The snake-eyed lizard (Ophisops elegans) occurs here and there in the south-east. It is a true lizard, not a skink: its scales are different, and it is larger and faster. Schneider’s skink (Eumeces schneiderii) belongs to the same family, but adults are several times larger and show a visible eyelid ring around the eye. The slow worm has no legs at all. If a lizard blinks, it is not this species.
    - >-
      In Georgia this skink is known from a single record: Vashlovani, in Kakheti, in 1973. Nobody has confirmed it there since. The 2026 Georgian checklist still treats the species as biologically confirmed, although it has no DNA-checked specimen. So don't look for it all over the country.
faq:
  - question: Does it occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The Asian snake-eyed skink is still on the 2026 checklist. Its place there rests on a 1973 record from Vashlovani Nature Reserve. No one has confirmed that record since, but the species has not been removed from the checklist because of this.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Vashlovani, Kakheti. It is the only place named for this skink, and no other region is mentioned. Even there, no one has confirmed seeing it today.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and is harmless to people. It is small and easily injured, so please do not pick it up.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      It is a very small lizard. Descriptions often give about 3.5 cm from the snout to the base of the tail. Skinks of this genus grow to about 6 cm, not counting the tail. The animal found in Georgia was never measured, so these figures come from general descriptions of the species.
  - question: How does it differ from the snake-eyed lizard?
    answer: >-
      You can't tell them apart by the eyelids: both have fused eyelids. The Asian snake-eyed skink (Ablepharus) is small, with smooth scales and short legs. The snake-eyed lizard (Ophisops), on the other hand, is not a skink at all. It is a true lizard, larger and faster, and lives on open ground. So watch the size, the legs and the way it moves, not the eye.
  - question: Why is it listed as CR on the Georgian Red List?
    answer: >-
      The 2014 list uses CR D2 because of a point range — one narrow locality
      in the country. Globally IUCN lists it as LC because the Asian range is
      wide.
  - question: Why is it called snake-eyed?
    answer: >-
      It is not a snake. Its eyelids are fused and transparent, so its eye resembles a snake's. In the Red List, the same species appears under the Georgian name “აზიური შიშველთვალა”.
