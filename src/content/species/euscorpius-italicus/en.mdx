overview: >-
  The Italian scorpion (Euscorpius italicus) is a true scorpion and belongs to
  the arachnid class (Arachnida). GBIF treats it as an accepted species in the
  family Euscorpiidae.

  Two independent source lines support its presence in Georgia: the Scorpion
  Files distribution summary lists Georgia, and the Georgian Biodiversity
  Database shows the species as native with several western Georgian localities.

  The database localities include Batumi, Kobuleti, Ortabatumi, Ozurgeti,
  Kutaisi, Zestafoni, Zugdidi, and Gagra. These records confirm the species in
  western Georgia, but they do not mean that the full Georgian range is already
  mapped.

  This page maps Adjara, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Imereti, Abkhazia,
  Shida Kartli, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, and Tbilisi for the species.

  According to the Scorpion Files, Euscorpius italicus is one of the larger
  members of the genus, with adults reaching about 50 mm. The same source
  describes it as a harmless, mildly venomous scorpion whose reported sting
  effects are mainly local.
habitat: >-
  The Scorpion Files links the Italian scorpion with warm habitats. It is common
  in ruins, buildings, wall crevices, and sometimes household furnishings; it is
  also reported under stones on grassy hills and in mountain forests in the
  eastern Mediterranean.

  The Georgian Biodiversity Database notes anthropogenic habitats for the
  Georgian record, which also points to houses, yards, walls, or other
  human-modified places.

  A Euscorpius-type scorpion found in a yard, wall crack, or near a building in
  Georgia may therefore be Euscorpius italicus, but habitat alone is not enough
  for a firm identification.
diet: >-
  The diet of the Italian scorpion in Georgia has not been studied separately in
  detail. Like other scorpions, it is predatory and hunts small arthropods.

  It uses strong pincers to seize prey and its sting and venom to subdue it.
  This is general scorpion biology, not the result of a separate Georgian diet
  study.
behavior: >-
  The species is secretive. By day it hides under stones, in wall cracks, in
  building crevices, and in other shelters; activity is more likely in the
  evening and at night.

  Do not pick it up by hand. If it must be moved from a house or yard, use a
  container and stiff card instead of direct contact, or ask a specialist.

  For exact identification, sharp photos from above and the side, plus the exact
  discovery location, are useful.

  This page treats those as database-level status fields, not as a standalone

interaction: >-
  The Italian scorpion is venomous in the biological sense that it uses venom to
  subdue prey. According to the Scorpion Files, available medical data mainly
  indicate local effects, and the species is assessed there as harmless to
  people.

  A sting can still be painful. Do not pick up the scorpion by hand, and do not
  let children or pets touch it. If pain is strong, a child is affected,
  allergic or other systemic symptoms develop, or the situation is unclear, call
  112 and seek medical care.
