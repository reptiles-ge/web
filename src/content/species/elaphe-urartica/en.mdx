commonName: Urartian ratsnake
location: Eastern Georgia
description: >-
  A harmless blotched ratsnake of dry eastern Georgia. Formerly treated as
  Elaphe sauromates; a candidate species in the 2026 Georgian checklist.
overview: >-
  The Urartian ratsnake (Elaphe urartica) is a large, non-venomous blotched
  ratsnake mainly tied to dry and semi-arid landscapes of eastern Georgia. It
  belongs to the family Colubridae. The species was split from Elaphe sauromates
  in 2019 and looks very similar to it. In the modern Georgian checklist, E.
  sauromates is not listed as a separate species for Georgia's fauna. The 2026
  checklist accepts E. urartica but treats it as a candidate species because the
  north-western Caspian transition zone has not yet been studied well enough.
  Total length is usually about 80–120 cm. It is not venomous to people.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, the Urartian ratsnake is mainly associated with dry and semi-arid
  eastern habitats — steppe, semi-desert, forest-steppe, open juniper woodland,
  rocky slopes, and dry forest edges. The clearest confirmed material is from
  Tbilisi and Kakheti, including the Dedoplistsqaro and Shiraki areas. In the
  2026 checklist, DNA-barcoded samples are also from Chankaani village in
  Dedoplistsqaro Municipality. This atlas map also marks Kvemo Kartli and
  Samtskhe-Javakheti, but a marked region does not mean a separate confirmed
  record at every marked place. The Alazani valley is within the published range,
  but this page does not cite a separate Lagodekhi record. Older literature
  describes the western range limit toward the Trialeti and Tsalka Plateau area,
  but this does not mean a broad, point-by-point confirmed distribution in
  western Georgia. Across the whole range, the species is described from about
  −25 m to 2,600 m above sea level. A complete separate elevation range for
  Georgia is not available.
diet: >-
  The diet of the Urartian ratsnake has not been studied separately in Georgia.
  Other Elaphe ratsnakes feed on rodents, small birds, nestlings, and eggs; they
  may also take lizards. This list should therefore not be treated as a directly
  confirmed diet for Georgia's population. It does not use venom — like other
  ratsnakes in the genus, it restrains prey with its body. Elaphe ratsnakes can
  also climb shrubs and trees, but this behaviour has not been separately studied
  for this species in Georgia.
behavior: >-
  It is active in the warm season, but its exact daily activity rhythm in Georgia
  has not been described separately. When threatened, it usually tries to flee.
  If caught, a large snake can bite strongly, so do not pick it up. The Urartian
  ratsnake lays eggs. The mating period and clutch size of Georgia's population
  have not been clarified in published data. The 4–16 egg figure given in older
  text is not supported by a reliable primary source for this species.
conservation: >-
  Elaphe urartica has not yet been assessed separately on the IUCN Red List. The
  2017 Least Concern assessment refers to E. sauromates and was prepared before
  the species split, so it cannot be transferred automatically to E. urartica.
  Georgia's 2014 Red List includes neither E. urartica nor E. sauromates. In the
  2026 Georgian checklist, E. urartica is treated as a candidate species because
  the north-western Caspian transition zone with E. sauromates has not yet been
  studied well enough. A separate complete assessment of the population status and
  specific threats for Georgia is not available.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large, non-venomous ratsnake with a round pupil, dark head, and large
    rounded blotches on the back. In Georgia, dry eastern habitat and the
    distinctive blotched pattern are especially helpful. Unlike the Levantine
    viper, the Urartian ratsnake has a round pupil, though you should not rely on
    pupil shape alone to identify a snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      Total length is usually about 80–120 cm and rarely exceeds 1.2 m. The head
      is large compared with the body and clearly set off from the neck. The
      snout profile often looks slightly hooked, and the pupil is round. There
      are 23–25 scale rows at midbody, of which 18–21 rows are keeled, meaning
      they have a small central ridge.
    - >-
      The body ground colour is yellowish or whitish, rarely bright yellow. The
      back has about 50–65 large rounded brown or black blotches, often with a
      pale edge. Toward the rear of the body, the blotches may become more
      transverse. The top of the head is dark, sometimes almost black, and a dark
      stripe runs from behind the eye toward the corner of the mouth. The belly
      is whitish or pale yellow and may have fine marbled speckling along the
      sides.
    - >-
      The steppe ratsnake (Elaphe dione) occurs in the same dry east, but
      usually has a finer pattern — often four lengthwise stripes and smaller
      transverse marks. Schmidt's racer (Dolichophis schmidti) is slimmer, and in
      adults the belly is often reddish. The Levantine viper (Macrovipera
      lebetina) is venomous, more heavily built, and has a vertical pupil. The
      smooth snake (Coronella austriaca) is much smaller and smooth-scaled.
    - >-
      Elaphe sauromates looks very similar to the Urartian ratsnake, so the two
      species are hard to separate by appearance alone. In the modern Georgian
      checklist, local populations belong to E. urartica, while E. sauromates
      occurs farther west. Geography is an important supporting clue for
      identification. Do not pick up a snake to count scales or inspect other
      close-range marks.
    - >-
      It has no venom. It may bite defensively, and the bite can be painful, but
      it is not a venomous bite. Because confusion with the Levantine viper is
      possible, observe an unknown large blotched snake from a safe distance and
      do not approach just to check the pupil or other marks.
facts:
  - >-
    Named for the ancient kingdom of Urartu (Armenian Highland, around Lake Van).
    Described in 2019 (Jablonski et al.); the type locality is Bitlis Province,
    Turkey — not Georgia.
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist Georgian name is “ურარტუს ხალებიანი მცურავი”; this atlas
    uses the shorter form ურარტუს მცურავი / Urartian ratsnake.
  - >-
    Georgian barcodes from Chankaani (CaBOL) match Dagestan Elaphe sequences,
    not Balkan E. quatuorlineata.
faq:
  - question: Is the Urartian ratsnake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The Urartian ratsnake is confirmed in Georgia and is mainly associated
      with the eastern part of the country. In the 2026 Georgian checklist the
      species is included, though its taxonomic status is treated as candidate.
      It is not distributed throughout Georgia.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Mainly in dry and semi-arid zones of eastern Georgia, especially Kakheti and
      the Tbilisi area. Confirmed material also comes from the Dedoplistsqaro and
      Shiraki areas. This atlas map also marks Kvemo Kartli and Samtskhe-Javakheti,
      but not every marked region represents a separately confirmed point record.
      The species is not tied to humid Colchic landscapes.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and is not considered a venomous snake for people. It
      may bite defensively, so do not pick it up. Because of possible confusion
      with the Levantine viper, observe an unknown large blotched snake from a
      safe distance and do not approach just to check pupil shape.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Typical features are a yellowish or whitish ground colour, about 50–65
      large rounded blotches on the back, a dark head, and a round pupil. Total
      length is usually about 80–120 cm. The steppe ratsnake has a finer pattern
      and often four lengthwise stripes. The Levantine viper has a vertical
      pupil, but do not rely on a single trait for identification.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      In measured material, total length is about 80–120 cm and usually does not
      exceed 1.2 m. Males have a relatively longer tail, while females have a
      higher average number of ventral scales. These differences are not enough
      for easy sexing in the field.
  - question: How does it differ from Elaphe sauromates?
    answer: >-
      Elaphe urartica and Elaphe sauromates look very similar, and they are hard
      to separate reliably by appearance alone. In the modern Georgian checklist,
      local populations belong to E. urartica, while E. sauromates occurs farther
      west. The 2026 checklist accepts the split but keeps E. urartica as a
      candidate species because the north-western Caspian transition zone is not
      yet sufficiently studied.
  - question: What does it eat, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      The Urartian ratsnake lays eggs. Its diet has not been studied separately
      in Georgia; other Elaphe ratsnakes feed on rodents, small birds, and eggs.
      The exact clutch size for Georgia's population is not confirmed.
