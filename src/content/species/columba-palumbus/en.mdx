commonName: Common woodpigeon
location: Year-round resident, migrant and winter visitor in Georgia
description: >-
  A large grey pigeon with a white neck patch and a broad white wing bar in
  flight. Confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident, a passage migrant, and
  a winter visitor on the 2024 national bird checklist.
overview: >-
  The common woodpigeon (Columba palumbus) is a large woodland pigeon (family
  Columbidae). The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia codes it
  YR-R; M; WV: a breeder present throughout the year, a bird of passage, and a
  winter visitor. Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence
  quality is high (H), with photographs and checklist evidence. The usual
  Georgian name is ქედანი; ტყის მტრედი is also used. Adults are about 38–45 cm
  long at species level. It is not a threat to people. IUCN lists the species as
  Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns the same category nationally.
habitat: >-
  Look for it in woodland, woodland edge, parks, gardens, floodplain trees, and
  farmland where trees meet open ground. At species level it feeds on the ground
  and in trees; nests are usually in trees. The 2024 checklist confirms
  country-level resident, migrant, and winter-visitor status but does not
  publish a regional gazetteer. This page does not colour a
  municipality-by-municipality map. Do not assume it occurs evenly across
  Georgia, including treeless alpine plateaus or bare high mountains. An NPLG
  encyclopaedia entry says it occurs almost throughout Georgia, especially in
  the east, that numbers rise in winter with birds from the north, and that it
  uses various woods and scrub, mostly floodplain forest. That is older
  country-level description, not a 2024 regional map. A separate national
  elevation band for Georgia is not published here. The European IUCN regional
  assessment gives an upper limit around 1,600 m at species level; that is not a
  Georgian series.
diet: >-
  There is no separate weighed Georgian diet study on this page. Across the
  range it takes mainly plant food: green leaves, buds, flowers, seeds, grain
  and berries; invertebrates are taken rarely. It often feeds on the ground and
  sometimes in trees. Nestlings are fed crop milk (a nutritious secretion from
  the crop of both parents). In Georgia it is a traditional game bird; hunting
  is regulated separately from the Red List.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. The song is a deep five-note coo, often rendered
  “ru-HOO hu-hu-hu”, with stress on the second note; it is often given from a
  treetop. Take-off is noisy, with clattering wings. In flight a broad white
  wing bar is obvious. The nest is a platform of twigs in a tree, sometimes
  quite high. Species-level clutches are usually two white eggs; incubation is
  about 17 days and both parents sit; young leave the nest after about three to
  four weeks, and more than one brood may be raised in a season — those figures
  are from the wider range, not a Georgian nest sample. The 2024 checklist
  treats it as a resident breeder in Georgia and also as a migrant and winter
  visitor. Northern and eastern populations are more migratory at species
  level; southern and central European birds are more resident or partial
  migrants. Exact arrival and departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated
  here.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Columba palumbus as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List; the global range and population are very large, and the European
  trend is increasing. The 2024 Georgian checklist assigns global and national
  category LC. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution
  No. 190). Checklist evidence includes hunter-collected material (CH), so
  regulated hunting is part of the documented Georgian picture. Published
  Environmental Supervision Department seasons allow Columba palumbus (ქედანი)
  from the fourth Saturday of August to 15 February, with a daily limit of 10
  birds — check the live DES notice for the hunting year. A pair count for
  Georgia is not given on this page. Do not disturb nests or take wild birds
  outside a lawful season and permit.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and is not a threat to people. Do not catch or keep wild
  birds without a permit. In Georgia it is a game species where hunting is
  lawful; follow current Georgian rules. Hunting in protected areas is
  forbidden unless the rules say otherwise.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Georgia’s largest pigeon: a white patch on each side of the neck, a broad
    white wing bar in flight, and a deep five-note coo. On the 2024 checklist it
    is a year-round resident, a migrant, and a winter visitor.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 38–45 cm at species level, wingspan often about 75–80 cm,
      mass often about 300–615 g. A separate measured series from Georgia is
      not given on this page. The body is bulky, with a full breast. Adults
      have a grey head and back, a pink wash on the breast, and a white patch
      on each side of the neck next to green–purple iridescent feathers. The
      bill is pink-orange with a pale tip and a white cere; the eye is pale
      yellow. A white edge shows on the folded wing and becomes a broad white
      bar in flight. The tail is grey with a dark tip.
    - >-
      Juveniles lack a clean white neck patch or show it weakly; the overall
      tone is duller. Sexes look similar in the field. The song is a deep,
      repeated five-note coo, not the three-note coo of collared dove. Birds
      often feed on the ground and flush with a noisy wing clap.
    - >-
      Rock dove (Columba livia) is a year-round resident in Georgia on the 2024
      checklist (YR-R). It is smaller, lacks the white neck patch, and does not
      show a broad white wing bar in flight; feral town birds are often
      piebald. Stock dove (Columba oenas), Georgian გულიო or გვიძინი, is listed
      as a breeder, migrant and winter visitor (BB; M; WV): more compact, with
      no white neck patch or white wing bar, and black spots on the wing.
      European turtle dove (Streptopelia turtur) is much smaller, with a
      chestnut-and-black scaly back and a striped neck patch — see that species
      page in this atlas. Eurasian collared dove (Streptopelia decaocto) is
      paler grey-buff with a single black half-collar on the nape. This atlas
      does not yet have separate pages for rock dove, stock dove, or collared
      dove.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. A large grey pigeon in Georgia with a white
      neck patch and a white wing bar in flight is this species, not a town
      rock dove.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the IOC
    World Bird List and the OSME Region List for names: order Columbiformes,
    family Columbidae, genus Columba. English checklist name is Common
    Woodpigeon.
  - >-
    Handbooks usually recognise C. p. palumbus (Europe east toward eastern
    Turkey and Iraq) and a paler eastern C. p. iranica (southern Transcaspia
    and Iran); the difference is clinal and weak in the field. Georgian birds
    sit near that boundary. Field identification is to species. A measured
    Georgian specimen series assigned to subspecies is not given on this page.
  - >-
    The usual Georgian name is ქედანი; ტყის მტრედი is also used. English names
    in use are Common Woodpigeon, common wood pigeon, and woodpigeon. Russian
    is вяхирь.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R; M; WV, data quality A, global and national Red
    List LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH; CH (photograph and checklist).
    That is country-level confirmation, not a regional map. Rock dove, stock
    dove, turtle dove, collared dove, and other pigeons are listed as separate
    species on the same checklist.
faq:
  - question: Is the common woodpigeon found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident, migrant, and winter visitor (YR-R; M; WV).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In woods, woodland edge, parks, gardens, and farmland with trees. This
      page has no municipality list and does not colour the regional map.
      Presence is confirmed at country level.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Adults have a white patch on the side of the neck and a broad white wing
      bar in flight. Rock dove and stock dove lack that combination. Turtle
      dove is much smaller with a scaly chestnut back.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 38–45 cm long at species level, with a wingspan often about 75–80
      cm. A separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly leaves, seeds, grain and berries; chicks are fed crop milk. Diet
      has not been studied separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year as a resident breeder; it is also a migrant and winter visitor
      on the 2024 checklist. Exact arrival and departure dates are not
      tabulated on this page.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R means resident breeder. Named nest sites inside Georgia are not
      listed on this page.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless to people. It is globally Least Concern and coded LC
      nationally on the 2024 checklist. It is not on the 2014 Georgian Red
      List. It is a game species in Georgia in the published season; do not
      hunt or catch birds outside the lawful rules.
