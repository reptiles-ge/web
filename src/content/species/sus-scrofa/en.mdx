description: >-
  Wild boar (Sus scrofa) in Georgia: identification marks, size, habitat, diet,
  behaviour, and when it can be physically dangerous to people.
overview: >-
  The wild boar (Sus scrofa) is a large even-toed mammal in the pig family
  (Suidae) and is resident in Georgia. The domestic pig is a domesticated form
  of the same species. At species level, head–body length is about 90–200 cm,
  shoulder height is 55–110 cm, and mass often ranges between about 44–320 kg.
  Size varies strongly by sex and population. It has no venom; the main risk
  for people is a charge or tusk wound. IUCN assesses the species globally as
  Least Concern, and it is not listed on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, wild boar occurs in deciduous and mixed forests, floodplains,
  scrub, ravines, and woodland edges where food and cover are available
  together. It sometimes enters agricultural land as well. The specific
  Georgian example used on this page is the Iori floodplain hunting farm. Its
  2020 management plan names oak woodland, poplar woodland, floodplain scrub,
  gullies, and ravines as suitable habitat for wild boar. In open fields,
  arable land, gardens, and marshes it is recorded more episodically. A
  Georgian secondary source names floodplain and mountain forests to about
  2,500 m, but that is not a separately measured complete elevation range for
  Georgia. This page does not present a full administrative-region map, and
  even density across the country should not be assumed.
diet: >-
  The wild boar is an opportunistic omnivore. Its food can include roots,
  tubers, herbaceous plants, acorns, beech mast, berries, insects, snails,
  carrion, and sometimes small vertebrates. It often enters agricultural land
  as well and can damage crops. The sources used on this page do not provide a
  separate quantitative diet study for the Georgian population, so the specific
  local diet should not be treated as precisely measured here.
behavior: >-
  Wild boar is mainly active at dusk and at night, and often rests during the
  day. Under strong hunting pressure or human disturbance, its activity can
  shift further toward night. Females move in groups with piglets, while adult
  males are often solitary outside the breeding season. Wild boar often wallows
  in mud and then rubs the body against a tree or another surface. At species
  level, gestation lasts about 112–130 days and often 5–7 piglets are born,
  though litter size can be larger or smaller. In the temperate zone, young
  are born mainly in spring. A precise Georgian breeding calendar has not been
  separately measured on this page. The pale longitudinal stripes of piglets
  fade over about 2–6 months. The track consists of two main hoof marks; on
  soft ground the smaller rear dew claws often print too. Rooted soil and
  wallowing sites are typical signs of its presence.
conservation: >-
  According to IUCN, the wild boar is globally Least Concern. It is not listed
  on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Local pressures can include habitat loss,
  hunting, persecution after agricultural damage, and hybridisation with
  domestic pigs. In Georgia it is listed as a hunting species, but the season
  and quota are set by current official rules and this page does not give
  specific current numbers. An older Georgian encyclopaedia entry states that
  the country’s number is below 2,000 individuals, but the source does not give
  enough information on date or counting method, so it should not be used as a
  modern population estimate. African swine fever was confirmed in Georgia in
  2007. The disease affects domestic and wild pigs, but it is not transmitted
  to people. The modern national population trend for Georgia’s wild boar is
  not separately assessed by the sources used on this page.
interaction: >-
  The wild boar is not venomous. The main danger to people is physical: the
  animal can charge or cut with its tusks, especially if cornered or if a sow
  is defending piglets. Do not approach it, do not feed it, and keep dogs back.
  African swine fever is not dangerous to people. Raw or undercooked wild-boar
  meat can be associated with a risk of trichinellosis, but the sources used on
  this page do not provide separate data for Georgia.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large even-toed ungulate with a short neck, a long snout, coarse
    bristles, and a relatively straight back line. Adult males often clearly
    show tusks directed outward and upward. It is a resident species in
    Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level, head–body length is about 90–200 cm, tail 15–40 cm,
      shoulder height 55–110 cm, and mass often ranges between about 44–320
      kg. Females are usually smaller than males. The sources used on this
      page do not provide a separate, broad measured series for Georgia’s
      population. A Georgian secondary source gives maximum values of about 2
      m body length, up to 130 cm at the shoulder, and 50–300 kg, but these are
      not measured Georgian samples on this page. The snout is elongated, the
      ears are erect, and the eyes are relatively small.
    - >-
      The body is covered with coarse bristles, with softer hair sometimes
      present underneath. Colour varies from brown to almost black and often
      becomes greyer with age. Male tusks are more developed and curve outward
      and upward. Female tusks are smaller and often less visible. Piglets have
      pale longitudinal stripes on a brown background — one of the most
      characteristic signs of young age.
    - >-
      No other wild suid occurs in Georgia. The domestic pig belongs to the
      same species as the wild boar and they can hybridise, so separating wild
      and escaped domestic forms in nature is not always easy. Roe deer
      (Capreolus capreolus) is much slimmer and longer-legged; males have
      antlers, not tusks. Brown bear (Ursus arctos) has a completely different
      build and leaves a plantigrade footprint instead of a hoof print.
    - >-
      The wild boar is not venomous. Its main defences are body strength and
      tusks. Take special care to avoid a sow with piglets, and do not try to
      handle or corner the animal.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758. The accepted name is Sus scrofa. Order
    Artiodactyla (even-toed ungulates), family Suidae, genus Sus. It is not a
    ruminant.
  - >-
    Subspecies counts are disputed (roughly 4 to 16 or more, depending on the
    author). The Georgian encyclopaedia assigns Georgia to Sus scrofa attila.
    The IUCN Wild Pig Specialist Group profile limits attila to the northern
    flank of the Caucasus and excludes Transcaucasia (Georgia, Armenia,
    Azerbaijan); Groves notes that pigs north and south of the Caucasus
    differ sharply and that Transcaucasian boars are not attila. Do not
    assign a Georgian individual to a subspecies in the field with
    confidence.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual name is გარეული ღორი; the male is often ტახი, the
    female ნეზვი, the young გოჭი. In English: wild boar, Eurasian wild pig,
    wild pig. The domestic pig is often written Sus scrofa domesticus or Sus
    domesticus — a domesticated form of the same species.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as native. It is in Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s
    2002 annotated mammal list. It is on the hunting-species list. That is
    country-level confirmation, not a regional map.

    answer: >-
      Yes. The wild boar is a native, resident species in Georgia. It is also
      included in the country’s mammal checklists and official hunting-species
      list.

    answer: >-

      often active at dusk and at night. This page does not present a complete

      farm, where the 2020 management plan also describes episodic summer
      occurrence.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      It is a large hoofed animal with coarse bristles, an elongated snout,
      and a relatively straight back line. Adult males often show clear tusks.
      Piglets have pale longitudinal stripes on the body. The domestic pig
      belongs to the same species. No other wild suid occurs in Georgia.

  - question: What does it eat?

    answer: >-
      It is mainly active at dusk and at night. In Georgia it is resident. It
      may move substantially at local scale depending on food, season, and
      disturbance, but it does not make regular seasonal migrations like many
      birds.
  - question: Is it heard?
