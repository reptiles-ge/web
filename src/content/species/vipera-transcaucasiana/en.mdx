commonName: Nose-horned viper
location: Lesser Caucasus
description: >-
  Venomous nose-horned viper of Georgia's dry, rocky Lesser Caucasus — a
  medically serious bite and a distinctive horn on its nose.
overview: |-
  The nose-horned viper (Vipera ammodytes) lives mainly in dry, rocky areas of the Lesser Caucasus in Georgia. The conspicuous horn on its nose is its main identifying feature. It is venomous and high risk; if bitten, call 112 immediately.
  Snakes living in Georgia and the South Caucasus belong to the subspecies V. a. transcaucasiana. Older scientific sources and some databases refer to this subspecies as a separate species, Vipera transcaucasiana.
  More specifically, it occurs around Meskheti, Trialeti and the upper reaches of the Mtkvari River. Humid Colchic forests and high mountain areas of the Greater Caucasus are not its usual habitat.
  Adult snakes of this species in the Caucasus are usually less than about 75 cm long.
habitat: |-
  The nose-horned viper is found mainly on dry, rocky slopes where plants grow sparsely. It may also be found in stony areas, among bushes, at forest edges, near stone walls and in vineyards. In Georgia, it also occurs in relatively dry, rocky and grassy places, especially in the east of the country.
  There are also records from Adjara, although its range there is not considered confirmed. Confirmed locations include Borjomi, Gomaro in Adigeni Municipality, Tashiskari and Gori. It was also photographed in Dzegvi in August 2026.
  According to older regional sources, in the Lesser Caucasus it ranges from the Tbilisi area toward the mountains of Adjara, in the direction of Shuakhevi. Those accounts also describe its range extending along the upper Mtkvari River toward Akhalkalaki. The exact boundary of its range in western Javakheti has not been fully confirmed.
  Unlike the Caucasian viper, it is not typical of the Black Sea coast or the damp forests of Colchis.
diet: |-
  Adult nose-horned vipers mainly eat lizards and small mammals. They rarely catch small birds. Young vipers hunt small lizards more often.
  They usually lie in wait for prey and use venom to subdue it when they bite.
behavior: |-
  The nose-horned viper is active during the warmer months. In cool spring and autumn weather, it often moves about during the day. In the summer heat, it is more active in the evening and early at night. It spends the winter in cracks in rocks and under stones.
  Its eggs develop inside the mother’s body, and the young are born alive. Mating takes place in spring, and the young are usually born in late summer or early autumn.
  If you encounter a snake, keep your distance and do not pick it up. It does not seek out people and usually bites in self-defense when it feels threatened.
conservation: |-
  In IUCN's 2009 global assessment, the Caucasian form was treated as the separate species Vipera transcaucasiana and listed as Near Threatened (NT).
  It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List, although it appeared in older Red Books of the USSR and Georgia. Under the Bern Convention, Vipera ammodytes receives special protection.
  The main threats are changes to the rocky and dry places where this snake lives, the expansion of agriculture, and people killing snakes. In Georgia, its populations have been documented relatively rarely and in scattered locations.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This is a stout-bodied viper. In Georgia, the main clues to identifying it are the clearly visible horn on its nose and the fact that it is found in dry, rocky areas of the Lesser Caucasus. Its pupils are vertical, and its scales have a raised line down the middle.
  traits:
    - >-
      Its main distinguishing feature is a clearly visible horn on its nose, covered with small scales. Other vipers in Georgia do not have a true horn like this on the nose. Its head is broad and clearly set apart from its neck. Adult snakes of the Caucasian form are usually no longer than 75 cm.
    -  |-
      The pupil looks like a vertical slit, and the scales on the back have a prominent raised ridge down the middle. Its color is often light gray, ash gray, or grayish white. The back usually has narrow crosswise bands or separate dark spots. The pattern varies, so it should not be used alone to identify the snake.
      There are usually no prominent dark spots on the horn or the top of the head. A faint V-shaped mark sometimes appears on the neck. The colored part of the eye is often golden or copper-colored.
    -  |-
      The cat snake (Telescopus fallax) has no horn on its nose. Its pupil is also vertical, but its body is slimmer, its eyes are large, and the scales on its back are smooth.
      The Caucasian viper also has no horn on its nose. It is found mainly in damp parts of Colchis and often has a brick-red or orange tint. Dinnik’s viper lives in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus and has no horn either. Darevsky’s viper is smaller and is found in the high mountains of Javakheti–Erusheti.
      The gyurza is much larger: adults are often about 1–1.5 metres long. It has no horn on its nose and has a blunt snout. In Georgia, it is found mainly in the dry lowlands and foothills of the southeast.
    -  |-
      A snake described from the middle Mtkvari had a slightly upturned but hornless snout and other features intermediate between the nose-horned and Caucasian vipers. Researchers consider it a possible hybrid, but appearance alone does not establish the ancestry of this particular snake.
      To identify a viper, do not pick it up or try to count its scales — observe it from a safe distance.
faq:
  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to humans?
    answer: |-
      This snake is venomous and poses a serious danger to people. Its bite can cause serious symptoms both at the bite site and throughout the body. A bite requires urgent medical care. It does not deliberately attack people and usually bites when it feels threatened. Do not approach or handle it.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
      Call 112 immediately. Help the person lie down, reassure them, and try to keep them from moving much. Do not cut the bite, suck out the venom, or tie a band tightly around a limb. Treatment must take place in a hospital.
  - question: Is the nose-horned viper found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. This snake is confirmed in Georgia. Snakes found here belong to the subspecies Vipera ammodytes transcaucasiana. The specimen used to describe this subspecies came from Borjomi. The species occurs only in certain regions and types of places.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: |-
      In Georgia, the nose-horned viper is found mainly in the dry, rocky parts of the Lesser Caucasus: in Meskheti, Trialeti and the valleys of the upper Mtkvari River. Older sources also mention the mountains of Adjara.
      There are also records from Adjara, although its range there is not considered confirmed. The humid coastal strip of Colchis is not a typical place for it to live.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: |-
      Among Georgia’s vipers, only the nose-horned viper has a true horn on its nose. Its pupils are vertical, and each scale on its back has a raised ridge down the middle. It is often gray, with narrow crosswise bands or dark spots on its back, but color alone is not reliable for identification.
      The cat snake (Telescopus fallax) has no horn on its nose. Its pupils are also vertical, but its body is slimmer, its eyes are large, and the scales on its back are smooth.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adult snakes of the Caucasian form are usually no longer than about 75 cm. The nose-horned viper is much shorter and smaller than the gyurza.
  - question: Is this the same as Vipera transcaucasiana?
    answer: |-
      Yes, these are the same snakes living in the Caucasus. The difference is whether they are treated as a separate species or a subspecies. The 2026 Georgian list uses the name Vipera ammodytes and treats the Caucasian group as its subspecies, V. a. transcaucasiana.
      IUCN's 2009 global assessment treated this form as the separate species Vipera transcaucasiana and listed it as Near Threatened (NT).
  - question: Why is it not on Georgia’s Red List if IUCN says Near Threatened?
    answer: |-
      The nose-horned viper is not included in Georgia’s 2014 state Red List. IUCN's 2009 global Near Threatened (NT) assessment applies to the Caucasian form, then treated as the separate species Vipera transcaucasiana.
      The nose-horned viper was included in older Red Books, but those entries do not mean it is included in Georgia’s current 2014 state Red List.
