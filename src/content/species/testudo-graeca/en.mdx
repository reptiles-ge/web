commonName: "Mediterranean Tortoise"
location: "Eastern Georgia (T. g. ibera); west — rare (T. g. nikolskii)"
description: >-
  The Mediterranean Tortoise (Testudo graeca) is Georgia’s only land tortoise. It lives mainly in dry, open places in the east of the country and is a vulnerable species.
overview: >-
  The Mediterranean Tortoise (Testudo graeca) is Georgia's only land tortoise. It is native to the country: it lives here naturally, and this is confirmed. In eastern Georgia its form T. g. ibera is widespread, while T. g. nikolskii, described from western Georgia, is considered rare. Females are usually larger than males. Among tortoises measured in eastern Georgia, males had shells averaging about 18 cm long and females about 21 cm. Georgia's 2014 Red List assesses it as a vulnerable species. It is also considered vulnerable worldwide, though that global assessment is old.
habitat: >-
  The Mediterranean Tortoise lives on land, in dry, open places: steppe, shrubland, semi-desert and the edges of open woodland. It does not live in water. In Georgia it is especially widespread in the east. It has been seen in Gori, Tbilisi, Bolnisi and Vashlovani National Park. In the map's records-by-region table, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Tbilisi and Shida Kartli have enough records. In western Georgia it is much rarer: the map has only single records from Imereti, Adjara, Abkhazia and Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti. In Georgia it has been recorded up to about 800–1000 m, though this is not a strict upper limit.
diet: >-
  The Mediterranean Tortoise feeds mainly on plants: grasses, other non-woody plants and flowers. In other places where it lives, it has occasionally been seen eating animal food. Its diet in Georgia has not been fully studied separately. Do not feed wild tortoises.
behavior: >-
  The Mediterranean Tortoise lives entirely on land. In warm weather it is active by day, but in strong heat it often hides at the base of shrubs, in burrows or under other cover. In neighbouring parts of the Caucasus, it stays active from roughly spring to autumn; a full seasonal calendar for Georgia has not been described. The species is known to lay several eggs at a time, but its breeding in Georgia has not been fully described.
conservation: >-
  The Mediterranean Tortoise is a native, confirmed species in Georgia, and the country's 2014 Red List assesses it as Vulnerable. Globally it is also considered Vulnerable, though that global assessment is regarded as outdated. It is also listed in CITES Appendix II, so international trade in wild tortoises is controlled. Its main threats include habitat loss, fires, changes linked to farming and the collection of tortoises from the wild. So do not take a wild tortoise home.
interaction: |-
  The Mediterranean tortoise (Testudo graeca) has no venom and is not dangerous to people. It is Georgia's only land tortoise and is most often found in dry, shrubby and grassy places. It lives mainly in the east of the country, while the form described from western Georgia is considered rare.

  When disturbed, it usually pulls back into its shell, but if picked up it may bite in self-defence. If a tortoise bites you, wash the bitten area with soap and clean water. Seek medical help if there is heavy bleeding, a deep wound or signs of infection.

  In Georgia, the Mediterranean tortoise is a vulnerable species. It is threatened by the loss of the places where it lives, changes linked to farming and the collection of tortoises from the wild. That is why you should not take a wild tortoise home or move it somewhere else.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Georgia's only land tortoise. It has a high, domed shell and thick, pillar-like legs. These two features will help you tell it apart from water turtles.
  traits:
    - >-
      The shell is high and domed, while water turtles have flatter shells. Colour ranges from yellowish brown to dark brown, often with dark marks. The shell pattern varies greatly from one tortoise to the next. So you cannot reliably tell the subspecies by colour and pattern alone.
    - >-
      The legs are thick and sturdy, like little columns. Unlike water turtles, it has no swimming webs. The front feet have five claws and the hind feet have four. The head is rounded. It has no teeth, and the mouth ends in a horny beak.
    - >-
      Each hind thigh usually has one small, cone-shaped horny spur. Georgia has no other land tortoise. So to tell it from water turtles, look for two simple signs: a high shell and thick legs without swimming webs.
    - >-
      Females are usually larger than males. Among tortoises measured in eastern Georgia, average shell length was 17.9 cm in males and 21 cm in females. Males have a longer tail, and in many males the underside of the shell is slightly concave. Young tortoises are smaller, and many of them have a sharper shell pattern.
    - >-
      The European Pond Turtle (Emys orbicularis), the Caspian Turtle (Mauremys caspica) and the introduced Pond Slider (Trachemys scripta) live by fresh water. They differ from the Mediterranean Tortoise in their flatter shells and feet better suited to swimming. The well-known form of the Pond Slider, T. s. elegans, has a broad red stripe behind the ear. Testudo hermanni has not been confirmed in Georgia.
    - >-
      Two forms of the Mediterranean Tortoise are named for Georgia: T. g. ibera from the east and the rare T. g. nikolskii from the west. Opinions differ on whether T. g. nikolskii is a separate subspecies, and its genetic distinctness has not been finally confirmed. So you cannot reliably tell the subspecies of a tortoise seen in the wild by its appearance alone.
