commonName: Levantine viper
location: Eastern Georgia
description: >-
  The Levantine viper is Georgia's largest venomous viper and lives in dry places in the east. Its bite is dangerous, and you cannot identify it by the spots on its back alone.
overview: |-
  The Levantine viper, or gyurza (Macrovipera lebetinus), is Georgia's largest [venomous viper](/venomous-snakes) and the only one that lays eggs. It lives mainly in dry and semi-dry parts of eastern Georgia, from Shiraki and Gareji to the foothills of Kvemo Kartli. In recent decades it has reached the Alazani Valley and Tbilisi. Its bite is among the most dangerous of any snake in Georgia. From a distance, its spotted back may make it look like an Urartian ratsnake or a spotted whip snake. Spots or size alone are not enough to [reliably identify it](/snakes/shxamiani-gvelis-amocnoba), so do not go near it. Some sources call it Macrovipera lebetina or Vipera lebetina.
habitat: |-
  The Levantine viper is found in dry, stony and rocky places. In Georgia, it lives in dry areas along the lower Iori, Mtkvari and Alazani rivers, in Shiraki and Gareji, and in the dry foothills of [Kvemo Kartli](/regions/kvemo-kartli). It is rare around [Tbilisi](/regions/tbilisi) and Rustavi. It occurs in [Kakheti](/regions/kakheti), Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. Confirmed locations include Kvareli, the area around Nekresi Monastery, the Alazani Valley and the Colorful Desert.
  As of 2026, the Levantine viper had spread over recent decades from Georgia’s far southeast to the Alazani Valley and Tbilisi. In Tbilisi it has been seen only in isolated cases: in the Botanical Garden, Tabori, Nadzaladevi and Saburtalo. Toward Rustavi, it has been seen in Rustavi, Gardabani and near the Jandari Reservoir, mostly in dry areas of Kvemo Kartli along the Mtkvari. This means it was seen there in a particular place and year, not that it lives in every part of the city or is often found there.
  The Levantine viper shelters in rock crevices, under stones and in rodent burrows, and sometimes gets into structures. In intense summer heat, it may appear near water. In Georgia, it is found up to about 1,500 m. Its natural presence in western Georgia has not been confirmed. It does not usually live in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus or in the Colchic forest.
diet: >-
  Rodents are the main prey of adult Levantine vipers. Besides rodents, they also catch small birds, lizards and other small animals with backbones. They usually hunt by lying in wait. Now and then they climb bushes or trees as well.
behavior: |-
  In the South Caucasus, the Levantine viper is usually active from April to the end of October, but it may appear as early as late March. When you may come across it depends on the weather and the daytime temperature. In spring and autumn it often moves about during the day, while in the summer heat it is more active at dusk and at night. In very hot weather it is also more likely to be seen near water. It spends the winter in rock crevices or burrows.
  Among Georgia's vipers, only the Levantine viper lays eggs. It mostly mates in April–May, and in summer it lays about 15–20 eggs. After 35–45 days, young vipers 23–25 cm long hatch from the eggs.
  The Levantine viper does not seek out people. It usually defends itself when it feels threatened. If you come across one, keep a safe distance and do not try to catch it.
conservation: |-
  A 2021 assessment found that the Levantine viper faces a low risk of extinction worldwide. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List, although older Georgian encyclopedias sometimes link it to the Red Book. The vipers on that list include [Dinnik’s viper](vipera-dinniki) and the [Caucasian viper](vipera-kaznakovi). The Levantine viper is threatened by changes to the places it lives, expanding settlements, overgrazing by livestock and deliberate killing. In dry areas it hunts rodents and is an important part of the local food chain.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The Levantine viper is a thick-bodied viper with a broad, triangular head. Its pupils are vertically elongated, and a raised ridge runs down the middle of each scale. If only its spotted back is visible from a distance, it may resemble a Urartu racer or a multicolored racer. So don't rely on the spots alone.
  traits:
    - >-
      The Levantine viper has a thick, heavy body, and its broad, triangular head stands out clearly from the neck. The snout is blunt, with no horn. It is usually 70–90 cm long, though large Levantine vipers can reach 1.5 meters. Reports of Levantine vipers longer than 2 meters are disputed.
    - >-
      The pupil looks like a vertical slit. Each scale on the back has a sharply raised line down the middle, so the back looks rough. The scales on the head, including those above the eyes, are mostly small.
    - >-
      It may be gray, sand-colored or brownish. Its back often has dark spots or bands running across it, which sometimes form a zigzag. The belly is lighter and often dotted with small dark spots. Some individuals are fairly plain in color.
    - >-
      The [Urartu racer](elaphe-urartica) has a thick body and large, rounded dark patches on its back. The dark-spotted back of the [variegated racer](hemorrhois-ravergieri) can also look like a Levantine viper's, especially when the snake's head is out of view. The difference is in the eyes: both racers have round pupils, while a Levantine viper's are vertical, like a cat's. But if you are moving closer to see the pupils, you are already too close.
    - >-
      The [steppe viper](vipera-renardi) also lives in eastern Georgia, but it is much smaller than the Levantine viper: usually about 40–60 cm long. The [nose-horned viper](vipera-transcaucasiana) has a horn on its nose, so you can recognize it easily, and it lives in more mountainous areas. [Dinnik’s viper](vipera-dinniki) lives in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus and is not found where the Levantine viper usually lives.
faq:
  - question: Is the Levantine viper found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. As of 2026, the Levantine viper has been confirmed in Georgia. It lives mainly in the dry and semi-dry areas of eastern Georgia. It is not found throughout the country.
  - question: Are “gyurza”, “giurza” and გიუზა the same snake?
    answer: >-
      Yes, it is the same snake. “Gyuza” is a popular folk form of the same name. In Georgian, Macrovipera lebetinus is called “giurza”; in English it is the Levantine viper. So if someone mentions a “gyuza”, they mean the giurza.
  - question: Does the Levantine viper occur in Tbilisi or Rustavi?
    answer: >-
      Yes, but rarely. It is mostly found in dry, open or rocky places. In Tbilisi, the Levantine viper has been seen in the Botanical Garden, on Tabor, and in Nadzaladevi and Saburtalo, and toward Rustavi in Rustavi, Gardabani and near the Jandari Reservoir. This means it was seen in these places in specific years, not that it is often found in every part of the city.
  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      Yes, the Levantine viper is a venomous snake. Among Georgia's snakes, its bite is considered one of the most serious threats to human health. It is a large snake and can inject a lot of venom in a single bite. Its venom may cause bleeding, disrupt blood clotting and damage the area around the bite. It does not usually attack people. It bites when it feels threatened: when someone comes too close, steps on it or tries to catch it. If you are bitten, call 112 immediately.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
      If a Levantine viper bites someone, call 112 right away. Have the person lie down, reassure them and try to keep them as still as possible. Do not cut the bite, suck out the venom or tie anything tightly around the bitten area. The person needs treatment in a hospital. If medicine against the venom is needed, only a doctor should choose and give it.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      The Levantine viper is usually 70–90 cm long, and large ones can reach 1.5 metres. It is the [largest viper in Georgia](/snakes/didi-gvelebi). Reports of Levantine vipers longer than 2 metres, however, are disputed.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Watch the snake from a safe distance and leave it where it is. Do not try to catch or kill it. If you give it room and do not disturb it, the Levantine viper will usually move away from you on its own.
