description: >-
  The Caucasian Brown Frog is the only Rana frog in Georgia and is widespread there. It is brown, with a horizontal pupil, and harmless to people.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian Brown Frog (Rana macrocnemis) is one of the most common amphibians in the Caucasus and the only Rana frog in Georgia. It is brown and harmless to people. The Marsh Frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) stays by the water, while this frog spends more of its time on land. Georgia's 2026 species checklist confirms that this frog lives in Georgia and names two neighbouring forms: R. m. macrocnemis and R. m. camerani. The camerani form is not counted as a separate species, because its status still needs to be clarified. The DNA-studied samples come from Tabatskuri (camerani) and from Bjoleti in Guria (macrocnemis). Worldwide, it is not under threat. This member of the family Ranidae was first described in 1885, from a frog found in the Turkish city of Bursa. In Georgian it is also called კავკასიური მურა ბაყაყი, and in English Caucasian brown frog or long-legged wood frog.
habitat: |-
  It is widespread in Georgia and is not tied to a single region. It lives in forests and, above them, in high-mountain meadows too. In dry areas it tends to stay near permanent water: adults usually go no more than a few hundred metres from it. It has also been seen in caves, but it only enters them from time to time and does not live there permanently.

  According to 1996 data, typical macrocnemis was known from 42 places in Georgia. They include Sokhumi, Gagra, Batumi, Kobuleti, Lomismta in the Borjomi district, Kazbegi, Lagodekhi, Svaneti, Turtle Lake in Tbilisi, the Nedzura valley, Omalo and the Telavi area. Typical camerani was known from 11 places, mainly on the Javakheti Plateau: Tabatskuri, Akhalkalaki, Paravani, Khanchali, Tsalka and Tskhratskaro. The atlas map marks regions from these records. The 2026 checklist rates knowledge of this frog's distribution as moderately reliable.

  camerani was first described from Georgia itself: from Lake Tabatskuri, which lies at about 2,500 m, and from Akhalkalaki. In the Nedzura valley (Borjomi district) the frogs breed at about 900–1,200 m. On 1 June 2015 it was also photographed in Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park. Do not expect this frog on the dry eastern plains, away from water.
diet: >-
  The adult Caucasian Brown Frog is a predator and eats whatever invertebrates it can find nearby. In frogs studied at Uludağ, Turkey, in 2011, about 96% of prey were insects. They mainly ate beetles, flies and insects related to bees and wasps. These figures describe frogs in Turkey, not in Georgia. Tadpoles feed in the water. Do not count on this frog to control garden pests.
behavior: |-
  It is more active on land than the Marsh Frog, but it returns to water to breed: to puddles, ponds or stream pools. It may spend the winter either on land or in water. The timing of hibernation depends on altitude: from about September–November to February–May. These dates apply to the species as a whole and are not an exact calendar for every part of Georgia. Across the species, breeding runs from February to late August, with April and May as the main months. At high elevations it breeds later.

  During mating, the male grips the female in her armpits, behind her front legs. Each female lays eggs once a season: about 580–3,500 eggs in a single jelly-like clump. Observations in the Borjomi Gorge in 1993 showed that brown frogs, this species included, spawn once a year. In the Nedzura Gorge, a 1999 study found that it lays its eggs in many temporary pools along the river. Most young frogs come from a few of these sites, and some pools dry out. Most tadpoles turn into frogs in June–July. In cold highland ponds, though, a tadpole may spend the winter as a tadpole. It usually reaches breeding age at 2–3 years.
conservation: |-
  The Caucasian Brown Frog is one of the most common amphibians in the Caucasus. Globally it is not under threat: the 2023 assessment rates it as “Least Concern”. It has the same rating for the Georgian Red List. It is not listed by CITES, the convention on international trade in wild species. Still, being common does not mean that it is safe everywhere it lives. Do not catch wild amphibians.

  Georgia's 2026 species checklist confirms that this frog lives in Georgia. The camerani form is not treated as a separate species: its status still needs to be settled by detailed DNA study. Older records of Rana dalmatina / R. agilis from Georgia are considered confusion with this species and have not been confirmed in the Caucasus since.
interaction: >-
  The Caucasian Brown Frog (Rana macrocnemis) is not venomous and poses no danger to people. When someone approaches, it usually tries to flee or hide. It is a confirmed species in Georgia, living in forests and mountains near water and damp places. It is best not to pick it up: amphibian skin is sensitive, and various substances may pass onto it from human hands. If you do touch it, wash your hands well with soap and water, and do not touch your eyes or mouth with unwashed hands. Do not take the frog home or move it somewhere else. The best way to protect a wild amphibian is to leave it in peace in its natural surroundings.

  summary: >-
    The Caucasian Brown Frog is a medium-sized brown frog with a horizontal pupil and a dark "mask" behind the eye. Folds of skin run along the sides of its back. It is the only frog of the genus Rana in Georgia, and people most often mistake it for the Marsh Frog.
  traits:
    -  |-
      The pupil is horizontal, and behind the eye sits a large dark patch, a kind of “mask”. This patch is the usual mark of brown frogs. The back is olive, grey, brownish, reddish or dark violet, and the number of dark spots varies from frog to frog. A pale stripe may run down the middle of the back, though not every frog has one. The belly is usually unmarked: brownish-pink, orange-red or whitish.

      The hind legs are long, as the name suggests: macrocnemis means “long shank”. When the shins are held at right angles to the body, the heels overlap. On the inner side of the hind foot there is an oval, relatively small bump. Males have internal vocal sacs and mating pads on the first finger. Across the species as a whole, adults are often about 4–8 cm long. Frogs measured in Turkey were 40–79 mm long. In the Nedzura canyon, frogs of breeding age exceed 50 mm before their second hibernation.
    - >-
      Two neighbouring forms of this frog are described in Georgia, and they look different. The forest form, macrocnemis, has smoother skin and pale spots. The stripe down the middle of its back is often missing, and the bump on the sole of its hind foot is small. The plateau form, camerani, has rougher skin: its back spots are bold, and the middle stripe stands out clearly. Where the two forms meet, for example on the northern slopes of Trialeti, frogs with in-between features occur. These differences help you tell the forms apart in the field, but they do not mean the two are separate species.
    - >-
      The Marsh Frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) stays closer to water, is often green or olive and calls loudly. The Caucasian Brown Frog, outside the breeding season, is found in forest or meadow. The Caucasian Parsley Frog (Pelodytes caucasicus) is small, with a vertical pupil and an oblique cross on its back. The Eastern Spadefoot (Pelobates syriacus) is stocky, with a large “spade”, and lives in the dry east of Georgia. Toads have warty skin and large glands behind the ears. Tree frogs have discs on their toes and climb trees. The agile frog (Rana dalmatina) does not occur in Georgia: old records of it are considered confusion with the Caucasian Brown Frog.
    - >-
      If you spot a brown frog by a forest-road puddle or a mountain pond, check its pupil and the area behind the eye. A horizontal pupil and a dark “mask” behind the eye fit the Caucasian Brown Frog better than the Marsh Frog. This frog is not venomous, but still do not pick it up.
    - >-
      The Caucasian Brown Frog is the only frog of the genus Rana in Georgia. The agile frog (Rana dalmatina) does not occur in Georgia. Georgia's 2026 checklist confirms the species and names two neighbouring forms in the country: R. m. macrocnemis and R. m. camerani. The exact status of camerani still needs further research, so it is not treated as a separate species. The specimens studied by DNA come from Tabatskuri (camerani) and from Bjoleti in Guria (macrocnemis).

  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It lives in forests, on meadows above the tree line and by mountain ponds, close to water. It is known from the Colchis coast, the western forests, Borjomi-Kharagauli and the Nedzura gorge. On the southern slope of the Greater Caucasus it occurs in Kazbegi, Svaneti, Lagodekhi and Omalo, and on the Javakheti plateau in Tabatskuri, Paravani and Akhalkalaki. Do not expect this frog on dry plains away from water.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      The Caucasian Brown Frog is brown or olive, with a horizontal pupil and a dark “mask” behind the eye. Folds of skin run along the sides of its back, and its hind legs are relatively long. Unlike the Marsh Frog, it is not tied to water. It differs from the Caucasian Parsley Frog by its pupil: the parsley frog’s pupil is vertical. So when you see a brown frog, look first at the pupil and the area behind the eye.
