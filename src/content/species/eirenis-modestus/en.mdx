commonName: "Ring-headed Dwarf Snake"
location: "Eastern and central Georgia"
description: >-
  The Ring-headed Dwarf Snake is a small non-venomous snake, harmless to people. It lives on dry, stony ground in eastern and central Georgia and has been found in Tbilisi.
overview: >-
  The ring-headed dwarf snake (Eirenis modestus) is a small, non-venomous snake
  of eastern and central Georgia. It belongs to the colubrid family
  (Colubridae). The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed;
  the subspecies here is nominotypical E. m. modestus. The checklist's
  illustrated specimen is from Tbilisi. The 2021 Georgian snake survey places it among dry-climate species of
  rocky or grassland settings, mostly in the east. This page has no named
  western or Colchic locality. Adults are usually under 70 cm; the measured
  maximum is about 67 cm, and most individuals are smaller. It is harmless to
  people — it has no venom. IUCN lists it as Least Concern. It is often confused
  with the collared dwarf snake (Eirenis collaris), which the checklist places
  in the southeast.

  species range it lives on dry stony slopes, steppe, sparse shrub, and field
  edges; by day it often hides under flat stones. A separate Georgian elevation
  range has not been published. Tbilisi specimens belong to lowland and
  foothill country. Eastern Anatolian literature cites about 2000 m — that
  should not be read as a Georgian ceiling. The wider range includes Turkey,
  the Caucasus, Greek islands, and neighbouring regions; type material is not
  from Georgia.
diet: >-
  It feeds mainly on invertebrates that live under stones. In western Turkey, the stomachs of 14 snakes held mainly insects. They also contained other small relatives of insects, such as centipedes, and rarely a small lizard. Other sources also list spiders, scorpions and small lizards as food. It has no venom and seizes its prey with its mouth.
behavior: |-
  The Ring-headed Dwarf Snake is a secretive snake, active from about April to November. It hunts at dawn or dusk and is sometimes seen by day. In the heat of the day it often stays under stones. Usually in June–July it lays 3–8 relatively large eggs; these figures apply to the species as a whole. Hatchlings are about 8–12 cm long.

  In dry places in the east you may also come across the Collared Dwarf Snake (in the southeast), the Smooth snake, the European Blind Snake and, rarely, the European cat snake (Telescopus fallax). Pupil, size and head pattern tell them apart. When threatened, the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake usually flees. It is not venomous, but even a small snake can bite, so do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  Globally, the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake is rated "Least Concern": it is widespread, and no specific global threat has been named for it. It is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List. The snakes listed there are Dinnik's viper and the Caucasus viper. The 2026 Georgian checklist includes it as a confirmed species, but its distribution in Georgia is known only with medium accuracy. The checklist describes its range broadly as the east and centre, and individual sightings do not add up to a complete map. Locally, it is threatened by changes to the places where it lives and by people who mistake it for a venomous snake and kill it. If you come across one, do not pick it up.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small, slender, non-venomous snake with a round pupil. Adults often have a plain brownish or grey body. Juveniles show clearer dark bands on the head and neck. It is not a viper, nor the worm-like European Blind Snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is slender and short: it looks like a dwarf snake, not a large racer. Adults are usually under 70 cm, and the largest measured length is about 67 cm. The head is only slightly set off from the neck, and the snout is rounded. The pupil is round and the back scales are smooth. At midbody there are usually 17–18 rows of back scales, rarely 19. The Collared Dwarf Snake often has 15 rows at midbody. Still, do not pick the snake up to count its scales.
    - >-
      The back is brown, grey or reddish-brown and often almost unspotted. The belly is pale. Juveniles have dark cross-bands on top of the head and a crescent-shaped collar on the neck. This pattern gave the snake its English name, ring-headed. In adults the head pattern often fades. The Anatolian subspecies E. m. semimaculatus has rows of spots along the back, while in Georgia E. m. modestus occurs.
    - >-
      The Collared Dwarf Snake (Eirenis collaris) is a similar size, but its collar usually stays clearer. In Georgia it occurs in the southeast, for example at Taribana in the Dedoplistskaro area. The Ring-headed Dwarf Snake, by contrast, lives in the east and centre. The Smooth snake (Coronella austriaca) is stouter, has two rows of small spots along its back and prefers forest edges and damper ground. The European Blind Snake (Xerotyphlops vermicularis) looks like an earthworm and has very small eyes. The European cat snake (Telescopus fallax) has a vertical pupil, while the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake's pupil is round.
    - >-
      It has no venom, and its pupil is round. A vertical, slit-like pupil belongs to a viper, a Levantine viper or a European cat snake. A small harmless snake is easily injured, and an unfamiliar snake may be venomous. So if you come across one, keep your distance and do not pick it up.
    - >-
      The Ring-headed Dwarf Snake lives in eastern and central Georgia, and the subspecies found here is E. m. modestus. The 2026 Georgian checklist lists it as a confirmed species, and the specimen shown there comes from Tbilisi. There is no record with a precise locality from western Georgia or Colchis. It is often confused with the Collared Dwarf Snake (Eirenis collaris), which the checklist links to the southeast.
facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1838 (Martin) as Coronella modesta; it is the type species of
    Eirenis. Type material is linked to the Euphrates Expedition and Trabzon,
    not Georgia. Older catalogues sometimes treated it as a synonym of E.
    collaris.
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist figure specimen is from Tbilisi. Museum specimens from
    that city also exist. The genus needs revision; in Georgia the current names
    are E. modestus and E. m. modestus.
  - >-
    The Georgian name წყნარი ეირენისი reflects a small, secretive snake; the
    English ring-headed dwarf snake refers to the dark head and neck bands,
    which are clearer in juveniles.
faq:
  - question: Does the ring-headed dwarf snake occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes, the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake lives in eastern and central Georgia. The 2026 checklist includes it as a confirmed species of Georgia. The specimen shown in the checklist comes from Tbilisi, but the snake has been seen in other places too. Just don't expect to find it all over the country.
  - question: Where might I encounter it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In eastern and central Georgia, on dry stony or grassy ground. It often hides under stones. It has been recorded in Tbilisi, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli and Samtskhe-Javakheti, and in Tbilisi it has even been photographed at Turtle Lake. In Shida Kartli and Mtskheta-Mtianeti, only a few separate sightings are known. That does not mean it lives everywhere in those regions. No specific place where it was seen has been named in western Georgia or Colchis.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom, and when threatened it usually flees. If a snake has a vertical pupil, it is not a Ring-headed Dwarf Snake: it may be a viper, a Levantine viper or a European cat snake. Still, even a small non-venomous snake can bite, so do not pick it up.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A small, slender snake with a round pupil and smooth scales. Its back is often a plain brownish or grey. Juveniles have dark bands on the head and neck. The Collared Dwarf Snake has a clearer collar and is found in the southeast. The European Blind Snake looks like an earthworm. Do not pick the snake up to count its scales.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adult Ring-headed Dwarf Snakes are usually shorter than 70 cm. The largest one measured was about 67 cm, and most are smaller than that. These figures apply to the species as a whole, not just to Georgia.
  - question: What does it eat, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      It lays eggs, 3–8 at a time, usually in June–July. These figures apply to the species as a whole, not only to Georgia. In western Turkey it feeds mostly on insects. There it also eats other small animals that live under stones, and rarely a small lizard.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is active from April to November, mainly at dawn and dusk. These dates apply to the species as a whole, not only to Georgia. In the warm season, expect it in dry places, under stones.
  - question: How does it differ from the collared dwarf snake?
    answer: >-
      Both are small, non-venomous Eirenis snakes. Look at the collar first: the Collared Dwarf Snake usually has a clearer one. They also live in different parts of Georgia: the Collared Dwarf Snake in the southeast, the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake in the east and centre. Scale rows at midbody often differ too: 15 in the collared species, 17–18 in the ring-headed one. But do not pick the snake up to check this.
