overview: >-
  Brown marmorated stink bug (Halyomorpha halys) is an insect in the stink bug
  family Pentatomidae. Georgian official sources use the names აზიური ფაროსანა
  and აზიური ბაღლინჯო. The [Georgian Biodiversity Database](https://biodiversity.iliauni.edu.ge/en/species/14072)
  at Ilia State University lists it as Halyomorpha halys (Stål, 1855) and as an
  introduced species in Georgia.

  In Georgia this is not a native wildlife species. [EPPO reports](https://gd.eppo.int/taxon/HALYHA/distribution/GE)
  the first Georgian records from 2016, in Khobi municipality and Pitsunda, and
  still lists the country status as Present, restricted distribution. Georgia's
  [National Food Agency](https://nfa.gov.ge/Ge/Page/Brown%20Marmorated%20Stink%20Bug%20%28BMSB%29)
  links the 2016 spread with damage to hazelnut crops and highlights western
  Georgian outbreak areas in 2017.

  Treating it only as a western Georgian pest is now too narrow. [NCDC guidance](https://test.ncdc.ge/pages/user/LetterContent.aspx?ID=6f8dffa7-e31f-4b8f-b7f6-f188c761e44b)
  used on this page states that by the end of 2017 the insect had also appeared
  in eastern Georgia. This page's distribution map now has records from all 12
  Georgian regions, but by the count rule its spread is treated as confirmed in
  8 regions; the other 4 regions are recorded-only at this stage.

  For a reader in Georgia the useful answer is simple: this is an invasive,
  plant-feeding agricultural pest, especially relevant around hazelnut orchards,
  fruit trees, vegetable plots, gardens, and houses.

  Adults are about 12-17 mm long. The body is broad, shield-shaped, brown and
  marbled; useful field marks are the pale bands on the antennae, legs, and
  outer edge of the abdomen.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, brown marmorated stink bug is mainly tied to agricultural and
  settled landscapes: hazelnut orchards, fruit gardens, vegetable plots, yards,
  garden edges, shrubs, and trees that provide host plants. It is not a native
  conservation-priority forest or steppe species; it is an invasive pest.

  The regional map on this page shows records in all 12 Georgian regions:
  distribution is treated as confirmed in 8 regions, while 4 regions are marked
  as recorded-only. Reptiles.ge field photo records are shown separately:
  Chiatura, Kutaisi, Lagodekhi, Kvareli, Akhmeta, Mtskheta, and Old Tbilisi.
  These are first-party photo-backed records on the page; place, date, and
  author are visible in the gallery and in the point layer on the map.

  The sources used here do not establish a reliable elevation range for Georgia.
diet: >-
  Brown marmorated stink bug is a polyphagous plant feeder. Adults and nymphs
  pierce plant tissues with sucking mouthparts and feed from leaves, shoots,
  fruit, seeds, and other soft plant parts.

  Georgian official material lists hazelnut, pome and stone fruits, berries,
  vegetables, sunflower, and maize, with examples including peach, nectarine,
  apple, pear, raspberry, tomato, and pepper. International risk analysis also
  treats it as a pest with a very broad host range.

  Feeding often leaves depressed, discoloured, or corky damage on fruit. This is
  an agricultural damage issue, not a biting or attacking behaviour toward
  people.
behavior: >-
  In Georgia, National Food Agency material places feeding from late April-May
  through late autumn. Eggs are laid in clusters on the underside of leaves;
  nymphs hatch after a few days, pass through five instars, and then become
  adults.

  NCDC material notes observations of two full generations in western Georgia.
  That is a western Georgian observation and should not be read as an exact
  calendar for every microclimate in the country.

  In autumn, adults seek dry, protected overwintering places. They may enter
  houses, attics, window and door gaps, wall cracks, tree hollows, and leaf
  litter. When disturbed or crushed, they release a strong odour.
conservation: >-
  For brown marmorated stink bug, the practical frame is monitoring and
  management. NFA material describes state monitoring, trap use, actions planned
  around outbreak areas, and public information work; in a private garden, the
  first step is regular observation. [Kharabadze 2022](https://journals.org.ge/index.php/aans/article/view/125)
  reviews the Georgian context for distribution, biology, and management.

  In orchards and hazelnut plantations, control should be based on actual pest
  presence and life stage. NFA material especially links chemical action with
  the nymph stage, but any product should be registered, label-compliant, used
  with protective equipment, and aligned with official agricultural advice.

interaction: |-
  The brown marmorated stink bug is not poisonous to people. It does not bite people or pets or transmit diseases to them.
  When disturbed, it releases a substance with an unpleasant smell. Some people may be sensitive or allergic to it, although the [NCDC guidance](https://test.ncdc.ge/pages/user/LetterContent.aspx?ID=6f8dffa7-e31f-4b8f-b7f6-f188c761e44b) does not describe it as life-threatening. There is no need to crush the bug by hand: the smell gets stronger and lingers on surfaces.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This bug is flat like a shield and brown with a marbled pattern. You can recognize it by the white bands on its antennae, alternating light and dark spots or stripes along the sides of its abdomen, and its broad body with a mosaic-like pattern.
  traits:
    - >-
      An adult is about 12–17 mm in size. Its body is broad and slightly flattened, and looks like a shield from above.
    - >-
      Colour alone is not enough to identify it: it can sometimes look greyer or dark brown. More reliable signs are the white stripes on its whiskers and the alternating light and dark patches along the outer edge of its belly.
    - >-
      Light-colored stripes are also visible on its legs. The corners of its shoulders do not have sharp spines, so simply seeing a “brown stink bug” is not enough to identify it with certainty.
    - >-
      Nymphs, or young insects that have not yet grown into adults, are smaller than adults. Their color changes as they grow: newly emerged nymphs may be reddish, then develop dark and whitish-brown coloring.
    - >-
      It can be confused with other stink bugs in Georgia. For confirmation,
      photograph the insect sharply from above so the antennae, abdomen edge,
      and body shape are visible.

faq:
  - question: Does brown marmorated stink bug occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. EPPO treats it as present in Georgia and dates the first records to
      2016, from Khobi municipality and Pitsunda. NCDC guidance states that by
      the end of 2017 it had also appeared in eastern Georgia.
  - question: Where is it confirmed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The map on this page shows brown marmorated stink bug records in all 12
      Georgian regions. By the count rule, distribution is confirmed in 8
      regions and 4 regions are recorded-only. The gallery also includes
      Reptiles.ge field photo records from Chiatura, Kutaisi, Lagodekhi, Kvareli,
      Akhmeta, Mtskheta, and Old Tbilisi.
  - question: How do I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Look for a 12-17 mm shield-shaped body, brown marbled pattern, pale bands
      on the antennae, and alternating pale-dark marks along the abdomen edge.
      Brown colour alone is not enough.
  - question: Does it bite people?
    answer: >-
      No. NCDC guidance states that it does not bite people or pets and does not
      spread human or animal diseases.
  - question: Why does it enter houses?
    answer: >-
      Adults search for dry protected overwintering sites in autumn. That is why
      they may enter attics, window and door gaps, and other cracks.
  - question: What crops does it damage most?
    answer: >-
      Hazelnut damage has been especially important in Georgia. It can also
      damage fruit trees, berries, vegetables, maize, and sunflower.
  - question: What should I do if I find one indoors?
    answer: >-
      Do not crush it. Remove it without direct handling and place it in soapy
      water, or use a vacuum that can be cleaned afterward. Use pesticides only
      according to official label and safety directions.
