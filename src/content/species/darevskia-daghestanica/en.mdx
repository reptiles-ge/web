description: >-
  A harmless rock lizard of north-eastern Georgia's mountains. Colour will not

overview: >-
  The Dagestan lizard (Darevskia daghestanica) is a rock lizard in the true lizard family (Lacertidae). It has no venom and is harmless to people. According to published measurements, its head and body are about 4.4–5.8 cm long, and its tail is often longer than the head and body combined. Its presence in Georgia has been confirmed in the northeastern mountains. It is also listed as a confirmed species in a 2026 Georgian checklist. Recent records name Tusheti and Mount Tbatana in Akhmeta Municipality. It is not expected in the lowlands of western or southern Georgia. In the past, it was often considered a close relative of the Caucasian lizard (Darevskia caucasica) or one of its subspecies. It is now considered a separate species.
habitat: >-
  Two modern Georgian points are named. In Tarkhnishvili and co-authors (2026)
  a barcode is from Tusheti; the scalation figure is from Akhmeta municipality,
  Tbatana Mountain. Both are in the mountains of Kakheti. On this atlas map
  Kakheti reflects those records; it does not mean the species occurs on the
  Alazani plain or in Kakheti's dry lowlands. Darevsky (1967) and Doronin
  (2015, 2022) place it on montane rocky, moderately dry ground: cliffs,
  slopes, and river gorges. The main range is the northern macroslope of the
  Greater Caucasus from Dagestan to the Darial Gorge in North Ossetia; it
  enters Tusheti and Azerbaijan to the south. In 1963 Darevsky described an
  isolated population in the Greater Liakhvi gorge above Java (Dzau), on the
  road toward the Roki Pass; Doronin and co-authors (2022) confirmed that
  population in 2021. That isolate is a south-western locality, not a reason to
  map all of Shida Kartli, so it is not expanded on the map. The 2026
  discussion names a contiguous range with D. caucasica on the upper Tergi
  (Terek), where hybridization is possible — a contact-zone note, not a
  complete list of villages. Elevation for the caucasica complex in the
  literature is about 500–3200 m; a measured range for Georgian populations is
  not published here. Occasional records come from forest without rock
  outcrops; look first on rock or stone.
diet: >-
  Stomach contents from Georgia have not been published for this species.
  Caucasian rock lizards in Darevsky's account feed mainly on small
  invertebrates — spiders, beetles, flies, grasshoppers, ants. The same is
  expected here, but a Georgian diet has not been measured.
behavior: >-
  A day-active rock lizard. The body is flattened, which helps it enter
  crevices. Tarkhnishvili and co-authors (2020) treat it as mainly saxicolous
  but also found away from rocks. Doronin and co-authors (2021) describe a
  forest site without rocky outcrops. No separate hourly or seasonal calendar
  has been published for Georgia. It lays eggs. In intra-mountain Dagestan
  (Kidov and co-authors 2024) breeding females were taken in mid-to-late May;
  eggs were laid from late May to mid-June, clutch size 1–5, artificial
  incubation 36–51 days. That is a Dagestan measurement, not a Tusheti
  calendar. The tail can be shed — a short or regenerated tail does not change
  the species.
conservation: >-
  IUCN listed the species as Least Concern in 2009 (assessment 2008): the
  population is presumed large and not declining fast enough for a higher
  category. That is a global status. It is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List
  (Resolution No. 190); among Darevskia that list includes Clark's, Dahl's, and
  the Adjarian rock lizards. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically
  confirmed; distribution confidence is medium. Deep genetic differences among
  populations are described; the checklist does not apply infraspecific names.
  The Liakhvi isolate is narrow — local pressure there could differ, but there
  is no separate national assessment. Do not collect animals.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and is harmless to people. Do not pick it up: its tail can break off easily, and handling stresses the animal. If you see one, photograph its back, side and the scales on its head. Note where you saw it and at what elevation, then leave it where it is. Colour alone cannot confirm the species, especially in Tusheti and near Tbatana, where the Caucasian lizard is also listed.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This is a small rock lizard (Darevskia) with a flattened head. In Georgia, it is found in the mountains of the northeast. Its color does not distinguish it from the Caucasus lizard.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is slender and flattened. The head is relatively narrow and flat, and the hind legs are long. According to Roitberg, these features can help distinguish it from caucasica where both occur, though not at a glance from a photo. The scales on its back are smooth or slightly raised. The Tbatana illustrations show the scales on the head and the plates in front of the rear opening; these details are often hard to see in photos taken in the wild.
    - >-
      The back is often grayish brown, yellowish brown, or ash gray. Two parallel rows of dark spots or a netlike pattern may run along the spine. Sometimes the pattern is faint or absent. The pale stripes on the sides often do not reach the scales on top of the head. Identification guides list this as one of the species’ features, but it does not appear in every individual. Color alone cannot distinguish Darevskia species.
    - >-
      It is small. One specimen from Levashi in Dagestan measures 49 mm from head to body. In inland Dagestan, larger females measure 43.95–56.20 mm from head to body. The tail is often longer than the head and body together, but the Levashi specimen’s tail is damaged, so its full length is unknown. Data on specimens from Georgia have not been published here.
    - >-
      The Caucasian rock lizard (D. caucasica) has a contiguous range on the
      upper Tergi and is also named at Tbatana. Colour will not tell them
      apart. D. caucasica often has clearer lateral stripes; in contact,
      D. daghestanica tends to have more scales around midbody, more ventrals,
      more femoral pores, and a narrower, flatter head. In the field the safer
      split is place: Tusheti is named for daghestanica, Truso/Zakagori for
      caucasica. Derjugin's lizard (D. derjugini) is in the same genus but is a
      ground-dwelling forest species, not a cliff lizard. The Adjarian rock
      lizard (D. mixta) belongs to western and central contact zones, not as a
      Tusheti substitute.
    - >-
      The 2026 list considers this species biologically confirmed. Recent records name Tusheti (barcode) and Mount Tbatana in Akhmeta Municipality (figure). Do not expect to find it throughout the country: it is not expected in the western and southern lowlands. Color will not distinguish it from the Caucasian lizard (D. caucasica); in the field, location is the safe way to tell them apart.
