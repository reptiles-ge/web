overview: >-
  Clark's rock lizard (Darevskia clarkorum) is a harmless rock lizard of Adjara
  in southwestern Georgia. It belongs to the true lizards (Lacertidae). It has
  no venom and is not dangerous to people. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it
  as biologically confirmed. Do not assume it throughout Georgia: named modern
  barcodes and head-scale figures are from the Charnali river valley
  (Khelvachauri Municipality). This atlas map marks Adjara from those records;
  it does not mean the species occurs in every gorge in the region. English
  names include Clark's lizard. The checklist Georgian name is ლაზური ხვლიკი
  (Laz lizard); this atlas keeps თურქული ხვლიკი, the name used on Georgia's 2014
  Red List. The type locality is in Turkey (Cankurtaran Pass between Hopa and
  Borçka, Artvin), not in Georgia. IUCN listed it as Endangered in 2009. It is
  on Georgia's 2014 Red List as EN.
habitat: >-
  It occurs in Georgia. Do not assume it in Kakheti, Javakheti, or the Greater
  Caucasus. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 figure a specimen from Charnali Gorge and
  barcode two animals from the Charnali river valley (CaBOL-IDs 1027431 and
  1007143). Distribution confidence is medium. IUCN (2009) also names a small
  isolated part of the range on the western slopes of Mount Mtirala near Batumi.
  That is a second named Adjara area, not a proof that Charnali and Mtirala are
  the same site, and not a nationwide map. A 2021 Reptile Database note that the
  species may reach Kintrishi Gorge to the north is personal communication, not
  a named checklist locality. Habitat is damp, bushy ground near streams, with
  stones, rocky outcrops, and talus; animals also use mountain meadow with rock.
  In Adjara look along forested gorge slopes and stony stream sides, not in dry
  steppe. Species-wide elevation figures disagree: IUCN gives 1,500–2,200 m;
  Darevsky and Tuniyev (1997) give about 900–1,700 m; Turkish series include
  much lower sites (about 450–2,250 m). Charnali is a Colchic gorge; a measured
  Georgian elevational limit is not on this page. The 2009 IUCN text also lists
  Armenia, citing Baran and Atatür (1998). The 2026 checklist and The Reptile
  Database do not treat Armenia as part of the range. Do not assume it there
  from this atlas.
diet: >-
  This page has no Georgian stomach-content study. Like other Darevskia it is
  expected to take small invertebrates — insects and similar prey — seized in
  the jaws, without venom. In Turkey, Franzen (1991) also recorded plant feeding
  in this species. That is a Turkish field note, not a Georgian menu.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. It is a rock lizard: it basks on stones and hides in
  crevices. In northeastern Turkey, Ilgaz (2007) collected animals from about
  08:30 to 18:30, often in cloudy or rainy weather. That is a Turkish timetable,
  not a Georgian hourly table. It lays eggs. A Georgian clutch size, laying
  month, and overwintering calendar are not measured here. In Turkish
  populations, Bülbül and co-authors (2016) found sexual maturity at about 1–3
  years depending on altitude, and maximum ages of about 10–12 years. Do not
  treat those figures as Georgian averages. If grabbed, the tail may break and
  the lizard may bite. Watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assessed it as Endangered in 2009 (Tuniyev and co-authors; EN B1ab(i,iii))
  because the known extent of occurrence was under 5,000 km², records were in
  fewer than five locations, and habitat was judged to be declining through
  overgrazing. That assessment is old. IUCN still lists the Charnali form
  separately as Darevskia dryada, Critically Endangered (2009). This atlas
  follows the 2026 checklist and treats dryada as a subspecies, but that IUCN
  listing remains a signal that the Charnali population is highly threatened.
  Georgia's 2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190) includes it as EN, citing IUCN.
  The decree's remark that it is considered Vulnerable worldwide does not match
  the 2009 Endangered assessment. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat it as
  biologically confirmed. A 2019 Charnali field report (Barateli, Rufford)
  estimated about 256 ± 48 animals in one study area and named construction and
  habitat loss as local threats. That is a grant report for part of the gorge,
  not a national census. It is not listed on CITES. Do not take wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and poses no medical risk. If handled it may bite; the
  tail may break. It is on Georgia's Red List. Leave it where it is. Do not keep
  wild animals.

    A small rock lizard of Adjara gorges. Colour is not enough. Field ID among

  traits:
    - >-
      It is a rock Darevskia with well-developed limbs — on stones and rock, not
      a large ground Lacerta. In Turkish series the back is ivy green, light
      green, brown, or grey-brown, often with small dark dots. A dark temporal
      stripe may hold a few pale-centred spots; one near the forelimb can be
      bluish. The belly is often bright yellow. That is typical appearance
      (Ilgaz 2007) and does not separate species by colour.
    - >-
      Snout–vent length in a large Turkish series was about 4.4–6.7 cm (mean
      near 5.9 cm); original measurements were slightly larger. The tail is
      often longer than the body; one male reached about 18.7 cm total length.
      Arribas and co-authors (2021) found the Charnali dryada form larger, with
      a longer head in adult males and more dorsal scales, than typical
      clarkorum. A full Georgian measurement series is not on this page.
    - >-
      The 2026 checklist keeps the southwestern Georgian form as D. c. dryada,
      a geographic subspecies, not a separate species page. Some authors (Arribas
      et al. 2021) still treat dryada as a valid taxon, more likely a subspecies;
      Schmidtler et al. (2002) treated it as an ecological race. Colour will not
      settle that argument in the field. Checklist figures show head scalation
      and preanal plates from Charnali.
    - >-
      The Ajarian rock lizard (D. adjarica) is a checklist candidate in the
      parvula complex; named barcodes are from Shuakhevi, not Charnali. Derjugin's
      lizard (D. derjugini) is a smaller ground-dwelling forest species — look
      for it in leaf litter and grass, not as a typical cliff lizard. The mixed
      rock lizard (D. mixta) is a close relative but the checklist treats it as
      absent from Adjara. Do not identify by colour in southwestern Georgia.
    - >-
      The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. Do not
      assume it throughout Georgia: named modern barcodes and head-scale
      figures are from the Charnali river valley (Khelvachauri Municipality).
      This atlas map marks Adjara from those records; it does not mean the
      species occurs in every gorge in the region. The Ajarian rock lizard (D.
      adjarica) is a candidate on the checklist; named barcodes are from
      Shuakhevi, not Charnali.

faq:
  - question: Is Clark's rock lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is biologically confirmed in the 2026 checklist. Named barcodes
      and figures are from the Charnali river valley in Adjara. Do not assume it
      throughout Georgia.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In southwestern Georgia (Adjara). The named modern locality is Charnali
      Gorge (Khelvachauri). IUCN 2009 also names the western slopes of Mtirala
      near Batumi. Look on stony, bushy slopes near streams. This is not a
      Kakheti or Javakheti species.
  - question: Is this Darevskia dryada?
    answer: >-
      On this atlas, dryada is a subspecies (D. c. dryada), not a separate
      Georgian species. Some papers still discuss species rank. The Charnali
      population is the form those papers mean.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      It is a small green-to-brown rock lizard of Adjara gorges. Colour is not
      enough. Compare range and habit with adjarica (Shuakhevi, parvula complex)
      and derjugini (forest floor). Head scales in the checklist figures are
      more reliable than back colour.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. If grabbed it may bite; the tail may break. It is
      Red-Listed in Georgia. Leave it where it is.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Snout–vent length in Turkish material is often about 4.5–7 cm. The
      Charnali dryada form is described as larger. A full Georgian series is not
      on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      There is no Georgian diet study on this page. It is expected to eat small
      invertebrates. Plant feeding has been noted in Turkey.
  - question: When is it active?

      By day. A full Georgian seasonal calendar is not on this page. Turkish
      collections include daytime hours, including cloudy weather.
