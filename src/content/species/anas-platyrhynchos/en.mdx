commonName: Mallard
location: Year-round resident, migrant and winter visitor in Georgia
description: >-
  A dabbling duck of wetlands and open water. Confirmed in Georgia on the 2024
  national bird checklist as a year-round resident, a passage migrant, and a
  winter visitor. Breeding is concentrated on highland lakes; numbers rise in
  autumn and winter.
overview: >-
  The mallard (Anas platyrhynchos) is a medium-sized dabbling duck in the family
  Anatidae. The usual Georgian name is გარეული იხვი. The 2024 annotated checklist
  of the birds of Georgia codes it YR-R; M; WV: a breeding resident present
  throughout the year, a bird of passage, and a winter visitor. Seasonality is
  classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality is high (H), with
  photograph, ringing data, and hunter-collected evidence. Adults are about
  50–65 cm long at species level. Breeding males have a green head and yellow
  bill; females are mottled brown. It is not a threat to people. IUCN lists the
  species as Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns the same category
  nationally. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  Look for it on lakes, reservoirs, rivers, marshes, flooded fields, and urban
  ponds. It is a dabbling duck: it feeds at the surface or by tipping forward,
  not by deep diving. The 2024 checklist confirms country-level resident,
  migrant, and winter-visitor status but does not publish a municipality map.
  Named Georgian localities with published counts or sampling: the Javakheti
  highland lakes Madatapa, Bughdasheni, Khanchali, Saghamo, Paravani and
  Kartsakhi (Samtskhe–Javakheti) are mapped on this page from waterfowl census
  and avian-influenza study sites. Lewis et al. (2013) place the main Georgian
  duck breeding and post-breeding moult concentrations on those shallow upland
  lakes (Madatapa about 2,100 m), and winter concentrations on the Kolkheti
  Lowland Wetlands of the Black Sea coast. Mid-winter duck counts on Kolkheti
  were of the order of about 50,000 birds of several species, not mallard alone.
  Those two regional blocks are coloured from those sources. Other wetlands may
  hold birds, but this page does not colour them without a compiled gazetteer.
  Domestic and feral mallards also use park lakes in towns; those birds are the
  same species and often hybridise with wild stock.
diet: >-
  There is no separate weighed Georgian diet study on this page. At species
  level it takes seeds, aquatic plants, invertebrates, and small vertebrates it
  can catch at the water’s edge or by dabbling. In parks it also takes bread and
  other food from people — that is not a wild diet. It is a traditional gamebird
  in Georgia; hunting is regulated separately from Red List protection.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day and often stays in loose flocks outside the breeding
  season. Flight is fast and direct, with rapid wingbeats; the blue-purple
  wing-panel (speculum) bordered with white is a useful mark in the air. The
  female’s loud quack is the familiar duck call; males give quieter, softer
  notes. The clip is xeno-canto XC1094846 (Petter Westberg, Busör, Halland,
  Sweden) — a male call, not a Georgian recording. Nesting is usually on the
  ground near water, in cover; clutches at species level are often about 8–13
  eggs with incubation of about 26–28 days — those figures are from the wider
  range, not a Georgian nest sample on this page. The 2024 checklist treats the
  species as a resident breeder in Georgia. Numbers increase with autumn
  migrants and winter visitors. Eclipse (non-breeding) males look much more like
  females for part of the year after the breeding season.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Anas platyrhynchos as Least Concern on the
  IUCN Red List (2025 assessment), with a very large global range and
  population. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global and national
  category LC. Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution No. 190) does not
  list the mallard; listed related waterfowl there include ruddy shelduck
  (Tadorna ferruginea, წითელი იხვი, VU) and white-headed duck (Oxyura
  leucocephala, თეთრთვალა იხვი, EN). Checklist evidence for this species includes
  ringing data (RD) and hunter samples (CH), so regulated hunting is part of the
  documented Georgian picture. Wetland loss, disturbance, and disease (including
  avian influenza sampled in Georgian ducks) are pressures discussed in regional
  studies; a separate national mallard population trend is not given on this
  page. Do not disturb nests or handle wild birds without a permit.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and is not a threat to people. Do not chase or catch wild
  birds. Feeding bread in parks is common but is poor nutrition; leave wild
  flocks alone. Domestic ducks are the same species and may look white, piebald,
  or otherwise atypical — those birds are still mallards. Hybrid young with wild
  birds occur where domestic stock mixes freely.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium dabbling duck. Breeding males have a glossy green head, white
    neck-ring, chestnut breast and yellow bill. Females and eclipse males are
    mottled brown with an orange-and-dark bill. Both sexes show a blue-purple
    white-bordered wing speculum. In Georgia the usual confusion is other brown
    ducks and domestic park ducks.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 50–65 cm at species level; wingspan often about 81–98 cm;
      mass often about 0.7–1.6 kg. A separate measured series from Georgia is not
      given on this page. Breeding male: bottle-green head, narrow white collar,
      purple-brown breast, grey body, black rear, curled central tail feathers,
      yellow bill. Female: cryptic mottled brown, darker crown and eye-stripe,
      bill mottled orange and dark. Both sexes have orange legs and a glossy
      blue-purple secondary panel (speculum) edged with white — clear in flight
      and often at rest when the wing is folded.
    - >-
      Juveniles resemble the female. After breeding, males enter eclipse plumage
      and look brown and female-like for weeks while they moult flight feathers;
      the yellow bill and (when visible) speculum still help. Domestic and feral
      birds may be white, black, piebald, or oversized; they remain this species
      and interbreed freely with wild mallards.
    - >-
      Eurasian teal (Anas crecca) is much smaller, with a green-and-chestnut head
      pattern in males; it is BB; M; WV on the 2024 checklist. Northern pintail
      (Anas acuta) is longer-necked with a needle tail in males; checklist status
      OB; M. Gadwall (Mareca strepera) males are grey with a black rear and a
      white wing-patch, not a green head; BB; M. Northern shoveler (Spatula
      clypeata) has a huge spatulate bill; BB; M. Diving ducks (Aythya) sit lower
      in the water and dive; they lack the mallard’s dabbling tip-up. This atlas
      does not yet have separate pages for those lookalikes.
    - >-
      It is harmless. A green-headed yellow-billed duck on open water in Georgia
      in spring is almost always this species. A mottled brown duck with a
      white-edged blue wing-panel is usually a female or eclipse mallard, or a
      closely related dabbling duck — check size and bill shape.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 in Systema Naturae. The 2024 Georgian
    checklist follows the OSME Region List for names: order Anseriformes,
    family Anatidae, genus Anas. An older synonym still seen in literature is
    Anas boschas. Field identification is to species.
  - >-
    Handbooks treat Caucasus birds as the nominate subspecies A. p.
    platyrhynchos (Holarctic except Greenland). The Greenland form A. p.
    conboschas does not occur here. Domestic ducks are derived from this
    species; “A. p. domesticus” is not a valid wild subspecies.
  - >-
    Georgian is გარეული იხვი (“wild duck”). English is mallard; older texts say
    wild duck. Russian is кряква. Related Red List names — წითელი იხვი and
    თეთრთვალა იხვი — refer to other species.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R; M; WV, data quality A, global and national Red
    List LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH; RD; CH (photograph; ringing
    data; collected by hunter). That is country-level status. The regional map
    on this page rests on named wetland studies, not on a checklist gazetteer.
faq:
  - question: Is the mallard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident, passage migrant, and winter visitor
      (YR-R; M; WV).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      On open water and wetlands. Named published sites include the Javakheti
      lakes (Madatapa, Bughdasheni, Khanchali, Saghamo, Paravani, Kartsakhi) and
      the Kolkheti Lowland Wetlands in winter. Park ponds also hold mallards,
      often with domestic-looking birds. This page does not map every region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Breeding male: green head, yellow bill, white collar, chestnut breast.
      Female: mottled brown with orange-and-dark bill. Both sexes show a
      blue-purple white-bordered wing speculum. Smaller teal and shovelers differ
      in size and bill shape.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 50–65 cm long at species level, with a wingspan often given around
      81–98 cm and mass about 0.7–1.6 kg. A separate measured series from Georgia
      is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Seeds, aquatic plants, invertebrates, and other small prey taken by
      dabbling. Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year as a resident, plus passage and winter visitors, according to the
      2024 checklist. Numbers are typically higher from autumn through winter on
      lowland wetlands.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means resident breeder. Published work
      points to the Javakheti upland lakes as the main duck breeding and
      post-breeding moult area; a nationwide nest census is not on this page.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. It is globally Least Concern and coded LC nationally on
      the 2024 checklist. It is not on the 2014 Georgian Red List. It is a
      traditional game species; follow current hunting rules. Do not disturb
      nests.
