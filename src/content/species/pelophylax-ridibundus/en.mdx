commonName: "Marsh Frog"
location: "near water throughout Georgia"
description: >-
  A harmless water frog. It is Georgia's most widespread amphibian; look for it
  at marshes, canals, lakes and slow streams.
overview: >-
  The marsh frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) is Georgia's most widespread harmless
  water frog. It belongs to the true frogs (Ranidae). At species level adults
  are usually about 6–12 cm from snout to vent; females are generally larger
  than males. A measured Georgian series is not on this page. It is not
  venomous. IUCN lists it as Least Concern. Look for it at open, warm water —
  marshes, canals, lakes, ponds and slow streams, including farm and town
  waters. A 1996 Georgian review treats it as nearly ubiquitous and cites 62
  documented localities. 2024 samples come from Black Sea and Caspian basins
  and from the Greater and Lesser Caucasus. The 2026 checklist figure is Skuri,
  Tsalenjikha. Named sites also include Borjomi Canyon and the Tbilisi
  lowlands. Taxonomic note: older books used Rana ridibunda. Some papers treat
  P. bedriagae as a separate species; the 2026 Georgian checklist treats the
  species as P. ridibundus.
habitat: >-
  This is a water frog and rarely leaves the water. The 1996 review records it
  in canals, streams and ponds; on treeless uplands of the Lesser Caucasus it
  is tied to relatively large pools and lakes. It prefers open, well-warmed
  shores with herbaceous cover. It is often abundant in farmland and towns. In
  Borjomi Canyon it belongs to the open-habitat spawning guild and lays more
  than one clutch in a season. Elevation is recorded from sea level to over
  2,500 m; it is rare in the subalpine and alpine belts of the Greater Caucasus.
  The atlas map marks every administrative region — that reflects a wide range,
  not even density. Named points include Tsalenjikha (Skuri), Borjomi Canyon
  (about 900 m) and the Tbilisi lowlands. Egg-laying in the southern Georgian
  highlands is described at about 1,950 m. Six 2024 barcodes come from
  different parts of the country; exact coordinates are in that paper's figure
  and supplement. This page does not list every municipality.
diet: >-
  A separate Georgian diet study is not on this page. At species level adults
  take invertebrates, mainly aquatic and shoreline insects. Large individuals
  sometimes take other amphibians, small fish or other small vertebrates.
  Tadpoles eat mainly detritus, algae and small invertebrates.
behavior: >-
  By day it often sits at the water's edge or at the surface with the head
  showing. When disturbed it jumps into the water. It is more active in the
  warm season. At species level it often overwinters in water; in the south
  activity may continue in mild weather. A full Georgian winter calendar is
  not on this page. Breeding depends on water temperature and starts later
  than in most other amphibians. In the Tbilisi lowlands males call from late
  March and egg clutches are recorded from mid-April to late September. In
  Borjomi Canyon (about 900 m) the season runs from about early May to
  mid-July. In the southern highlands (about 1,950 m) eggs are laid from
  mid-June for about a month — these are named sites, not a single national
  calendar. Males have a pair of grey vocal sacs at the corners of the mouth;
  choruses carry far. If you find one, watch and leave it. Do not pick it up
  or move it between water bodies. Wash your hands after contact.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists it as Least Concern. It is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List. At
  species level it is relatively tolerant of pollution and persists in
  settlements. Local declines can follow drainage, concrete banks or loss of
  vegetated spawning shores. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically
  confirmed. P. bedriagae is not accepted as a separate species here: Georgian
  mitochondrial sequences are closer to a West Asian and Balkan haplogroup
  that some authors call bedriagae than to typical Central European
  ridibundus (difference over 5%), but both haplogroups occur in the type area
  of the species. Do not assign a Georgian individual to bedriagae in the
  field.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and is harmless to people. The skin produces mucus; that
  is a defence, not a medical venom. Do not handle it. Wash your hands after
  contact.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large, water-tied frog with smooth skin, a horizontal pupil and no sticky
    toe pads. In Georgia it is most often confused with the long-legged wood
    frog.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is stocky, the hind legs long, the toes webbed. There are no
      sticky discs on the toe tips — that separates it from treefrogs. At
      species level adults are usually about 6–12 cm from snout to vent;
      females are often larger than males. Georgian measurements are not on
      this page.
    - >-
      The back is green, olive, grey or brownish, often with dark spots. A
      pale stripe down the middle of the back is common. The belly is pale
      and sometimes dark-spotted. It usually lacks the dark mask behind the
      eye that brown frogs have. Colour varies; a green back alone is not
      enough.
    - >-
      The pupil is horizontal. The eardrum is visible. Two skin folds run
      along the sides of the back (dorsolateral folds). Males have a pair of
      grey vocal sacs at the mouth corners and a nuptial pad on the first
      finger in the breeding season. These marks help, but size and habitat
      should be used together.
    - >-
      The long-legged wood frog (Rana macrocnemis) is more a land and forest
      frog. It often has a dark temporal mask and stays away from open water
      outside the breeding season. The marsh frog stays at the water by day
      and jumps in when disturbed. AmphibiaWeb notes that the overlapping-heel
      test does not always work in Transcaucasian animals — this page does
      not rely on that character.
    - >-
      Eastern and yellow-lemon treefrogs are small, with sticky toe pads, and
      climb. Toads have warty skin and large glands behind the eyes. The
      Caucasian parsley frog and eastern spadefoot have vertical pupils.
faq:
  - question: Does the marsh frog occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. The 1996
      review records it almost throughout the country. Barcodes come from
      different parts of Georgia; the figure is Skuri, Tsalenjikha.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      At marshes, canals, lakes, ponds and slow streams — from lowlands into
      the foothills, including villages and towns. It is scarce in high
      subalpine and alpine belts. The map marks every region, but density is
      not even.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It is not venomous and is harmless to people. Do not pick it up.
      Wash your hands after contact.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A medium to large frog at the water's edge, with smooth skin, a
      horizontal pupil, webbed toes and no sticky toe pads. It is often green
      or olive with dark spots. Forest brown frogs usually have a dark mask
      behind the eye and stay farther from open water.
  - question: How does it differ from the long-legged wood frog?
    answer: >-
      The marsh frog stays in water and is often green. The long-legged wood
      frog (Rana macrocnemis) is browner, lives in forest and on land, and
      often has a dark patch behind the eye.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      At species level adults are usually about 6–12 cm from snout to vent.
      Females are generally larger than males. Georgian measurements are not
      on this page.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      In the warm season, at water. In the Tbilisi lowlands breeding is
      recorded from spring into autumn; it is later and shorter at higher
      elevations. A full Georgian winter calendar is not on this page.
  - question: Is bedriagae a separate species in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Not in this checklist. Georgian taxonomy in 2024–2026 treats P. bedriagae
      as a junior synonym of P. ridibundus or a geographic subspecies. Use
      Pelophylax ridibundus in the field.
