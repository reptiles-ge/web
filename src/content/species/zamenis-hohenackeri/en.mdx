commonName: Transcaucasian Ratsnake
location: Georgia — dry rocky and grassland country
description: >-
  A harmless, blotched ratsnake of dry rocky and grassland habitats in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The Transcaucasian rat snake (Zamenis hohenackeri) is a nonvenomous, spotted snake. It lives in dry, rocky and grassy places. As of 2026, its presence in Georgia is confirmed; the subspecies found there is Z. h. hohenackeri. Adults are often less than a metre long. Adults measured in neighbouring Azerbaijan had bodies about 62–78 cm long, with tails shorter than their bodies. Globally, the species faces a low risk of extinction. It is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. It is often confused with the Aesculapian snake and other spotted snakes, including vipers.
habitat: >-
  According to a 2021 study of snakes in Georgia, this species is associated with relatively dry, rocky or grassy places and is found mainly in the east, alongside the sakheebiani mtsuravi, the mottled grass snake and the nose-horned viper. The same study notes that several of these grass snakes, including this species, have also been recorded in suitable places in the west. Previously, they were thought to occur only in the east. This atlas’s map marks Imereti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Shida Kartli, Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. Kakheti does not appear on the map, although published descriptions associate the species mainly with dry areas in the east. The map of Georgia is still incomplete. Specific records come from the village of Gomaro in Adigeni Municipality (a specimen listed in 2026, with photographs of its head) and from Boshuri (a genetically studied specimen, Jandzik et al. 2013). Gori Municipality has both Zemo Boshuri and Kvemo Boshuri. Tbilisi is also historically listed among the places from which specimens used for the species’ original description came (Tiflis, Strauch 1873). This does not mean the snake occurs in equal numbers throughout the country. Across its range, the species is often found in mountain areas: Jandzik gives elevations mostly between 900 and 3,000 metres, with lower elevations in the southern part of its range. The elevations at which it occurs in Georgia have not been established separately on this page. Unlike the Aesculapian snake, it is not considered a species of the damp Colchic forest.
diet: >-
  This page does not include a separate study of its diet in Georgia. According to Armenia’s Red Book, adults catch small rodents and lizards, while young ones also catch insects. Descriptions from Azerbaijan and the Caucasus say the same. It kills its prey by constriction and is not venomous. In rocky and grassy places in Georgia, it likely eats the same animals, though no local data is provided here.
behavior: >-
  It lives out of sight. It is active mainly at dusk and at night, so it is rarely seen during the day. Its pupils are round. When threatened, it usually flees and does not seek out people. It lays eggs; across the species, it often lays 3–7 eggs at a time. In Armenia, it has been reported to emerge after winter in April, lay eggs in mid-June, and hatch in early September. These dates refer to Armenia and have not been established through observations in Georgia.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous. Its bite does not pose a significant health risk, but do not pick up a wild snake. Its spots may cause you to mistake it for a viper. First, look at its pupil and the shape of its head, then keep your distance.
conservation: >-
  In 2009, the species was assessed as being at low risk on the IUCN Red List. It is absent from Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Decree No. 190). The snakes listed there are the Caucasian viper, Dinnik’s viper and the lizard snake (formerly named Malpolon monspessulanus). In Armenia and Azerbaijan, the species is assessed as vulnerable at the national level. Some sources from neighboring countries also list it in Georgia’s older Red Book, although it is absent from the 2014 list. Information published in 2021 greatly expanded what is known about where it occurs. The IUCN’s assessment of a species’ risk also depends on how large an area it occupies. New records from the west therefore change that assessment. Locally, it faces threats from changes to the places where it lives and from being killed because it is mistaken for a venomous snake. No assessment of how its numbers are changing in Georgia is given here. Do not pick up a wild snake.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This is a nonvenomous, relatively slender snake with round pupils. Unlike the Aesculapian snake, it retains two rows of dark spots and a light line along its back into adulthood.
  traits:
    - >-
      Its pupil is round, unlike the vertical slit of a viper’s pupil. The head is moderately distinct from the body, and there is no horn on the nose. Scales on the front half of the back are smooth. Near the tail, the scales may have a slight raised ridge down the middle (in Azerbaijani specimens).
    - >-
      The subspecies that shares the species’ name has two lengthwise rows of yellowish-brown or brown spots on its back, with a narrow pale line between them. It often has a dark fork-shaped mark running from the head to the neck. A dark diagonal stripe runs from the eye to the corner of the mouth, and there is often a vertical dark line below the eye. Its belly is reddish or yellowish with gray spots. The spots stand out more clearly in young individuals; as they age, the background color darkens and the spots become less visible.
    - >-
      Older descriptions of the species often give its total length as about 65 cm. In Azerbaijan, adults have a body length of 61.5–78.1 cm and a tail length of 11.2–16.4 cm. Females are larger on average. This page does not provide separate measurements for Georgia.
    - >-
      Adult Aesculapian snakes (Zamenis longissimus) are often an almost uniform olive brown, while this species keeps its spots. The Aesculapian snake is more closely associated with damp, wooded places. The patterned snake (Elaphe dione) often has four stripes running along its body and is more closely associated with dry, steppe areas. The Urartian snake (Elaphe urartica) is larger and has big, rounded spots. The smooth snake (Coronella austriaca) is smaller, has smooth scales and a different pattern on its head. The spotted whip snake (Hemorrhois ravergieri) is faster and has a longer head. The nose-horned viper has vertical pupils, scales with a raised ridge down the middle, and a true horn on its nose. It is venomous.
    - >-
      The 2026 list includes it as a biologically confirmed species. The subspecies found in Georgia is Z. h. hohenackeri, whose name repeats the species name. It is often confused with the Aesculapian snake (Zamenis longissimus). Adult Aesculapian snakes are often more uniform in colour and are associated with wetter, wooded places. This species, however, keeps its spots.
facts:
  - >-
    Strauch described it in 1873 as Coluber hohenackeri. Type localities include
    Helenendorf/Elisabethpol (today Goygol and Ganja, Azerbaijan) and Tiflis
    (Tbilisi). Utiger et al. 2002 moved it to Zamenis. Older Georgian and Soviet
    books use Elaphe hohenackeri.
  - >-
    Only Z. h. hohenackeri occurs in Georgia. The wider range also includes
    Z. h. tauricus (southern Anatolia–Levant) and Z. h. lyciensis (south-western
    Türkiye; Hofmann et al. 2018). Those subspecies do not occur in Georgia.
  - >-
    Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat Georgian animals as genetically close to
    other Caucasus and eastern Türkiye populations. Barcode: CaBOL-ID 1027409,
    Gomaro.
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist rejects an unconfirmed Tbilisi mention of Elaphe situla;
    possible confusion with this species or with E. dione.
faq:
  - question: Does the Transcaucasian ratsnake occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. According to the 2026 list, biological evidence confirms its presence. The same list names the subspecies Z. h. hohenackeri for the country.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It is found in dry, rocky and grassy areas, more often in the east and the southern foothills. It is also found in suitable places in the west. The atlas map marks Imereti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Shida Kartli, Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. Named locations include Gomaro (Adigeni), Boshuri and, historically, Tbilisi.
  - question: Is it venomous?
    answer: >-
      No. This is a nonvenomous racer snake in the colubrid family. It is harmless to people. Do not pick it up.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Its pupils are round. Two rows of dark spots run along its back, with a pale line between them. A dark stripe runs from the eye to the corner of the mouth. The spots remain in adults. A viper has vertical pupils and often a horn on its nose.
  - question: How does it differ from the Aesculapian snake?
    answer: >-
      As an adult, the Aesculapian snake often has a more uniform color and is associated with wetter forests. The Transcaucasian snake keeps its spots into adulthood and prefers dry, rocky places. Neither is venomous.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Older descriptions often give a total length of about 65 cm. In Azerbaijan, adults have a body length of about 62–78 cm, plus a short tail. This page has no separate measurements for Georgia.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      General information about the species indicates that it eats small rodents and lizards, while young ones also eat insects. This page does not present a study of its diet conducted in Georgia.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      A 2009 assessment found that this species faces relatively little threat worldwide. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. In Armenia and Azerbaijan, it is classified as vulnerable.
