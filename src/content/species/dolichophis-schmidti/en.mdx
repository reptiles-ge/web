commonName: Red-bellied racer
location: Eastern Georgia
description: >-
  The Red-bellied racer is a large, fast snake with a red or orange belly. It is non-venomous, it is not a viper, and it lives mainly in dry parts of eastern Georgia.
overview: |-
  The red-bellied racer (Dolichophis schmidti) is a large, fast, non-venomous snake with a red or orange belly. Some are longer than 120 cm. In Georgia, it lives mainly in dry, rocky and grassy places in the east and is not considered widespread across the whole country. According to the 2017 global assessment, it faces a low risk of extinction. It poses no danger to human health.

  It belongs to the family Colubridae. It was once treated as a subspecies of Coluber jugularis, while the 2026 Georgian list includes it as a separate species. Still, it is not fully settled whether it truly is a separate species. Where it meets the Caspian whipsnake (Dolichophis caspius), the local groups of both snakes need further study. The genes of one snake found in Tbilisi have also been studied.
habitat: |-
  In Georgia, the red-bellied racer is found mainly in the dry, rocky and grassy areas of the eastern part of the country. It is considered a snake linked to dry environments. It often lives in dry grassy fields, rocky slopes, stony riverbanks, farm fields, gardens and vineyards. The humid Colchis area and damp places in high mountains are not its usual habitat.

  Its presence in Tbilisi was confirmed in recent years through genetic study. The 2026 Georgian checklist also includes photos of red-bellied racers found in Tbilisi. In Georgia, it has been recorded in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Tbilisi, Mtskheta-Mtianeti and Shida Kartli. This does not mean it is found everywhere in these regions or equally in every type of habitat.

  A separate 2026 analysis based on records of Georgia’s colubrid snakes showed the same for the red-bellied racer: a map made only from known records may leave out part of the area it actually occupies. For this reason, such [maps and regional lists](/snakes/gavrtseleba) show recorded places, not the full boundary of the species’ range.

  Its overall range extends from the Caucasus to Anatolia, northern Iran and Turkmenistan. According to available records, it has been found up to about 2,000 meters above sea level.

  The range boundaries of the red-bellied racer and the Caspian whipsnake (Dolichophis caspius) are not fully clear. One 2015 paper lists, within the known range of D. schmidti, a snake that looks like D. caspius. However, according to the 2026 Georgian checklist, this record needs additional confirmation.
diet: |-
  In Georgia, it mainly catches small mammals, other reptiles and birds. In other places where this snake occurs, it also eats lizards, rodents, birds and other snakes. It has no venom and usually catches its prey by chasing it quickly.
behavior: |-
  The red-bellied racer is active mainly during the day. In the warm season, it is more likely to be seen in dry, rocky places. As a rule, it lays about 5–11 eggs.

  In 2026, an unusually large winter group was found in Georgia. A reinforced-concrete septic tank held 133 red-bellied racers of different ages, along with one [Dahl's whip snake](platyceps-najadum) (Platyceps najadum) and one [dice snake](natrix-tessellata) (Natrix tessellata). This is the largest known winter group of this species. It is also the first confirmed case in the Caucasus of snakes of different species wintering together.

  The measured snakes were 82–158 cm long and weighed 82–753 g. Males and females were almost equal in number, with slightly more females. The snakes were gathered in the last section of the tank, where the temperature was about 11 °C. Because of construction work, they were moved to a safe place and released back into the wild in spring. This single case does not mean that the red-bellied racer always winters in such large groups or in similar man-made structures.

  When threatened, it usually tries to escape. If cornered, it may defend itself and bite. It is not venomous, but a bite from a large snake can be painful, so do not pick it up.
conservation: |-
  Worldwide, the Red-bellied racer does not face a high risk of extinction: according to the 2017 assessment, that risk is low. It is a widespread snake. It is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List, while the 2026 Georgian list still marks it as a candidate species. The accuracy of information on where it lives in Georgia is rated as moderate.

  In Georgia, it is threatened by changes to the places where it lives, deaths on roads and killing by people. It is killed especially often when people mistake it for a venomous snake. A winter shelter found in 2026 showed that a human-made structure can sometimes be an important shelter for it too. Construction work in such a place may harm the whole group wintering there or destroy the shelter. Do not pick up the snake and do not kill it.
interaction: |-
  The Red-bellied racer is not venomous and is not dangerous to human health. Still, do not pick it up: it is a large snake, it may bite you, and the bite can be painful. If a snake's pupil looks like a narrow vertical line, do not assume it is a Red-bellied racer, and keep a safe distance.

  traits:
    -  |-
      In Georgia, the red-bellied racer is [among the large snakes](/snakes/didi-gvelebi). Some are longer than 120 cm. Snakes of different ages that had gathered at one site in Georgia for the winter measured 82–158 cm.

      Its body is long and slender, and the head is clearly set apart from the neck. The eyes are relatively large, the pupils round and the scales on the back smooth. Do not pick the snake up to count its scales or look at it closely.
    -  |-
      The belly is red or orange, and this is where its Georgian name comes from. The Red-bellied racer can often be recognized by this very feature. Adults often have a plain back: copper-colored, reddish or reddish-brown. Young snakes, by contrast, have dark spots on the back. This is related to age and does not mean you are looking at a different species.
    -  |-
      The Caspian whip snake (Dolichophis caspius) looks especially similar to the red-bellied racer. Belly color is one of the features that help tell them apart. In the 2026 Georgian list, the Caspian whip snake remains a candidate.

      The [Dahl's whip snake](platyceps-najadum) (Platyceps najadum) is slimmer. It has dark spots with pale edges on its neck, and unlike an adult red-bellied racer, its back is not evenly reddish. The [spotted whip snake](hemorrhois-ravergieri) (Hemorrhois ravergieri) has sharp, diamond-shaped dark spots along its back.

      The [Urartian ratsnake](elaphe-urartica) (Elaphe urartica) has a thicker body, a relatively darker head and larger, more rounded spots on its back. The [eastern Montpellier snake](malpolon-insignitus) (Malpolon insignitus) has a raised, eyebrow-like spot above the eye. It is often olive or gray, and its belly is not red.
    -  |-
      [Vipers](/venomous-snakes) are heavier-bodied than the red-bellied racer and have vertical pupils. Their scales have a raised ridge down the middle. The [Levantine viper](macrovipera-lebetina) (Macrovipera lebetina) is a particularly large snake, with a broad head and a vertical pupil. Its venom is dangerous to health.

      The red-bellied racer has round pupils and is not venomous. Still, do not pick it up: if you disturb or catch it, it may bite, and the bite may be painful. [You cannot identify a snake species by pupil shape alone](/snakes/shxamiani-gvelis-amocnoba). But if a snake has vertical pupils, do not assume it is a red-bellied racer, and keep a safe distance.
    -  |-
      The 2026 Georgian list treats the red-bellied racer (Dolichophis schmidti) as a separate species, but the question is not fully settled yet. It was once considered a form of Coluber jugularis. Where it occurs together with the Caspian whipsnake (Dolichophis caspius), the local red-bellied racers need further study. A specimen known from Tbilisi has also had its genes studied. In Georgia it is found mainly in dry areas of the east, so do not assume it occurs across the whole country.
facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1909 (Nikolsky) as Coluber schmidti. The lectotype locality is
    the Mugan steppe (Azerbaijan). Older Georgian literature often used Coluber
    jugularis schmidti or C. j. erythrogaster. The modern genus Dolichophis
    follows a 2004 revision. The name is probably after the ichthyologist Petr
    Yulevich Schmidt.
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist leaves it a candidate: it belongs to the eastern group of
    the D. jugularis complex and is not fully isolated geographically from the
    nominotypical form. Mitochondrial difference from D. caspius is about 6–7%,
    but population-level study is still needed in the western Caspian contact
    zone. That does not change the field picture: in dry eastern Georgia the
    red-bellied form is what you actually meet.
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist COI barcode is from Tbilisi (CaBOL-ID 1027412;
    BOLD:AGI6555). Head photographs in the same paper are from Tbilisi.
  - >-
    In 2026 a winter aggregation of 133 individuals was documented in Georgia in
    a man-made structure — the largest recorded group of the species and the
    first interspecific snake hibernaculum in the Caucasus.
faq:
  - question: Is the red-bellied racer found in Georgia?
    answer: |-
      Yes. The red-bellied racer does live in Georgia. It is found mainly in dry places in the east and is not considered widespread across the whole country. In recent years it has also been seen in Tbilisi, and this was confirmed by a gene study. In the 2026 Georgian list it is still marked as a candidate species, meaning it is not yet fully settled whether it is a separate species. That question concerns its status, not whether it lives in Georgia.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: |-
      The red-bellied racer lives mostly in eastern Georgia, in dry, rocky and grassy places. It is found on dry grasslands and rocky slopes, in vineyards and near settlements. It has been seen in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Tbilisi, Mtskheta-Mtianeti and Shida Kartli. Wet places in western Georgia and the Colchian forests are not its usual habitat. A sighting in a region does not mean it lives everywhere there or is equally common throughout.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: |-
      No. The red-bellied racer is not venomous and is not dangerous to people. When threatened, it usually tries to escape. Still, it is a large snake, and if you catch or disturb it, it may give you a painful bite. So do not pick it up.

      If a snake's pupil is elongated from top to bottom, it is not a red-bellied racer. But pupil shape alone will not tell you for sure whether you are looking at a viper or a Levantine viper. So keep a safe distance.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: |-
      The red-bellied racer has a red or orange belly. It is a slender, fast snake with round pupils and smooth scales. Adults often have an entirely reddish back, while young ones show dark spots.

      Dahl's whip snake (Platyceps najadum) has dark spots with pale edges on its neck. Vipers, including the Levantine viper, have pupils stretched from top to bottom. If a snake has pupils like that, do not take it for a red-bellied racer.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: |-
      The red-bellied racer is one of Georgia’s large snakes. A snake longer than 120 cm is also known. In Georgia, snakes of different ages were measured at one site where they spent the winter. They were 82–158 cm long and weighed 82–753 g. These figures apply only to the snakes gathered at that one site and do not show the average size of this snake across Georgia.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: |-
      The Red-bellied racer mainly catches small mammals, reptiles and birds. It has no venom; it chases its prey at speed and catches it that way.
  - question: Does it lay eggs? When is it active?
    answer: |-
      The red-bellied racer lays eggs. About 5–11 eggs are often given for this species. In the warm part of the year, it is mostly active during the day. In Georgia, a large group of these snakes once spent the winter together in a human-made structure. This does not mean they always overwinter in such places.
  - question: What should I do if I meet one?
    answer: |-
      Watch the snake from a safe distance. If you want, take a photo and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up or try to catch it.

      If the pupil looks like a narrow vertical line, or if the head is broad and triangular, do not assume it is a red-bellied racer. It may be a viper or a Levantine viper. In that case, keep especially far away.
