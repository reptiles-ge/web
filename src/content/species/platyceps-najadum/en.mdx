overview: |-
  The slender whip snake (Platyceps najadum) is a nonvenomous, very slender snake. In Georgia, it is mainly associated with dry, rocky places in the east and south. It belongs to the family Colubridae.
  Adults are usually about 70–100 cm long and rarely exceed 1 metre. A 2026 Georgian list confirms the species’ presence; a modern specimen named in the list is from Tmogvi, in Aspindza Municipality.
  According to a 2021 Georgian study, the species is associated with a dry climate and is found mainly in rocky and grassy areas of eastern Georgia. It is classified as Least Concern.
habitat: |-
  In Georgia, the ochre-colored racer may be found on dry, rocky slopes, near cliffs, among shrubs, at forest edges, in dry meadows and near farmland. It shelters in cracks between rocks and hollows in cliffs. It has also been seen in a human-made structure in winter.
  According to a 2021 study, this species belongs to a group associated with dry climates. It is linked especially to rocky and grassy areas in the east. There is a recent record from Tmogvi. Older museum records also list Tbilisi and Borjomi.
  There are some older records from western Georgia, too, but old museum labels cannot provide an accurate map of where the species lives today. This page does not include specific recent records from Guria or Imereti.
  Across its wider range, the species has been recorded at elevations of up to about 2,000 m. Complete, separate data on the elevations where it occurs in Georgia are not available.
diet: >-
  The diet of the tawny racer in Georgia has not been studied separately. Elsewhere, this snake mainly hunts lizards, but it also catches small mammals, birds and other snakes. It does not use venom to catch its prey; it relies on speed.
behavior: |-
  In other parts of its range, the olive-colored racer is active during the day. It moves quickly and often warms itself in the sun. The hours when it is active in Georgia have not been studied separately.
  When threatened, it usually tries to escape. If cornered, it may raise the front of its body and open its mouth.
  It lays eggs. Accounts of this species often say that it lays 3–16 eggs, but no separate data have been published on when it mates or how many eggs it lays in Georgia.
  In 2026, one olive-colored racer was found in a shared winter shelter in Georgia with 133 red-bellied racers and one water snake. The shelter was in a reinforced-concrete septic tank. This is a single observation and does not mean that the species usually spends the winter with other snakes.
conservation: |-
  The IUCN classifies the reddish racer as Least Concern. It is not listed in Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
  European populations of this species are protected under Appendix II of the Bern Convention and Annex IV of the EU Habitats Directive. However, these EU rules do not automatically apply in Georgia.
  The species is confirmed in the 2026 Georgian list, while the accuracy of the information about its distribution is rated as moderate.
interaction: |-
  The olive-colored racer is not venomous and does not pose a medical danger to people. Do not pick one up — a large snake may bite in self-defense, and the bite can be painful.
  If the black part of a snake’s eye is vertically elongated, it is not an olive-colored racer. Watch an unfamiliar snake from a safe distance, leave it a way through, and take photos from afar.

Around the middle of its body, the scales are usually arranged in 19 rows, rarely 17. Do not pick up the snake to count its scales.

facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1831 (Eichwald), originally as Tyria najadum; the type
    locality is Baku, not Georgia. Older literature often used Coluber najadum
    or Zamenis dahlii. The modern genus Platyceps became standard after Schätti
    & Monsch 2004. Caucasian animals belong to the nominotypical subspecies
    (P. n. najadum).
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist specimen and COI barcode are from Tmogvi (Aspindza;
    CaBOL-ID 1027410). The same paper notes about 6.65% COI difference between
    Georgian and Armenian sequences — a reason to revisit taxonomy, not a
    completed species split.
  - >-
    The Georgian name reflects the olive–buckthorn dorsal tone. The English
    name whip snake refers to the extremely slender, whip-like body. Neck
    ocelli are the main field mark.

  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to humans?
    answer: |-
      No. The rust-colored racer is not venomous and poses no threat to human health. When threatened, it usually tries to escape, though it may bite if picked up.
      If a snake has a vertical pupil, it is not a rust-colored racer. In that case, do not approach it to identify the species. Give the snake room to move away.
  - question: Is Dahl’s whip snake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The olive-colored racer is listed as a confirmed species in the 2026 Georgian checklist. A modern record gives the area around Tmogvi as a place where it was seen. It is not found equally throughout Georgia.
  - question: Where might I encounter it in Georgia?
    answer: |-
      The olive-colored racer is mainly associated with dry, rocky or grassy places in eastern and southern Georgia. A modern record mentions the area around Tmogvi in Samtskhe–Javakheti.
      Older records also mention Tbilisi and Borjomi. There are older museum records from western Georgia, but they do not give a precise picture of where the species occurs today. This page does not present specific modern records from Guria or Imereti.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: |-
      To identify it, look for a very slender, whip-like body, round pupils, smooth scales, and dark spots with pale edges on the neck and front of the body. The back part of the body is often almost one color.
      The multicolored racer has spots along a much larger part of its back. A snake with vertical pupils is not the ochre-colored racer. Even so, do not rely on just one feature to identify an unfamiliar snake.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: |-

      The sources used for this page do not give a separately confirmed maximum length for Georgia.
