overview: >-
  The meadow lizard (Darevskia praticola) is a harmless, ground-dwelling lizard
  of eastern and central Georgia. It belongs to the true lizard family
  (Lacertidae). It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. The 2026
  Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed, not as a candidate
  species. Georgian populations here belong to the nominotypical lineage
  (D. p. praticola). Pontic populations of the northwest are treated separately
  in this atlas as the Pontic meadow lizard (Darevskia pontica), a candidate
  species. Snout–vent length at species level is usually under 6.5 cm. IUCN
  assessed it as Near Threatened in 2009. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
  Do not assume it throughout the country: humid Colchis and the Black Sea
  coast are pontica in this account, not this species.
habitat: >-
  It occurs in Georgia. Look for it at deciduous forest edges, clearings, grassy
  openings, and woodland meadows — on the ground and in leaf litter, not on
  rock faces. Most Darevskia live on rocks; this species and the Artvin lizard
  (D. derjugini) are exceptions. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 assign Georgian
  populations to eastern and central Georgia. Named recent points are Sioni
  Reservoir (head-scalation figure) and Sioni–Tushurebi (COI barcodes; Tushurebi
  is in Tianeti Municipality). Distribution confidence is medium. This atlas map
  marks Mtskheta — Mtianeti from those records; that does not mean the species
  is limited to two points. Older material indicates a wider eastern–central
  range: Lagodekhi (ZISP, 1980, Darevsky), the Tsiv-Gombori Ridge, the Surami
  Pass (ZISP, 1963, Shida Kartli), and the Kartli ridges (Muskhelishvili
  1964–1970; Tuniyev et al. 2013). Those are historical and museum records;
  present status at every site is not re-checked on this page. Whether Armenian
  loriensis reaches adjacent eastern Georgia is, according to Tuniyev et al.
  2013, still unclear — do not treat it as confirmed here. An exact Georgian
  elevational limit is not given in the checklist. In Armenia the nominotypical
  complex is reported from about 600–2,000 m in the forest belt; that is an
  Armenian interval, not a measured Georgian range. Do not search for this
  species on the Colchic lowland or the Black Sea coast.
diet: >-
  This page has no Georgian stomach-content study. In Bulgaria it eats mainly
  arthropods: often spiders, beetles, and leafhoppers; it usually avoids ants
  (Vacheva & Naumov 2022). That is a Balkan menu, not a list for Georgian sites.
  It seizes prey in the mouth; it has no venom.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. It moves on the ground and basks at forest edges and in
  grass; in Serbian material activity may stop on very hot days as animals seek
  shade (Ćorović & Crnobrnja-Isailović 2018). That is a Balkan observation, not
  a Georgian hourly table. It lays eggs. In Serbia mating is in April–May,
  eggs are laid in June, one clutch a season, often 4–8 eggs (Ljubisavljević et
  al. 2008). In the Hyrcanian subspecies (Azerbaijan) clutches are 1–6 eggs.
  Neither calendar is measured for Georgian populations. If threatened it runs;
  if grabbed, the tail may break and the lizard may bite. Watch it and leave it
  where it is. Do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assessed it as Near Threatened in 2009 (Agasyan and co-authors) because
  of a fragmented range. That assessment is old and covers the wide, disjunct
  species range, not a separate Georgian population trend. It is not on Georgia’s
  2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190). Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat it as
  biologically confirmed. Freitas et al. 2016 recover three clades (Balkans;
  western Greater Caucasus; Transcaucasia) and do not fully support the
  morphology-based subspecies. Rank of pontica (species vs subspecies) is
  inconsistent in the literature; the checklist leaves it as a candidate because
  effective genetic isolation is not shown. Literature names forest-edge
  clearance and fragmentation as local threats. A Georgian population trend is
  not measured here. It is not listed on CITES. Do not take wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and poses no medical risk. If handled it may bite; the tail
  may break. Leave it where it is. Do not keep wild animals.

  summary: >-
    A small ground lizard with an unflattened head, at forest edges and meadows.
    Colour is not enough among Darevskia — use habitat, head shape, and range.
  traits:
    - >-
      It lives on the ground, not on rock faces. The head is less flattened than
      in rock Darevskia. The back is often brown or olive-brown; a pale stripe
      often runs along each side of the back. That is a typical look and does
      not separate species by colour.
    - >-
      Snout–vent length at species level is usually under 6.5 cm. The measured
      East Georgian series is small (Tuniyev et al. 2013: a few adults); a full
      Georgian morphometric series is not on this page. Females are often
      slightly larger than males. Do not catch the lizard to count scales.
    - >-
      The Pontic meadow lizard (D. pontica) in this atlas is the northwestern
      Pontic populations. The type locality is Gagra. Colour will not tell them
      apart; range is more reliable than dorsal pattern. The Artvin lizard
      (D. derjugini) is also terrestrial but a different forest lineage and is
      more widespread in the west; it is also barcoded at Sioni — they can
      co-occur, and colour does not separate them. The sand lizard (Lacerta
      agilis) is larger, has a different pattern, and is another genus. Rock
      Darevskia may be in the same valley but are on rock, with a flatter head.
    - >-
      Described in 1834 (Eversmann) as Lacerta praticola. The type locality is
      in the North Caucasus (Kislovodsk / Narzan), not in Georgia. The genus
      Darevskia dates from 1997. Checklist figures show head scalation and
      preanal plates (Sioni Reservoir).
    - >-
      The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed, not as
      a candidate species. Georgian populations belong to eastern and central
      Georgia. Humid Colchis and the Black Sea coast are pontica in this
      account, not this species. Colour does not separate the Pontic meadow
      lizard (D. pontica); range is more reliable than dorsal pattern.

faq:
  - question: Is the meadow lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. Georgian
      populations belong to eastern and central Georgia. It is not nationwide.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      At forest edges and grassy clearings in eastern–central Georgia. Named
      recent points are Sioni and Tushurebi (Tianeti). Literature also names
      Lagodekhi, Tsiv-Gombori, and the Surami Pass. Do not search the Colchic
      coast.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. If grabbed it may bite; the tail may break. Leave it
      where it is.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A small ground lizard with an unflattened head, at forest edges. Colour is
      not enough. A flattened head on rock is another Darevskia. In the
      northwest it may be pontica; in the same forest it may be derjugini.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Snout–vent length is usually under 6.5 cm. A full Georgian measurement
      series is not on this page.
  - question: Is it the same as the Pontic meadow lizard?
    answer: >-
      Not in this atlas. pontica is the northwestern Pontic populations and a
      candidate species. Genetic isolation is not fully demonstrated; rank
      varies in the literature. Colour will not tell them apart.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia. A Balkan study found
      mainly spiders, beetles, and other small invertebrates.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      By day, in the warm season. A full Georgian calendar is not on this page.
      In the Balkans it is described from spring into late summer.
