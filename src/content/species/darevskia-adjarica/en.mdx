commonName: "Ajarian Rock Lizard"
location: "Adjara (Shuakhevi) and Abastumani"
description: >-
  A harmless rock lizard of Adjara and Abastumani. Colour is not enough to
  identify it.
overview: >-
  The Ajarian rock lizard (Darevskia adjarica) occurs in Georgia as a harmless
  rock lizard of Adjara and the Abastumani area. It belongs to the true lizard
  family (Lacertidae). It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. Other
  English names include Adjarian rock lizard; red-bellied lizard is the older
  name for the whole D. parvula complex. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it
  as a candidate species: it was long a geographic subspecies of D. parvula.
  Arribas and co-authors (2018, 2022) treat it as a full species, separate from
  Turkish D. parvula and from D. tuniyevi. The checklist still asks for
  population-level study of the contact zone, so free gene flow is not yet
  ruled out. This atlas follows the checklist rank: candidate, not confirmed.
  The type locality is in Georgia — Abastumani, at about 1,400 m. Do not assume
  it throughout the country. Named modern points are Shuakhevi and Abastumani;
  a close sequence from Ajaristskali is deposited as D. parvula. The checklist
  text sometimes spells ajarica — that is the same name, not another taxon.
habitat: >-
  It occurs in Georgia. Do not assume it on the dry eastern plains or along the
  whole Greater Caucasus. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 summarise the range as
  southwestern Georgia; distribution confidence is medium. The head-scale
  figure and COI barcodes (CaBOL 1027440, 1007137–1007139) are from Shuakhevi.
  A 2019 voucher from the same area is at about 600 m (Arribas et al. 2022).
  The type locality is Abastumani (about 1,400 m); a 2019 voucher is from the
  same area at about 1,300 m. The Ajaristskali sequence (NCBI MN015094,
  deposited as D. parvula) matches the Shuakhevi barcodes at 99.21% and is
  tied to this taxon in the checklist. This atlas map marks Adjara from the
  Shuakhevi records and Samtskhe — Javakheti from the Abastumani type and 2019
  voucher. That does not mean the species is limited to two points, and it does
  not mean every suitable valley has been surveyed. The 1980 description is
  broader: the Black Sea coast from Trabzon to Batumi, the Chorokh below
  Borçka, Adjara, and Georgia between Meria (southeast of Poti) and Borjomi,
  plus an isolate near Bakuriani. Guria, Imereti and Bakuriani are not on this
  atlas map for now — that is historical literature, not a 2026 named barcode.
  Habitat is rock, stone and crevices in wooded valleys, often near a stream —
  not open steppe and not the forest floor where derjugini lives. In Turkish
  material the then-subspecies D. p. adjarica was taken in forest, near
  streams, by day (Ilgaz 2009); that is Turkish habitat, not a Georgian
  elevational table. Named Georgian points on this page run from about 600 m
  to 1,400 m.
diet: >-
  This page has no Georgian stomach-content study. As in other rock Darevskia,
  small invertebrates — insects, spiders and similar prey — are expected. It
  has no venom; it seizes prey in the jaws.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. It is a rock lizard: it basks on stones and hides in
  crevices. A Georgian hourly calendar is not on this page. In Turkish samples
  the then-subspecies D. p. adjarica was taken between 08:00 and 15:30, in
  sunny or cloudy weather (Ilgaz 2009) — that is a Turkish timetable. The 2019
  Shuakhevi and Abastumani samples were taken on 8 September: animals were
  still on the surface in late summer. It lays eggs. Clutch size for Georgian
  populations is not on this page. If grabbed, the tail may break and the
  lizard may bite. Watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 list it as a candidate. Species rank relative to
  D. parvula still needs contact-zone evidence. The checklist cites a
  cytochrome b difference of up to 3.7% between Georgian material and some
  northeastern Turkish D. parvula sequences; a 2018 paper reported about 14.4%
  between the two taxa. The figures are not interchangeable: sampling and the
  2022 split of D. tuniyevi differ. The practical point is the same — rank is
  not settled for Georgia. IUCN assessed D. parvula as Least Concern in 2009
  (Tuniyev and co-authors), then including this taxon. D. adjarica has no
  separate IUCN assessment. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Resolution
  No. 190). A Georgian population trend is not measured here. It is not listed
  on CITES. Do not take wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and poses no health risk. If you pick it up, it may bite, and its tail may break off. Leave it where it is. Do not keep a wild animal.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a medium-sized rock lizard found in southwestern Georgia. Color alone is not enough to identify it. To distinguish it from other Darevskia species in the wild, consider its scales, the type of place it lives in, and the areas where it is found.
  traits:
    - >-
      This rock-dwelling Darevskia has well-developed legs and a relatively flat head. It is found on rocks and in crevices, unlike the forest-floor derjugini and the large, ground-dwelling Lacerta. Its body is stockier and more rounded than that of D. parvula. The middle of its back often stands out clearly from the dark stripes that pass through the temple area. A pale stripe or a row of whitish spots is often visible along the edges of these dark stripes. Adult males often have orange or brick-red bellies; a blue ring near the armpit is seen rarely. Any greenish tint comes from a sheen, not a separate pigment. These are features of its appearance, and color alone cannot be used to tell the species apart.
    - >-
      Head and body length is often about 5–5.5 cm. In one study, it was about 4.2–6.2 cm among the animals examined. Most were from Turkey, with others from Abastumani and Shuakhevi. This page does not give separate measurements for the specimens from Georgia. The tail is usually longer than the body. Total length is not given in the table.
    - >-
      The sixth scale under the jaw can help tell them apart. According to the original description, it is triangular with outward-curving edges in about 95% of cases, while this scale is often flat in D. parvula. In about half of cases, there is one large scale in front of the scale by the rear opening; in the remaining cases, there are two. The images from Shuakhevi in the list show the scales on the head and those in front of the rear opening, rather than the animal’s coloring. These features are hard to make out in the wild without a magnifying glass.
    - >-
      Clark’s rock lizard (D. clarkorum) is also in Adjara, in Charnali Gorge —
      a different lineage, confirmed in the checklist. Ground-dwelling
      D. derjugini may share a valley but lives on the forest floor and has a
      rounder head. The mixed rock lizard (D. mixta) is endemic to western
      Georgia and absent from Adjara; the old English name “Ajarian lizard”
      belonged to mixta and should not be used for this taxon. True D. parvula
      is not on the Georgian checklist. D. tuniyevi is Turkish; a narrow strip
      toward the Ajaristskali is mentioned as possible and is not confirmed in
      Georgia. Colour will not tell them apart.
    - >-
      The 2026 Georgian list includes it only as a possible species, not a confirmed one. For a long time, it was considered a local subspecies of D. parvula. The species was described from a lizard found in Abastumani, Georgia. Recent accounts name Shuakhevi and Abastumani as places where it has been found. This does not mean it occurs throughout Georgia. D. parvula itself is not included in Georgia’s species list. The Colchian rock lizard (D. mixta) does not occur in Adjara.
faq:
  - question: Is the Ajarian rock lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is a candidate species in the 2026 checklist. Type locality:
      Abastumani. Barcodes and the head figure: Shuakhevi.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Named modern points are Shuakhevi (Adjara, about 600 m) and Abastumani
      (about 1,300–1,400 m). Look on rock and stone in wooded valleys. Do not
      assume it throughout Georgia.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      It is a medium-sized rock lizard with a dark, contrasted pattern and
      often an orange or brick-red belly. Colour is not enough. Compare it
      with clarkorum at Charnali, forest-floor derjugini, and do not confuse
      it with mixta, which is absent from Adjara.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. If grabbed it may bite; the tail may break. Leave
      it where it is.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Snout–vent length is often about 5–5.5 cm; about 4.2–6.2 cm in the
      measured sample. A Georgian-only series is not on this page.
  - question: Why is it a candidate species?
    answer: >-
      The checklist treated it as a former subspecies of D. parvula and the
      contact zone has not been studied at population level. Arribas and
      co-authors treat it as a full species. This atlas keeps the checklist
      candidate rank.
  - question: Is this D. mixta or D. parvula?
    answer: >-
      mixta is absent from Adjara. D. parvula is not on the Georgian
      checklist — it is a Turkish taxon. An older Ajaristskali sequence is
      deposited as parvula but almost matches the Shuakhevi barcodes.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      By day. A full Georgian seasonal calendar is not on this page. The
      Shuakhevi and Abastumani sample is from early September.
