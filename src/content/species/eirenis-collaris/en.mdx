commonName: "Collared Dwarf Snake"
location: "Southeastern Georgia"
description: >-
  The Collared Dwarf Snake is a small, non-venomous snake with a dark collar on its neck. It is confirmed in southeastern Georgia, at Taribana in Dedoplistskaro Municipality.
overview: >-
  The Collared Dwarf Snake (Eirenis collaris) is a small snake with a dark collar on its neck. It has no venom and is harmless to people. Adults are usually under 40 cm long, and the longest one ever measured was about 40 cm. It is found in southeastern Georgia. The 2026 Georgian list treats its presence in the country as confirmed, and the snake mentioned in that list comes from Taribana in Dedoplistskaro Municipality. It belongs to the colubrid family (Colubridae), and the subspecies found in Georgia is E. c. collaris. Globally, its risk of extinction is low. It is often confused with the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake (Eirenis modestus), which the same list links to eastern and central Georgia. Do not expect to find the Collared Dwarf Snake in every part of Georgia.
habitat: |-
  The Collared Dwarf Snake lives in dry, stony or semi-desert places. It is found on soft soil with stones and sand, and on grassy slopes in oak woods. It is often found under a stone, under a mound of earth or in a crevice, sometimes together with the European Blind Snake.

  According to 2026 data, in Georgia it occurs in the southeast. The atlas map counts only Kakheti as range, from enough field records (Dedoplistskaro and Sagarejo municipalities); that does not mean it is even across the whole region. Kvemo Kartli (Gardabani Municipality) has only a few records, so the map does not count that region as range. One specimen was found at Taribana in Dedoplistskaro Municipality, and photos were taken in the Yagluja Hills. Both places lie in lowlands and foothills. Information on its distribution is moderately reliable.

  The species lives in eastern Turkey, the South Caucasus, Dagestan, Iraq and western Iran. It is known from elevations of up to about 1,700 m above sea level, but this figure does not come from Georgia. The species was first described from specimens collected at Beshbarmag. That place is on the Caspian coast of Azerbaijan, not in Georgia.
diet: >-
  The Collared Dwarf Snake mainly eats animals without backbones that live under stones. Its reported prey includes scorpions, centipedes, worms, beetles and other insects, as well as spiders and small lizards. It has no venom and catches its prey with its mouth.
behavior: |-
  It lives a hidden life and, in the heat of the day, often stays under stones or beneath the soil. Its photos were taken in May and July. According to published information on the species, it lays 4–8 eggs in summer. The young hatch in August–September and are about 10–13 cm long. There are reports that snakes of this species spend the winter underground, including in groups.

  When threatened, it mostly flees. In the same dry southeast, you may come across the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake, the Smooth snake, the European Blind Snake and, rarely, the European cat snake. These snakes can be told apart by their pupils, size and neck markings. The Collared Dwarf Snake has no venom, but even a small snake may bite you, so do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  Globally, the Collared Dwarf Snake is assessed as being at low risk of extinction. It has a wide range, and no specific global threat has been singled out for it. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List: among snakes, that list includes Dinnik’s viper and the Caucasus viper. The 2026 list confirms its presence in Georgia, although information about its distribution is rated as moderately precise. Taribana is the only specific place named in the country; the southeast is a general indication, not complete information about its range. Locally, it faces two threats: changes to its habitat, and people killing it after mistaking it for a venomous snake. Do not pick it up.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small, slender, nonvenomous snake with a dark collar around its neck. Its back is olive or light brown, and its pupils are round. It is neither a viper nor the worm-like European Blind Snake. People often confuse it with the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake, but in adult Ring-headed Dwarf Snakes the neck band often fades.
  traits:
    - >-
      Its body is slender and short, so it looks more like a small snake than a large racer. Adults are usually under 40 cm long, and the longest one measured was about 40 cm. The head barely stands out from the neck, the tip of the snout is rounded and the pupil is round. The scales on its back are smooth: around the middle of the body there are often 15 rows, sometimes 15–17. The Ring-headed Dwarf Snake often has 17–18 rows there. Even so, do not pick up a snake to count its scales.
    - >-
      A dark, collar-like band on the neck narrows toward the underside. This collar is where its Georgian name comes from. The back is olive or light brown, and each scale often has a pale center and a dark edge. Sometimes a faint band runs across the head, with two spots on the back of the head. The belly is yellowish white. The Turkish subspecies E. c. macrospilotus has a row of dark spots on its back. In Georgia, the subspecies E. c. collaris occurs.
    - >-
      The Ring-headed Dwarf Snake (Eirenis modestus) looks like the Collared Dwarf Snake but is larger, though it is usually under 70 cm long. In adult Ring-headed Dwarf Snakes, the collar-like band on the neck often fades. Their ranges differ too: the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake is linked to eastern and central Georgia, the Collared Dwarf Snake to the southeast (Dedoplistskaro, Taribana). The Smooth snake (Coronella austriaca) has a thicker body, with two rows of small spots running along its back. It is more often found at forest edges and in damp places. The European Blind Snake (Xerotyphlops vermicularis) looks like a worm and has small eyes. The European cat snake (Telescopus fallax), by contrast, has vertical pupils.
    - >-
      It is a nonvenomous snake with round pupils. A pupil shaped like a vertical slit points to a viper or a Levantine viper. If you come across one, keep your distance and do not pick it up. Even a small nonvenomous snake is easily injured, and an unfamiliar snake may turn out to be venomous.
    - >-
      In Georgia, it is found in the southeast, not across the whole country. The 2026 list includes it as a confirmed species, and the snake mentioned there comes from Taribana in Dedoplistskaro Municipality. The Georgian subspecies repeats the species name: E. c. collaris. The Collared Dwarf Snake is often confused with the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake (Eirenis modestus), but the list links the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake to eastern and central Georgia.

    answer: >-

The similar Ring-headed Dwarf Snake has this band too, but in adults it is often fainter. The Ring-headed Dwarf Snake has been seen in the east-central part. The European Blind Snake, on the other hand, looks like a worm. Do not pick the snake up to count its scales.

    answer: >-

  - question: What does it eat, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      Based on what is known about the species, it lays 4–8 eggs in summer. By the same accounts, it eats mainly insects and other animals without backbones that live under rocks. Occasionally it also feeds on a small lizard.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      In the warm season. At that time you may come across it in dry places, under stones. Photos of it were taken in May and July.
  - question: How does it differ from the ring-headed dwarf snake?
    answer: >-
      The main difference is the collar: the Collared Dwarf Snake shows it more clearly. Both are small, nonvenomous snakes of the genus Eirenis, but the collared one is usually shorter than the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake. Their ranges differ too: the Collared Dwarf Snake occurs in southeastern Georgia, while the Ring-headed Dwarf Snake is linked to eastern and central Georgia. Around the middle of the body, the collared one often has 15 rows of scales, the ring-headed one 17–18. Still, do not pick up a snake to count its scales.
