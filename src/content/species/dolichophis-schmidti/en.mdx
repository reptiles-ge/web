commonName: Red-bellied racer
location: Eastern Georgia
description: >-
  A fast, non-venomous racer of dry eastern Georgia. The belly is red or orange;
  it is not a viper.
overview: |-
  The red-bellied racer (Dolichophis schmidti) is a large, fast, nonvenomous snake. In Georgia, it is found mainly in dry, rocky and grassy places in the east. This does not mean it occurs throughout the country. It belongs to the snake family Colubridae.

  It is still not entirely clear whether it should be considered a separate species. A 2026 Georgian list includes Dolichophis schmidti as a separate species, but its status still needs clarification. It was previously considered a subspecies of Coluber jugularis. More study of the local groups of these snakes is needed where the red-bellied racer meets the Caspian whipsnake (Dolichophis caspius). A specimen from Tbilisi has also been studied genetically.

  The longest snake described was over 120 cm long. In Georgia, snakes gathered in one place during winter were measured at 82–158 cm long.

  It poses no medical danger to people. According to a 2017 assessment, the species faces a low risk of extinction worldwide (Least Concern).
habitat: |-
  In Georgia, the red-bellied racer is found mainly in dry, rocky and grassy places in the east. It is considered a species associated with dry climates. Typical places for it include dry grasslands, rocky slopes, stony riverbanks, farm fields, gardens and vineyards. The humid Colchis region and damp places in the high mountains are not typical for it.

  A recent record from Tbilisi was confirmed by studying its genes. A 2026 Georgian checklist also includes photos of red-bellied racers found in Tbilisi. Its presence has been confirmed in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Tbilisi, Mtskheta-Mtianeti and Shida Kartli. This does not mean it is found equally often in every kind of place within these regions.

  Its wider range extends from the Caucasus to Anatolia, northern Iran and Turkmenistan. Reports place it at elevations up to about 2,000 metres above sea level, but separate information on the elevations where it occurs in Georgia is not available here.

  The boundaries between the ranges of the red-bellied and Caspian racers (Dolichophis caspius) are not fully clear. A 2015 paper mentions a snake within the known range of D. schmidti that looks like D. caspius. According to a 2026 Georgian checklist, this record needs further confirmation.
diet: |-
  The diet of the red-bellied racer has not been separately and fully studied in Georgia. The 2021 Georgian review places it among snakes that mainly catch small mammals, reptiles, and birds.

  In other parts of its range, its prey includes lizards, rodents, birds, and other snakes. It has no venom and catches prey mainly through speed and active pursuit.
behavior: |-
  The red-bellied racer is mainly active by day. The 2021 Georgian review does not list it among nocturnal species. Its hourly or seasonal activity calendar has not been described separately in Georgia, although encounters are more likely in warm seasons in dry, rocky places.

  When threatened it mostly tries to flee, but if cornered it may defend itself and bite. It has no venom, although the bite of a large individual may be painful, so do not pick it up.

  The species lays eggs. The literature usually mentions about 5–11 eggs, although separate data for the Georgian population are not published here.

  In 2026 an unusually large winter aggregation was recorded in Georgia: 133 red-bellied racers of different ages were wintering in a reinforced-concrete septic tank. They were found together with one Dahl's whip snake (Platyceps najadum) and one dice snake (Natrix tessellata). This case is described as the largest known winter aggregation for the species and the first documented case in the Caucasus of different snake species wintering together.

  Snakes measured in the aggregation were 82–158 cm long and weighed 82–753 g. The numbers of males and females were almost equal, with a slight female bias. The snakes were gathered in the final chamber of the tank, where the temperature was about 11 °C. Because of construction work, they were moved to a safe place and released in spring.

  This case does not mean that the red-bellied racer always winters in such large groups or in similar artificial structures.
conservation: |-
  According to the 2017 IUCN assessment, the red-bellied racer is globally listed as Least Concern. Its range is broad. The species is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red List.

  In the 2026 Georgian checklist, the red-bellied racer is left as a candidate species, and the confidence of its distribution in Georgia is assessed as medium.

  Local threats include habitat transformation, road mortality, and killing by people, especially when it is confused with a venomous snake. The winter shelter found in 2026 showed that a human-made structure can sometimes become an important refuge. In such cases, construction work can harm an entire wintering group or destroy the shelter.

  Do not pick up or kill the snake.
interaction: |-
  It has no venom and poses no risk to human health. Even so, do not pick it up: a large one may bite, and the bite may be painful. If its pupil looks like a vertical slit, do not assume it is this species. Keep a safe distance.

  summary: |-
    This is a nonvenomous, slender, fast-moving snake with round pupils. Its red or orange belly is one of its main identifying features. The red-bellied racer is not a viper and should not be mistaken for a gyurza.
  traits:
    -  |-
      It has a long, slender body and a head clearly set apart from its neck. Its eyes are relatively large, with round pupils. The scales on its back are smooth.

      The red-bellied racer is among the larger snakes in Georgia. Some are longer than 120 cm. Snakes of different ages measured at one place where they had gathered in Georgia during winter were 82–158 cm long.

      Do not pick up a snake to count its scales or examine it closely.
    -  |-
      Adults often have a back that is a single color: copper, reddish or reddish-brown. Young ones have dark spots on their backs. This difference comes with age and does not mean they belong to another species.

      The belly is red or orange. This is one of the main ways to recognize the red-bellied racer, and it is also where its Georgian name comes from.
    -  |-
      The olive-colored racer (Platyceps najadum) is thinner and more slender. It has dark spots with pale edges on its neck. Unlike an adult red-bellied racer, its back is not uniformly reddish.

      The variegated racer (Hemorrhois ravergieri) has distinct, dark diamond-shaped spots along its back.

      The Urartu racer (Elaphe urartica) has a thicker body and a relatively darker head. The spots on its back are larger and rounder.

      The lizard-eating snake (Malpolon insignitus) has a raised ridge above the eye that looks like an eyebrow. It is often olive-colored or gray and does not have a red belly.

      The Caspian racer (Dolichophis caspius) looks especially similar to the red-bellied racer. It remains listed as a candidate in the 2026 Georgian list. Belly color is one way to tell them apart. It does not yet have its own public page in this atlas.
    -  |-
      Vipers differ from the red-bellied racer in their stockier bodies, vertical pupils, and scales with a raised ridge down the middle. The gyurza (Macrovipera lebetina) is especially heavy-bodied, with a broad head and vertical pupils. Its venom is dangerous to health.

      The red-bellied racer has round pupils and is not venomous. If a snake has vertical pupils, do not assume it is a red-bellied racer, and keep a safe distance. You cannot identify a snake’s species by its pupils alone.

      Do not pick it up. If disturbed or caught, it may bite, and the bite may be painful.
    -  |-

It was previously considered a subspecies of Coluber jugularis. Red-bellied racers living in areas where both they and the Caspian racer (Dolichophis caspius) are found need further study.

      A specimen from Tbilisi has also had its genes studied. In Georgia, the species is found mainly in dry areas of the east, so its presence across the whole country should not be assumed.
facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1909 (Nikolsky) as Coluber schmidti. The lectotype locality is
    the Mugan steppe (Azerbaijan). Older Georgian literature often used Coluber
    jugularis schmidti or C. j. erythrogaster. The modern genus Dolichophis
    follows Nagy et al. 2004. The name is probably after the ichthyologist Petr
    Yulevich Schmidt.
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist leaves it a candidate: it belongs to the eastern group of
    the D. jugularis complex and is not fully isolated geographically from the
    nominotypical form. Mitochondrial difference from D. caspius is about 6–7%,
    but population-level study is still needed in the western Caspian contact
    zone. That does not change the field picture: in dry eastern Georgia the
    red-bellied form is what you actually meet.
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist COI barcode is from Tbilisi (CaBOL-ID 1027412;
    BOLD:AGI6555). Head photographs in the same paper are from Tbilisi.
  - >-
    In 2026 a winter aggregation of 133 individuals was documented in Georgia in
    a man-made structure — the largest recorded group of the species and the
    first interspecific snake hibernaculum in the Caucasus.
faq:
  - question: Is the red-bellied racer found in Georgia?
    answer: |-
      Yes. The red-bellied racer is confirmed in Georgia, although the 2026 Georgian checklist still leaves its status as candidate. A modern genetically confirmed record is from Tbilisi. The species is mainly linked to dry zones of eastern Georgia and is not considered widespread across the whole country.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: |-
      In Georgia the red-bellied racer is found mainly in dry, rocky, and grassy places in the east — steppes, rocky slopes, vineyards, and near settlements. In this page's records table, distribution is confirmed in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Tbilisi, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, and Shida Kartli.

      The humid zones of western Georgia and Colchic forests are not typical habitat for it. A confirmed region does not mean the species is equally widespread everywhere inside it.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: |-
      No. The red-bellied racer is not venomous and does not pose a medical threat to people. When threatened it usually tries to flee, although a large individual can bite painfully if caught or disturbed, so do not pick it up.

      If a snake has a vertical pupil, it is not a red-bellied racer. You cannot identify a viper or Levantine viper precisely by pupil shape alone, so keep a safe distance.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: |-
      The red-bellied racer has a slender and fast body, round pupil, smooth scales, and a red or orange belly. Adults often have a fairly uniform reddish back, while juveniles show dark blotches.

      Dahl's whip snake (Platyceps najadum) has characteristic dark, pale-edged blotches on the neck. Levantine viper and other vipers have vertical pupils, so a snake with that feature should not be treated as a red-bellied racer.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: |-
      The red-bellied racer is one of Georgia's large snakes. Its maximum length in the literature exceeds 120 cm.

      Individuals of different ages measured in one winter shelter in Georgia were 82–158 cm long and weighed 82–753 g. These data come from only one aggregation and do not represent the average size of the country's population.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: |-
      The red-bellied racer's diet in Georgia has not been fully studied. According to available information, it mainly catches small mammals, reptiles and birds. It has no venom and catches its prey by chasing it down quickly.
  - question: Does it lay eggs? When is it active?
    answer: |-
      The red-bellied racer lays eggs. Accounts of this species often mention about 5–11 eggs, but no separate figures on how many eggs the snakes living in Georgia lay have been published here.

      During the warmer part of the year, it is mostly active during the day. In Georgia, a large group of these snakes was recorded spending the winter together in a structure built by people. This does not mean the species always spends the winter in such places.
  - question: What should I do if I meet one?
    answer: |-
      Watch the snake from a safe distance. Take a photo if you wish, and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up or try to catch it.

      If its pupil is vertical or its head looks broad and triangular, do not assume it is a red-bellied racer—it may be a viper or a gyurza. In that case, stay well back.
