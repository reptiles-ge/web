description: >-
  The Northern Banded Newt is the only banded newt in Georgia. It is not venomous and lives in mountain forests and by high-mountain lakes. Older books call it the Asia Minor newt.
overview: >-
  The northern banded newt (Ommatotriton ophryticus) is the only banded newt in Georgia. It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. Unlike frogs, it is a tailed amphibian (Caudata). It lives in mountain forests and above the tree line, by mountain lakes. In western Georgia its range is more continuous, and in places it also occurs on the Black Sea coast. In the east it is patchy, reaching as far as Telavi and Lagodekhi. Adults are usually about 12–18 cm long, and males are larger than females. The 2026 Georgian checklist lists it as a confirmed species in Georgia. It was first described from the area around Tbilisi. The same checklist places Georgia’s banded newts in the subspecies O. o. ophryticus. Older books often call the same animal Triturus vittatus ophryticus or the Asia Minor newt, but it is now treated as a separate species. If you come across these names, they refer to this same newt.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia this is a newt of forest and mountain water. When breeding, look
  for it in coniferous, mixed or deciduous forest, on subalpine meadow, and at
  small lakes, ponds or slow streams — especially fish-free water. On land it
  hides under stones, logs and leaf litter; it may also appear on drier ground.
  Elevation in Georgia runs from the coast to about 2,000 m; it is not
  restricted to above 1,200 m. A 1996 review treats the west as more continuous
  and the east as scattered; on the southern Greater Caucasus the eastern limit
  is near Telavi. On the atlas map, two regions have enough records to be marked as its range: Samtskhe-Javakheti and Adjara. There are also records from Kvemo Kartli, Tbilisi, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Racha-Lechkhumi-Kvemo Svaneti, Shida Kartli, Imereti, Kakheti, Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti and Guria. In these regions the records are too few, or their approximate coordinates cannot be placed in a single region, so the map does not count them as its range. Named sites include
  Gagra, Sukhumi, Gumista, Ritsa, Poti, Batumi, Kintrishi, Charnali, Ozurgeti,
  Kutaisi, Ajameti, Lechkhumi, the upper Rioni, Baniskhevi, Didi Mitarbi,
  Triala Lake in Adigeni, Kazbegi, Saguramo, Tsodoreti Lake, Chiantba near
  Telavi, Mariamjvari, Lagodekhi, the upper Liakhvi and Lake Ertso. The 2026
  DNA samples are from Triala Lake and Sakisto Lake. The Tbilisi area (Turtle
  Lake, Betania, Akhaldaba / Tskhneti, Chili-Tba) has historical records; some of the old populations are treated as extinct. The range continues in
  north-eastern Anatolia and the western Caucasus of Russia; records from
  northern Armenia do not agree across sources.
diet: >-
  In water, adult newts catch molluscs, crustaceans and aquatic insects, and often the larvae of other amphibians. Egg-eating has also been described, especially in females. Larvae start on tiny crustaceans, then add molluscs and insects. Once they transform, young newts eat small land invertebrates: springtails, aphids, and beetle and fly larvae. This describes the species in general; its diet has not been studied separately in Georgia.
behavior: |-
  The Northern Banded Newt stays in water while breeding. The rest of the time it often hides on land, under stones, logs and fallen forest leaves. In the Caucasus it usually spends the winter on land as well. It goes into hibernation in September–October and wakes up in February–March at low elevations, or in April–May high in the mountains.

  Breeding runs from late February to July, depending on weather and altitude. Eggs are laid on water plants, singly or in short chains. Clutches of about 60–240 eggs have been described. The larvae stay in water and usually turn into small newts within a few months. In some mountain ponds, late larvae spend the winter in the water as well.

  If you find a newt, watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up or move it from one body of water to another: its skin produces mucus, and you could spread a fungal disease. It usually poses no medical risk to people, but its skin secretions can irritate, so wash your hands after any contact.
conservation: >-
  The 2009 IUCN assessment (2019 errata version) lists it as Near Threatened.
  That assessment predates the split of O. nesterovi; the 2019 errata updates
  the range map. The species is not on Georgia’s 2014 official Red List
  (Decree No. 190) — the only amphibians there are the Caucasian salamander
  and the Syrian spadefoot. Older Georgian Red Data Books listed it as
  Triturus vittatus ophryticus / მცირეაზიური ტრიტონი. The South Caucasus
  biodiversity map treats the national status as NT. In Georgia it is more
  common than the other newts, but local numbers fall if forest is cut, ponds
  dry, become polluted or are stocked with fish; coastal recreation and past
  pet trade add pressure. It occurs in protected areas, including Kintrishi,
  Gumista, Ritsa, Kazbegi, Mariamjvari and Lagodekhi. Some old Tbilisi sites
  are no longer occupied.
interaction: |-
  The northern banded newt (Ommatotriton ophryticus) is not venomous to people and usually poses no danger. When frightened, it tries to escape or hide. In Georgia it is widespread, especially in the west, where its range is relatively continuous. In the east it is more patchy: sites are named in and around Tbilisi, in Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Shida Kartli and Kakheti, as far as Lagodekhi. The atlas map does not yet count these eastern regions as its range, because there are too few precise records from them. It lives in small forest lakes, pools and slow-moving water, especially where there are no fish. It occurs from sea level up to about 2,000 m.

  If you see one by the water or in a forest, simply watch it: do not catch it or pick it up. Amphibian skin is delicate, and substances on human hands can harm it. Above all, do not move it from one pond to another, because moving amphibians can help spread disease. Do not take it home or release it into other water either. If you do touch it, wash your hands well with soap and water, and do not touch your eyes or mouth before that. Accidental contact usually poses no medical danger. The best help you can give a newt is to leave it where you found it: that way you protect both the species and local amphibians.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A banded newt whose main mark is a pale stripe along the flank. The belly is yellow or orange, with no spots. In the breeding season, males have a high, notched crest. In Georgia it is most often confused with smooth and crested newts, so look for both marks together: the pale flank stripe and the unspotted belly.
  traits:
    - >-
      Adults are usually about 12–18 cm long including the tail, rarely up to 20 cm. Males are larger than females. The body is long and slender, and the skin is almost smooth or slightly rough. The tail is about as long as the head and body together. The legs and toes are long too, and longer still in males.
    - >-
      To recognise it, look at the flank first: a pale, silvery stripe with dark edges is this species' main identifying feature. The belly is yellow or orange, without spots. In water the back is bronze-olive or olive-brown with small dark specks, while on land it is more reddish.
    - >-
      In the breeding season the male grows a very high, notched crest along the back and tail, up to about 3 cm. The crest is yellowish or brownish with dark vertical bars. The sides of the tail have blue or greenish spots. The female has no such crest and is a plainer olive colour.
    - >-
      The Caucasian Smooth Newt (Lissotriton lantzi) is smaller and has no pale flank stripe, and the male's crest is low. The Eastern Crested Newt (Triturus karelinii) has rougher skin and dark spots on its belly, while the Northern Banded Newt's belly is unspotted. The Caucasian Salamander (Mertensiella caucasica) is a yellow-spotted stream salamander, not a pond newt. What sets the Northern Banded Newt apart from the smooth and crested newts is the pale flank stripe and the unspotted belly seen together.
    - >-
      Of all the banded newts, only this species lives in Georgia. The other banded newts, O. vittatus and O. nesterovi, do not occur here. One DNA sample from Tsodoreti Lake is sometimes labelled O. nesterovi, but its DNA falls in the same group as the other Georgian samples. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats Georgia's banded newts as O. ophryticus.

    answer: >-

  - question: Is a newt a frog?
    answer: >-
      No. A newt has a tail, while adult frogs and toads (Anura) do not. The Northern Banded Newt also has a pale stripe along its side and a yellow or orange belly with no spots. So look at the tail first: if an adult animal has one, it is not a frog or a toad.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. The Northern Banded Newt has no venom and is harmless to people. Substances released by its skin can, however, cause irritation. That is why you should not pick it up. If you do touch it, wash your hands afterwards.
  - question: How can it be told from other newts?
    answer: >-
      The Northern Banded Newt has a pale stripe along its side and a yellow or orange belly without spots. In the breeding season, males also have a high, notched crest. The smooth newt has no such stripe on its side, while the crested newt has spots on its belly. So it is these two features together, the pale side stripe and the unspotted belly, that set the Northern Banded Newt apart from both.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adult Northern Banded Newts are usually about 12–18 cm long, including the tail. Rarely, they grow to 20 cm. Males are larger than females.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      In water, the Northern Banded Newt eats aquatic invertebrates and often amphibian larvae as well. On land, young newts feed on insects and other small invertebrates.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      In the Caucasus, the Northern Banded Newt breeds from late February to July. The exact timing depends on altitude. It usually spends the winter on land.
  - question: What should I do if I find one?
    answer: >-
