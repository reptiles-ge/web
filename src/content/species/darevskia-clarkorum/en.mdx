overview: >-
  The Turkish lizard (Darevskia clarkorum) is a rock lizard found in Adjara, southwestern Georgia. It belongs to the true lizard family (Lacertidae). It is not venomous and is not dangerous to people. A 2026 Georgian checklist lists it as a confirmed species. This does not mean it is found throughout Georgia: the recent DNA data and images of head scales mentioned here come from the Charnali Valley in Khelvachauri Municipality. The atlas map shows Adjara based on these records, but this does not mean the lizard occurs in every valley in the region. In English, it is called Clark's rock lizard or Clark's lizard. The Georgian checklist calls it the Lazian lizard, while this atlas retains the name “Turkish lizard” used in Georgia's 2014 Red List. The species was first described in Turkey, at the Cankurtaran Pass between Hopa and Borçka in Artvin, not in Georgia. In 2009, the IUCN assessed it as Endangered. Georgia's 2014 Red List also places it in the Endangered (EN) category.
habitat: >-
  It occurs in Georgia. Do not assume it occurs in Kakheti, Javakheti or the Greater Caucasus. A figure from the Charnali Valley is cited in a 2026 publication; DNA data also come from this valley. The distribution information is considered moderately reliable. A 2009 IUCN text also names a small, separate area on the western slopes of Mtirala, near Batumi. This is the second named location in Adjara, but the records do not establish that Charnali and Mtirala are the same place. Nor do they provide a map of the species’ distribution across the country. A 2021 note in The Reptile Database says the species may reach as far north as the Kintrishi Valley. This information comes from personal communication, and the valley is not a named location in the checklist. The species lives in moist, shrubby places near streams, among stones, protruding rocks and loose rock; it also lives in rocky mountain meadows. In Adjara, it can be found on the slopes of wooded valleys and beside stony streams, not in dry steppe. Sources give different elevation ranges for the species: IUCN (2009) gives 1,500–2,200 m; Darevsky and Tuniyev (1997), about 900–1,700 m; and Turkish data include lower places too, at about 450–2,250 m. Charnali is a Colchian valley; this page gives no measured elevation range for the species in Georgia. The 2009 IUCN text also mentions Armenia, citing Baran and Atatür (1998). The 2026 checklist and The Reptile Database do not include Armenia in the species’ range. Do not assume from this atlas that it occurs in Armenia.
diet: >-
  This page has no Georgian stomach-content study. Like other Darevskia it is
  expected to take small invertebrates — insects and similar prey — seized in
  the jaws, without venom. In Turkey, Franzen (1991) also recorded plant feeding
  in this species. That is a Turkish field note, not a Georgian menu.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. It is a rock lizard: it basks on stones and hides in
  crevices. In northeastern Turkey, Ilgaz (2007) collected animals from about
  08:30 to 18:30, often in cloudy or rainy weather. That is a Turkish timetable,
  not a Georgian hourly table. It lays eggs. A Georgian clutch size, laying
  month, and overwintering calendar are not measured here. In Turkish
  populations, Bülbül and co-authors (2016) found sexual maturity at about 1–3
  years depending on altitude, and maximum ages of about 10–12 years. Do not
  treat those figures as Georgian averages. If grabbed, the tail may break and
  the lizard may bite. Watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assessed it as Endangered in 2009 (Tuniyev and co-authors; EN B1ab(i,iii))
  because the known extent of occurrence was under 5,000 km², records were in
  fewer than five locations, and habitat was judged to be declining through
  overgrazing. That assessment is old. IUCN still lists the Charnali form
  separately as Darevskia dryada, Critically Endangered (2009). This atlas
  follows the 2026 checklist and treats dryada as a subspecies, but that IUCN
  listing remains a signal that the Charnali population is highly threatened.
  Georgia's 2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190) includes it as EN, citing IUCN.
  The decree's remark that it is considered Vulnerable worldwide does not match
  the 2009 Endangered assessment. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat it as
  biologically confirmed. A 2019 Charnali field report (Barateli, Rufford)
  estimated about 256 ± 48 animals in one study area and named construction and
  habitat loss as local threats. That is a grant report for part of the gorge,
  not a national census. It is not listed on CITES. Do not take wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and does not pose a health risk. It may bite if picked up, and its tail may come off. It is on Georgia’s Red List. Leave it where you found it. Do not keep a wild animal at home.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The small rock lizard is found in the valleys of Adjara. Color alone is not enough to tell it apart from other Darevskia species in the wild. Its scales, body shape, and where it is found also matter.
  traits:
    - >-
      This rock-dwelling Darevskia has well-developed legs. It lives on rocks and cliffs and is not a large, ground-dwelling Lacerta. In specimens studied in Turkey, the back is ivy green, light green, brown or grayish brown, often with small dark spots. The dark stripe along the side of the head, near the temple, may have several spots with pale centers. There may also be a blue spot near a front leg. The belly is often bright yellow. This appearance is typical, but color alone is not enough to tell the species apart.
    - >-
      Among the many individuals studied in Turkey, head and body length was about 4.4–6.7 cm (about 5.9 cm on average); the original measurements gave a slightly higher figure. The tail is often longer than the body. One male measured about 18.7 cm in total length. According to Arribas and coauthors (2021), the dryada form from Charnali is larger than the typical clarkorum form; its males have longer heads and more scales on their backs. This page does not provide the full set of measurements for individuals measured in Georgia.
    - >-
      The 2026 list still identifies the form found in southwestern Georgia as D. c. dryada, a geographic subspecies. It does not have a separate species page. Arribas and colleagues (2021) recognize dryada as a distinct group, most likely a subspecies; Schmidtler and colleagues (2002) considered it a form associated with local environmental conditions. Colour alone cannot settle this question in the field. The study’s figures show the head scales and the plates in front of the anal opening of specimens from Charnali.
    - >-
      The Adjarian rock lizard (D. adjarica) is listed as a candidate from the parvula group. The DNA records cited are from Shuakhevi, not Charnali. The Artvin lizard (D. derjugini) is a smaller forest species. It lives on the ground among leaves and grass and is not a typical rock lizard. The Colchian rock lizard (D. mixta) is a close relative, but the list does not include it as a species found in Adjara. In southwestern Georgia, do not identify lizards by color alone.
    - >-
      The 2026 list treats it as a biologically confirmed species. But its presence across all of Georgia should not be assumed: the recent DNA data and images of head scales cited here come from the Charnali Valley (Khelvachauri Municipality). These records are why Adjara is marked on the atlas map, but that does not mean the species occurs in every valley in Adjara. The Adjarian rock lizard (D. adjarica) is listed as a candidate species in the same list; the DNA data cited for it come from Shuakhevi, not Charnali.
faq:
  - question: Is Clark's rock lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is biologically confirmed in the 2026 checklist. Named barcodes
      and figures are from the Charnali river valley in Adjara. Do not assume it
      throughout Georgia.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In southwestern Georgia (Adjara). The named modern locality is Charnali
      Gorge (Khelvachauri). IUCN 2009 also names the western slopes of Mtirala
      near Batumi. Look on stony, bushy slopes near streams. This is not a
      Kakheti or Javakheti species.
  - question: Is this Darevskia dryada?
    answer: >-
      On this atlas, dryada is a subspecies (D. c. dryada), not a separate
      Georgian species. Some papers still discuss species rank. The Charnali
      population is the form those papers mean.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      It is a small green-to-brown rock lizard of Adjara gorges. Colour is not
      enough. Compare range and habit with adjarica (Shuakhevi, parvula complex)
      and derjugini (forest floor). Head scales in the checklist figures are
      more reliable than back colour.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. If grabbed it may bite; the tail may break. It is
      Red-Listed in Georgia. Leave it where it is.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Snout–vent length in Turkish material is often about 4.5–7 cm. The
      Charnali dryada form is described as larger. A full Georgian series is not
      on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      There is no Georgian diet study on this page. It is expected to eat small

  - question: When is it active?
