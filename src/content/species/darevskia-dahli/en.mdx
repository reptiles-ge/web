description: >-
  A harmless rock lizard of mountain forest. Confirmed in Georgia; not

overview: >-
  Dahl’s lizard (Darevskia dahli) is a nonvenomous rock lizard that lives in Georgia’s mountain forests. It belongs to the lizard family (Lacertidae) and is harmless to people. Females lay eggs without males, so almost all the lizards seen in the wild are female. Among adults studied in Georgia, body length from the head to the rear opening is usually about 5.4–5.8 cm. According to a 2026 list, Dahl’s lizard is confirmed to have arisen from a cross between two species: the Colchian rock lizard (D. mixta) and the Kura lizard (D. portschinskii). The named modern specimen is from Kojori. At one rocky site near Tbilisi, covering about 1 hectare, Dahl’s lizard was about five times as numerous as the Kura lizard. A survey in Georgia in 2008–2009 recorded it at 82 sites, mainly in the mountain forests of Trialeti. It should not be expected in every part of Georgia. Georgia’s 2014 Red List classifies it as Vulnerable (VU), while the IUCN classifies it as Near Threatened.
habitat: >-
  Its presence in Georgia has been confirmed. A 2010 field study listed 82 places where it was found: rocky areas in mountain forests, meadows, and above the tree line. These places lie at about 830–1960 m above sea level (about 1200 m on average). At 28 sites, it was found alongside the Mtkvari lizard; its range barely overlaps that of the Colchic rock lizard. A barcode record and image from 2026 are from Kojori. According to a 2021 study on a rock face near Tbilisi, dahli was more closely associated with a water source than the Mtkvari lizard was. A model of its range shows the southern slope of the Trialeti Range from Tsalka to Tbilisi’s Vere Valley; it is not a complete map of the species’ range. These records do not establish that the species occurs in Abkhazia, Adjara, or the Colchis Lowland. This atlas map shows records by region; it does not mean the species is found equally throughout every marked region. The place given in the species’ original description is near Shagali in northern Armenia, not in Georgia. The species is also found in Armenia and, according to a reptile database, in Ukraine; this page focuses on records from Georgia.
diet: >-
  This species’ diet has not been studied separately in Georgia, and exactly what it eats there has not been established. Rock lizards generally catch small animals without backbones, including insects, spiders and their relatives, and other animals with jointed legs. A 2010 study reports that this species competes with the Kura lizard for food and shelter, but found no substantial difference in their diets.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day: it basks on rock and hides in crevices. In Georgia, the
  2010 counts followed mass emergence from about April below 1000 m through July
  above 1800 m. It lays eggs. Parthenogenesis means fertilisation is not needed,
  so populations are almost all female. Literature often cites 2–5 eggs per
  clutch; a Georgia-specific clutch size is not published here. Kura lizard
  males sometimes bite parthenogens during mating attempts, but that does not
  create a sexual population. Rare triploid hybrids are infertile. Do not pick
  it up.
conservation: >-
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List classifies the species as Vulnerable (VU): it occurs across a small area in separate locations. The 2009 IUCN assessment classified it as Near Threatened. In the 2026 list, its presence is confirmed by biological evidence, while the accuracy of the information about its distribution is rated as moderate. Groups are often isolated from one another. Changes to its habitat and the expansion of cities are considered threats. It is not listed as a candidate species in the 2026 list.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is harmless to people. A bite from this small lizard poses no health risk either. Do not pick it up: its tail breaks off easily. When seen in the wild, it is often mistaken for other Darevskia species. Photograph its head, side and the underside near the base of its tail, then leave it where you found it.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a small rock lizard. In Georgia, the color of its back alone is not enough to tell it apart from other Darevskia. Where it is found, what its scales look like, and the fact that nearly all are female are more useful clues.
  traits:
    - >-
      It has a small, slender body, a flattened head and well-developed legs. In adult lizards found in Georgia, body length is usually about 5.4–5.8 cm. The greatest reported body length is about 63 mm. Its tail is longer than its body and breaks off easily. Do not pick up the lizard to count its scales.
    - >-
      The back is gray, brownish or olive-colored, often with dark spots or a netlike pattern. The belly may be yellowish. Other rock lizards have similar coloring, so color alone cannot identify the species. The 2026 images from Kojori show the scales on the head and the plates in front of the anus.
    - >-
      The plates just in front of the base of the tail are a more reliable way to tell these lizards apart than color: this lizard has a pair. The Armenian rock lizard (D. armeniaca) has one broad plate. It is larger (body length reaches up to about 74 mm) and occurs in southern Georgia (Ninotsminda / Saghamo, Akhalkalaki), but not in Kojori.
    - >-
      The Kura lizard (D. portschinskii) is often found on the same rocks as dahli. Individuals found in Georgia are similar in size. The Kura lizard has both males and females, while almost all dahli lizards are female. To tell them apart reliably, you need to look at the scales on their heads and the area around the opening under their tails; colour alone is not enough. The Colchian rock lizard (D. mixta) is found only in western Georgia, outside Adjara. Its range barely overlaps with that of dahli.
    - >-
      The 2026 list recognizes this species as a confirmed form that arose from a cross between the Colchian rock lizard (D. mixta) and the Kura lizard (D. portschinskii). It reproduces without mating with males, so you will see almost only females in the wild. A recent specimen identified by this name is from Kojori. Do not expect to find it throughout Georgia.
facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1957 (Darevsky) as Lacerta saxicola dahli. Type locality: near
    Shagali, northern Armenia. The genus Darevskia follows Arribas 1997. Synonym:
    Lacerta dahli.
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist voucher and COI barcode are from Kojori (CaBOL-ID
    1027435). Mitochondrial DNA matches D. mixta and D. armeniaca because mixta
    is the maternal ancestor of both — a barcode is not a field colour mark.
  - >-
    The name dahli honours the zoologist Sergei Konstantinovich Dahl. The
    Georgian name დალის ხვლიკი uses the same eponym.
faq:

      Yes. Its presence is considered confirmed in the 2026 list. Kojori is the location named in recent records. A 2010 survey confirmed its presence at 82 sites in mountain forest. It is not found throughout the country.

    answer: >-
      It is found in rocky parts of mountain forests, at elevations of about 830–1,960 metres, mainly around Trialeti. Kojori is also listed in recent records. Do not look for it in Abkhazia, Adjara or the Colchis Lowland. The map shows the region where it is found, not every individual rocky site.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and poses no risk to your health. Do not pick it up: its tail breaks off easily.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      It is a small rock lizard. Almost all individuals are female. Colour alone is not enough to identify it. It has two plates in front of the anus. The Armenian rock lizard has one broad plate there and is found in southern Georgia. The Mtkvari lizard also has males and is often found on the same rock.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adults found in Georgia usually have a body length of about 5.4–5.8 cm. Published descriptions give a maximum body length of about 63 mm. They are longer when the tail is included, but the tail breaks off easily, so total length can vary.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Its diet has not been studied separately in Georgia. Rock lizards mainly catch small animals without backbones. It is not venomous.
  - question: When is it active, and how does it reproduce?
    answer: >-
      It is active during the day. In Georgia, it has been recorded appearing in large numbers from April to July, depending on the elevation. It reproduces without males and lays eggs. Available reports often mention 2–5 eggs. The number of eggs laid in Georgia is not given separately.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Observe it from a distance. Photograph its head, side and the underside at the base of its tail, then leave it where it is. Do not pick it up. It is often mistaken for another rock lizard; color alone is not enough to tell them apart.
