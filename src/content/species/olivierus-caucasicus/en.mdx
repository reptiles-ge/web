overview: |-
  The Caucasian scorpion belongs to the Buthidae family. The species was first described from a scorpion found in Georgia. Sources give the place where it was found as Tiflis, now Tbilisi.
  Its accepted scientific name today is Olivierus caucasicus. In older scientific works, it often appears as Mesobuthus caucasicus or under other former names. In 2018, scorpions living in the Caucasus were separated from the broader group in which they had previously been placed alongside scorpions from Central Asia.
  Only Tbilisi is marked on the map of Georgia on this page, because sources confirm the scorpion’s presence there.
habitat: |-
  This page does not yet have detailed information confirmed in Georgia about where the Caucasian scorpion lives. The species’ original description lists Tbilisi as its location, while the specimens discussed in a 2018 study are linked to the Caucasus and neighboring areas that are relatively dry.
  We can therefore only tentatively say that this scorpion lives in warm, dry, rocky or open places where scorpions hide in shelters during the day.
  This does not mean the species is found in every part of Tbilisi or every dry place in Georgia.
diet: >-
  In Georgia, the diet of the Caucasian scorpion has not been studied separately
  in detail. Like other scorpions, it is predatory and mainly hunts small
  invertebrates and arthropods.

  It uses pincers to catch prey, and the sting and venom to subdue it.
behavior: |-
  Scorpions often hide under rocks, in crevices or in other sheltered places during the day. They become more active in the evening and at night.
  The Caucasian scorpion does not seek out people or deliberately attack them. Stings usually happen when someone touches a scorpion with their hand, presses against it or disturbs its hiding place.

interaction: |-
  The Caucasian scorpion is venomous and uses its venom to overpower prey. That does not mean it seeks out or attacks people. But a prick from its stinger can be painful, so take care.
  Do not touch a scorpion with your hands. If the pain is severe or a child has been stung, seek medical care promptly. If someone has trouble breathing, loses consciousness, or becomes seriously unwell overall, call 112. See the [guide](/scorpions/morielis-nakbeni) for what to do first.

  summary: |-
    This yellowish-brown scorpion is medium-sized or relatively large and belongs to the Buthidae family. It has two pincers and a tail made up of several sections, with a stinger at the tip.
    Its species cannot be reliably identified from its color alone or from a photo taken at a distance.
  traits:
    - >-
      According to 2018 data, adult males are about 50–55 mm long and females about 58–75 mm. The Caucasian scorpion is larger than the small Euscorpius scorpions found in Georgia.
    - >-
      Its body is yellowish brown. The shield and plates on its back are darker. The front of the fifth section of its tail is also darker.
    - >-
      Many scorpions in the Buthidae family have relatively slender pincers and a well-developed tail. These features help identify them, but a more detailed photo and an expert’s assessment are often needed to determine the exact species.
    -  |-
      When comparing a scorpion with the [mottled scorpion](mesobuthus-eupeus) (Mesobuthus eupeus), consider its size, the details of its coloring, the size of its tail and pincers relative to its body, and where it was found.
      A yellow or yellowish color alone is not enough to identify the species.
    -  |-
      The [Italian scorpion](euscorpius-italicus) and [Mingrelian scorpion](euscorpius-mingrelicus) belong to the Euscorpiidae family, while the Caucasian scorpion belongs to Buthidae.
      To tell these scorpions apart, consider the shape of their pincers, the relative sizes of the parts of their tails, their body size and coloring, and the kinds of places where they are found. No single feature is enough to identify them accurately.

    The accepted scientific name is Olivierus caucasicus (Nordmann, 1840). The
    protonym is Androctonus caucasicus Nordmann, 1840.
  - >-
    In the 2018 revision, Mesobuthus caucasicus sensu stricto is restricted to
    the Caucasus; many older Central Asian records are assigned to other taxa.
  - >-
    The type locality is Georgia, Tiflis, now Tbilisi. This page uses only that
    confirmed administrative unit on the regional map.
  - >-
    Source-supported global range framing includes Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia,

    Scientific sources often rely on the Latin name rather than a fixed English
    common name.
