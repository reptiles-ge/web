commonName: Tawny owl
location: Year-round resident in Georgia
description: >-
  A medium-sized woodland owl with a round head, dark eyes and no ear tufts.
  Confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident and breeder on the 2024
  national bird checklist.
overview: >-
  The tawny owl (Strix aluco) is a medium-sized woodland owl in the family Strigidae. Its presence in Georgia is confirmed by a photograph and a sound recording. Reliable records show that it breeds here and stays throughout the year. It is also called the forest owl, grey owl, and common forest owl. Adults are about 37–46 cm long. They have dark eyes and no ear-like tufts of feathers on their heads. They pose no direct threat to people. It is considered a species at low risk of extinction both worldwide and in Georgia.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, look for it in forests, at forest edges, and in parks and gardens with old trees. It is not as common on high, treeless plateaus or in open steppes. Municipalities are not marked on the map on this page. A 2024 list confirms that the species lives in Georgia year-round, but it contains no records of where it has been found by region. More generally, it lives in deciduous and mixed forests, floodplain forests, cemeteries, large gardens, and city parks with old trees. It needs a tree hollow or another cavity for nesting. It is less common in dense, closed northern coniferous forests. No separate information on the elevations where it occurs in Georgia is published here. Elsewhere in its range, it also reaches high elevations in the mountains. According to published information about the species, it occurs up to about 2,350 m in Turkey; this figure does not apply to Georgia. The atlas map remains blank until information is gathered from sources that name specific places where it has been found in Georgia.
diet: >-
  This page does not include a study of what this species eats in Georgia. It describes its diet in other parts of its range. There, its main prey is small forest mammals, especially voles and mice. It also eats birds, amphibians, beetles and earthworms. In populated areas, birds often make up a larger share of its diet. It usually swallows its prey whole. Later, it brings up part of it as a pellet; bones and fur may be found at the base of a tree.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at night. By day it roosts in the canopy or against a
  trunk; it is often heard before it is seen. It is territorial and stays on
  site year-round; it is not a long-distance migrant. The male territorial
  call is a quavering hoot; the contact call is a short, sharp kee-wick. Both
  sexes can give both calls; the familiar “twit twoo” is often a pair duet,
  not one bird saying both parts. Nesting is usually in a tree hole, less
  often in a building cavity or an old magpie nest; nest boxes are used.
  Species-level clutches are often two or three white eggs; incubation is
  about 28–30 days (BTO figures from the United Kingdom). Young leave the
  cavity some days before they can fly and hide on nearby branches. Those
  figures are from the wider range, not a Georgian nest sample on this page.
  The 2024 checklist treats the species as a breeding resident in Georgia. Do
  not handle it; birds may strike people near the nest.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Strix aluco as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List. The 2024 Georgian checklist assigns LC globally and nationally.
  It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190); among owls that
  list includes barn owl (Tyto alba, EN D1). A Georgian population trend is
  not measured on this page. Documented pressures at species level include
  loss of old cavity trees, road mortality and secondary poisoning from
  rodenticides; that is not a ranked national threat list for Georgia. It is
  listed on CITES Appendix II, so international trade is regulated. Do not
  catch wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is harmless to people if you do not go near it. Near its nest, it may attack your head. You must not catch a wild bird.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium-sized, stocky owl with a round head and dark eyes. It has no ear-like tufts of feathers on its head. According to 2024 records, it lives in Georgia year-round. It is often confused with the long-eared owl, which has feather tufts on its head and orange eyes.
  traits:
    - >-
      Birds of this species are about 37–46 cm long. Their wingspan is about 81–105 cm, and they often weigh about 385–800 g. Females are usually about 5% longer and more than 25% heavier than males. They have large, round heads without ear-like tufts of feathers. A round outline around the face is clearly visible. Their eyes are dark brown or black, not yellow. Their beaks are short, yellowish and hooked. They have stocky bodies, broad wings and relatively short tails.
    - >-
      Its coloring is mainly gray-brown or reddish brown, though shades between the two also occur. In both cases, the underside is pale with dark lengthwise stripes. Light and dark patches on the back help it blend in. Young ones are fluffier and have crosswise stripes, but their eyes are dark too. Gray and dark brown coloring has also been described in the South Caucasus, including Georgia. In the wild, its species can be identified, but its color type cannot be determined.
    - >-
      According to the 2024 list, the long-eared owl (Asio otus) also lives in Georgia year-round. It has long, ear-like feather tufts, orange eyes and a relatively slender body. The short-eared owl (Asio flammeus) lives in open areas and can also be seen during the day. Its feather tufts are short. The Eurasian eagle-owl (Bubo bubo) is much larger; it also has ear-like feather tufts and orange eyes. The little owl (Athene noctua) is small and has yellow eyes. The Eurasian scops owl (Otus scops) is small and has ear-like feather tufts. It nests in Georgia and migrates. The barn owl (Tyto alba) has a heart-shaped face and is pale, almost white. It is on the 2014 Red List. The boreal owl (Aegolius funereus) is a small forest owl with yellow eyes. In this atlas, the Eurasian eagle-owl, little owl, Eurasian scops owl, barn owl and boreal owl have their own pages. The long-eared owl and short-eared owl do not yet have separate pages.
    - >-
      It poses no direct danger to people. Do not pick it up. In Georgia, the round-headed owl with dark eyes and no visible ears that calls “hoo-hoo” in the forest at night is this species, not an eagle owl.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows IOC
    and the OSME Region List for names: order Strigiformes, family Strigidae,
    genus Strix. IOC treats Maghreb owl (Strix mauritanica) as a separate
    species of north-west Africa; it is not on the Georgian list. Birds of
    the World still treats mauritanica as a subspecies in some accounts.
  - >-
    The subspecies expected in the Caucasus is S. a. willkonskii (Menzbier,
    1896). The name refers to a collector based in Batumi; that is taxonomic
    history, not a modern range map. NPLG assigns this subspecies to Georgia.
    Range accounts often place willkonskii from north-east Turkey to
    north-west Iran and Turkmenistan. Sharikov and co-authors (2015) separate
    Caucasian willkonskii from eastern European aluco by territorial calls;
    those recordings are from the Russian Black Sea coast, not a Georgian
    sample. Field identification is to species.
  - >-
    Georgian names in use are ჩვეულებრივი ბუ, ტყის ბუ (SABUKO), რუხი ბუ and
    ჩვეულებრივი ტყის ბუ. Avibase lists ბუ and ტყის ბუ. English names are
    Tawny Owl (IOC and the 2024 checklist), also brown owl and Eurasian tawny
    owl.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photo) and SR (sound recording).
    That is country-level confirmation, not a regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is tawny owl found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia treats it as
      a confirmed year-round resident (YR-R): a breeder present all year.
      Evidence is photographs and sound recordings.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It is found in forests, at forest edges, and in parks or gardens with old trees. This page does not list municipalities, and the regions are not shaded on the map. Its presence in the country has been confirmed, but its presence in individual regions has not.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      It has a round head with no tufts of feathers that look like ears. Its eyes are dark, its body is compact, and its pale belly has lengthwise stripes. At night, its wavering “hoo-hoo” and “ki-vik” can be heard. The long-eared owl has tufts of feathers that look like ears and orange eyes.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 37–46 cm long at species level, wingspan about 81–105 cm.
      Females are usually larger than males. There is no separate measured
      Georgian series on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly small mammals, especially rodents; also birds and invertebrates.
      Diet in Georgia is not separately studied on this page.
  - question: When is it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year, according to the 2024 checklist. It is not treated there as a
      passage migrant or winter-only visitor. It is easiest to detect at
      night by voice.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means a breeding resident. The nest is
      usually a tree hole. Georgian clutch dates are not tabulated here.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless to people, but it may attack to defend itself near its nest. Do not go near it. It faces little threat worldwide. The 2024 national list places it in the Least Concern (LC) category, meaning it faces little threat. It is not on the 2014 Red List. Do not catch wild birds.
