commonName: Caucasian badger
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A stocky, short-legged mustelid with a black-and-white striped head and a
  grizzled grey back. It is a resident native species in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian badger (Meles canescens) is a mammal in the weasel family. In
  Georgia it is known simply as the badger, though some sources still refer to
  it as the European badger (Meles meles). Under modern taxonomy, the badger
  found in Georgia and the South Caucasus is treated as a separate species —
  Meles canescens. The badger is not venomous. Risk to people mainly arises if
  the animal is caught or cornered; in that situation it may bite. The badger is
  not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Meles canescens has not yet been
  assessed separately by IUCN.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia the badger occurs in forests, woodland edges, scrub, farmland
  landscapes and foothills, especially where the soil is suitable for digging a
  sett. According to the National Museum of Georgia and National Geographic
  Georgia map, the species uses many different habitats and is distributed across
  almost the whole country. It is rare in the alpine belt. The same source gives
  an upper range limit of about 2,700 m above sea level, but that is the figure
  from that particular map, not the result of a separate elevation study of
  Georgia’s population. Confirmed records are known from Vashlovani, Lagodekhi,
  Batsara, Kintrishi, Ajameti Managed Reserve and Sataplia. The species is also
  mentioned in the management plan for the Iori floodplain hunting farm. Although
  the badger is widespread in Georgia, this does not mean that it occurs at the
  same density everywhere. Its frequency depends on local environment and habitat
  conditions.
diet: >-
  The badger is an omnivore and chooses food according to its environment. Its
  diet may include earthworms, insects, fruit, small vertebrates and crops. Food
  composition varies considerably across Europe. In wetter regions, earthworms
  are often important, while in dry and Mediterranean-type environments insects,
  fruit and other available foods play a larger role. Studies from western
  Anatolia also describe fruit, insects and earthworms. This page does not have
  a separate diet study for Georgia, so it would be wrong to give specific local
  percentages. The likely picture here is also opportunistic feeding, with diet
  changing by local environment and season.
behavior: >-
  The badger is mainly active at night and at dusk, though it may sometimes
  appear by day, especially in quiet places with little disturbance. Seeing one
  during the day does not by itself mean that the animal is sick. The badger
  lives in a sett. Its den may include several entrances, underground tunnels
  and chambers. Spoil heaps are often visible near the entrances. A well-developed
  sett may be used for many years, sometimes by several generations. Delayed
  implantation is characteristic of badgers in the genus Meles: after
  fertilisation, embryo development pauses for a time. Mating may occur in
  spring and summer, while cubs are usually born in late winter or early spring.
  A litter often contains several cubs. This information is based on biology
  studied across the wider range, not specifically on Georgia’s population. The
  badger’s track is broad and easy to notice. The foot has five toes, and the
  long claws of the forefeet are often visible in tracks; they are adapted for
  digging setts. The track shown on this page was photographed in Vayots Dzor,
  Armenia, on 18 August 2026 and is not a separate Georgian data series.
conservation: >-
  In the 2016 IUCN assessment, the European badger (Meles meles) is assessed as
  Least Concern (LC). That assessment included some populations now often treated
  as the separate species Meles canescens. Meles canescens itself currently has
  no separate IUCN assessment. The badger is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red
  List. On the national map its status is given as NE, and the population is
  described as “small”. That is the wording of a particular source, not the
  result of a new nationwide population assessment. In Georgia the badger is
  listed as a hunting species. In the official list the Latin name is mistakenly
  written as Martes meles, but the intended animal is the badger, not a marten.
  Hunting seasons and quotas are set by current agency documents, so this page
  does not copy changing numbers. Locally, pressure on badgers may be connected
  with hunting, road mortality and conflict with people, for example near crops
  and apiaries. The available data on this page do not assess a national
  population trend. Do not try to handle or catch a wild badger.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom. If handled or cornered it can bite. It has a strong jaw and
  long claws. Any wild mammal can in principle carry rabies; Georgian sources
  more often name fox, jackal, wolf, and dog as the main sources. Do not feed
  or handle it. Keep dogs vaccinated.

  traits:
    - >-
      The body is low and broad, the legs are short and the head is wedge-shaped.
      The ears are small and have white tips. The Caucasian badger (Meles
      canescens) is slightly smaller than the European badger (Meles meles). In
      nearby regions, including Anatolia, described specimens have a head-body
      length of about 73–84 cm and a weight of about 5–9 kg. These are regional
      data and do not represent a separately measured Georgian population series.
      The European badger, especially in northern Europe, often reaches a higher
      autumn weight, so those data should not be transferred directly to
      Caucasian badgers in Georgia.
    - >-
      A clear white stripe runs from the muzzle toward the forehead. On both
      sides of it are black stripes that pass through the eyes and ears. The back
      and sides are grizzled grey; the legs and often the belly are darker. The
      badger found in the South Caucasus often has a darker, brownish-grey back
      than the European badger of northern Europe. In the field, the easiest way
      to recognise a badger is by the distinctive black-and-white facial “mask”.
      However, Meles canescens and Meles meles are difficult to separate reliably
      from appearance alone.
    - >-
      The least weasel is much smaller and more elongated than a badger and has
      no distinctive black-and-white face mask. Beech martens and pine martens
      are slimmer, with long bushy tails, and moving in trees is more typical for
      them. This atlas does not yet have separate pages for them. The red fox is
      clearly dog-like in build, with a narrow muzzle, relatively longer legs and
      a long bushy tail. The honey badger (Mellivora capensis) is not considered
      a characteristic species of Georgia’s fauna.
    - >-
      The badger is not venomous, but you should not try to handle a wild animal.
      If you see an animal near a woodland edge or on a road with black-and-white
      facial markings, short legs and a grey body, it is more likely to be a
      badger than a marten.
facts:
  - >-
    Blanford described Meles canescens in 1875 from Iran (Abadeh). Abramov and
    Puzachenko in 2013, using cranial morphometrics in agreement with molecular
    data, recognise four parapatric species: Meles meles (Europe), Meles
    canescens (Southwest Asia and the mountains of Middle Asia), Meles leucurus,
    and Meles anakuma. The name used on this page for the Georgian population is
    Meles canescens. Order Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Mustelidae,
    genus Meles.
  - >-
    Satunin described Meles meles minor in 1905, type locality Borjomi, Georgia.
    That name is now treated as a synonym of Meles canescens. Other historical
    names include Meles urartuorum and Meles meles ponticus. Official Georgian
    documents still write Meles meles. Do not assign a field individual to a
    subspecies.
  - >-
    The boundary between Meles meles sensu stricto and Meles canescens lies in
    the North Caucasus and is not sharply defined; hybridisation has also been
    mentioned. For Georgia this page follows canescens as the accepted
    Transcaucasian name and does not claim that northern Meles meles is
    confirmed in Georgia.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as native for Meles meles (lumped concept). It is in
    Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s 2002 annotated list and in Eliava et al.
    2007. That is country-level confirmation plus named localities, not a full
    regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the badger found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The badger occurs in Georgia and is part of the country’s mammal
      fauna. Older sources and international assessments often list the Georgian
      population under Meles meles, while modern taxonomy assigns it to
      Meles canescens.
  - question: Why do some sources say Meles meles and others Meles canescens?
    answer: >-
      In older sources, Georgia’s badger often appears under the name Meles meles,
      because populations of the South Caucasus, Anatolia and the Near East were
      formerly included in that same species. In modern taxonomy these
      populations are treated as a separate species — Meles canescens. For that
      reason newer sources more often use Meles canescens for the Georgian
      badger, while some official Georgian documents still use the older name,
      Meles meles. In the field, this difference is not easy to see: both have
      the typical black-and-white badger face.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, badgers may be found in forests, woodland edges, scrub and
      farmland landscapes, especially where the soil is suitable for digging a
      sett. Confirmed records are known from Vashlovani, Lagodekhi, Batsara,
      Kintrishi, Ajameti, Sataplia and the Iori floodplain. This list does not
      show the full range: the badger is more widely distributed in Georgia.
  - question: How can I identify a badger?
    answer: >-
      You can recognise a badger by the black-and-white stripes on the face, a
      stocky short-legged body, a grizzled grey back and a short tail. The least
      weasel is much smaller and more elongated and has no such facial mask. A
      marten is slimmer and has a long bushy tail.
  - question: How large is a badger?
    answer: >-
      The Caucasian badger is slightly smaller than the European badger. In
      Anatolia, described specimens have a head-body length of about 73–84 cm.
      This page does not have a full separate set of measurements for Georgia’s
      population, so these data should be treated as regional reference values.
  - question: What does a badger eat?
    answer: >-
      The badger is an omnivore. Its food may include insects, earthworms, fruit,
      small vertebrates and sometimes crops.
  - question: When is a badger active?
    answer: >-
      The badger is mainly active at night and at dusk, though it may sometimes
      appear by day. It remains in its home area throughout the year and does not
      make seasonal migrations.
  - question: Is a badger dangerous? Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      The badger is not venomous and normally does not attack people. However,
      it is a wild animal and may bite if someone tries to catch or handle it,
      so keep a safe distance. IUCN assesses Meles meles globally as Least
      Concern. In Georgia the badger is not included in the 2014 Red List and is
      defined as a hunting species. Hunting seasons and quotas are set by current
      official rules.
