commonName: Caucasian leopard
location: Rare vagrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A large spotted cat with rosettes and a long tail. Confirmed in Georgia, but
  not as a large resident population.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian leopard, also called leopard (Panthera pardus), is a mammal in
  the cat family (Felidae). Animals in Georgia belong to the West Asian
  subspecies Panthera pardus tulliana — the Persian leopard. At species level
  IUCN lists the leopard as Vulnerable; the subspecies tulliana was assessed as
  Endangered in 2023. Georgia’s 2014 Red List lists the leopard as Critically
  Endangered (CR, D1). IUCN maps Georgia as extant and vagrant: the species
  occurs, but not as a stable numerous population. It has no venom. The human
  risk is a large predator’s bite and livestock killing, not poison.
habitat: >-
  The subspecies mainly uses mountain forest, woodland-scrub, and rocky slopes.
  It usually avoids deserts and open plains, though it can appear in lowlands.
  In the Caucasus it prefers southern slopes; deep snow can block movement.
  Named recent Georgian localities are Vashlovani Protected Areas (tracks and
  camera-traps 2003–2009), Tusheti Protected Areas (camera-trap 2021; IUCN 2023
  reports one male moving between Tusheti and Khunzakhsky Nature Park in
  Dagestan), and Algeti National Park. On 17 September 2025 the Agency of
  Wildlife reported two camera-traps on the outer fence of a deer farm in
  Algeti — the third such record in 20 years. The agency infers that the animal
  has been on the Trialeti Ridge for some time; that is an inference, not an
  independent survey. Mongabay/WWF identify the same animal as Aren, a male
  moving from Armenia; the agency text does not make that identification. These
  are named records, not a complete map. Administrative regions are not coloured
  here. The 2010–2014 national action plan states that in the early twentieth
  century habitat covered most of Georgia except the Javakheti Plateau and the
  south-east lowlands — that is historical, not the present range. A
  Georgia-only elevation band is not measured on this page.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. In northern Iran and
  Armenia, wild ungulates make up 80–95% of consumed biomass. Caucasus
  literature names bezoar goat (Capra aegagrus), wild boar, roe deer, Caucasian
  tur, and red deer. When wild prey is scarce, sheep, goats, cattle, or dogs may
  be taken. The Georgian menu has not been measured. IUCN notes that African
  swine fever in wild boar in Armenia and Georgia may add pressure on the prey
  base.
behavior: >-
  It is a solitary predator. It is mainly active at night and at dusk; in the
  cold season it may also show by day. It marks ranges with scrapes on trails,
  urine, faeces, and tree scratches. In this subspecies the mating peak is often
  January–March; gestation is about 90–106 days, usually 1–4 cubs. Those figures
  come from the wider range. Breeding in Georgia is not confirmed on this page.
  IUCN 2023 states that the Caucasus population is not yet stable: wide-ranging
  males appear, but females and breeding events remain too few. A realistic
  Georgian encounter is a camera-trap or a track — not a guaranteed tourist
  sighting. The territorial call is often a repeated sawing rasp. No Georgian
  recording is on this page; the clip is xeno-canto XC1077461 (Marc Anderson,
  Northern Tuli, Botswana, 7 September 2023), African subspecies P. p. pardus —
  not Caucasian tulliana.
conservation: >-
  Panthera pardus is Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. The subspecies P. p.
  tulliana is Endangered C2a(i) as of 2023: about 750–1,044 wild individuals
  globally, of which 450–626 mature, with a declining trend. Minimum
  camera-trap counts in the South Caucasus (Khorozyan et al. 2022, in the IUCN
  account) were 6–17 in Azerbaijan, 3–9 in Armenia, and one in Georgia.
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Decree No. 190) lists it as CR under D1 — a very
  small population. The action plan states legal protection since 1977; hunting
  is prohibited. It is on CITES Appendix I. Threats include poaching, prey
  decline, habitat fragmentation, and border infrastructure. In the Soviet
  period, state-sponsored leopard hunting with bounties continued in the
  Caucasus into the 1970s. Do not handle wild animals, and do not post exact
  coordinates of a live animal.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous. It is a large predator: it may defend itself or a kill,
  and rare attacks on people are documented elsewhere in the range. A confirmed
  series of attacks on people in Georgia is not on this page. If you find tracks
  or see the animal, leave the area, do not approach cubs, and do not feed it.
  Livestock losses are possible; numbers are not measured here. Seek medical
  care after a bite.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large cat with a long tail, short ears without tufts, and a coat of
    rosettes — not a zigzag and not solid spots all over. In Georgia it is a
    rare vagrant.
  traits:
    - >-
      In a measured Iranian series (Farhadinia et al. 2014; then treated as P. p.
      saxicolor) adult males often have a total length of 200–240 cm (mean about
      221 cm) and mass 40–91 kg (mean about 66 kg); females are smaller — often
      182–228 cm and 26–60 kg. There is no separate wild Georgian measurement
      series on this page. Ears are short and rounded, without tufts. The tail
      is about two-thirds of body length and darker toward the tip.
    - >-
      Ground colour runs from yellowish-grey to golden-brown. Spots are
      rosettes: dark rings with a paler centre, not solid black dots over the
      whole body. The belly is paler. Cubs are darker and more heavily marked.
      Museum mounts in Tbilisi show historical specimens, not a living wild
      animal.
    - >-
      Eurasian lynx (Lynx lynx) occurs in Georgia: it is much smaller, has ear
      tufts, and a short tail with a black tip. The leopard has a long tail and
      rosettes. Grey wolf (Canis lupus) is taller and not spotted. Snow leopard
      (Panthera uncia) does not occur in Georgia. This atlas has no lynx page
      yet.
    - >-
      Live photos on this page are captive or zoo animals, not wild Georgian
      frames. Wild Georgian records are mainly camera-trap images released by
      organisations.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758. Order Carnivora, family Felidae, genus
    Panthera. Caucasus and Iranian Plateau populations are treated as one
    subspecies, P. p. tulliana (Valenciennes, 1856), following Kitchener et al.
    2017 and IUCN. Synonyms include P. p. ciscaucasica (Satunin, 1914) and
    P. p. saxicolor Pocock, 1927. Do not split field sightings among those
    names.
  - >-
    Georgian names in use are ჯიქი, კავკასიური ჯიქი, ლეოპარდი, and წინააზიური
    ჯიქი. English names include Caucasian leopard, Persian leopard, and
    Anatolian leopard. IUCN lists Georgian names as Jiki and Leopardi.
  - >-
    The 2010–2014 action plan gives 1986 as the last recorded killing; some
    secondary sources give 1954. The dates disagree — both are reported here.
  - >-
    Tracks were found in Vashlovani in autumn 2003, then a young male on camera;
    the last Vashlovani record in the action plan and CMS briefing is 2009. In
    August 2021 NACRES/WWF photographed one in Tusheti. In September 2025 the
    Agency of Wildlife published the Algeti camera-trap. WWF/Mongabay link that
    record to a male named Aren; the agency infers a longer presence on
    Trialeti. The versions do not fully match. Those three modern foci do not
    mean the leopard occurs throughout Georgia.
  - >-
    The agency notes that in the Middle Ages the species was more widespread,
    and that older Georgian ვეფხვი could mean leopard. That is a historical
    name, not evidence that tiger (Panthera tigris) occurs in Georgia today.
faq:
  - question: Is the leopard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes, but rarely. IUCN lists the country as extant and vagrant. The
      national Red List status is CR. Modern confirmation is by camera-trap in
      Vashlovani, Tusheti, and Algeti. The 2025 Algeti record was published by
      the Agency of Wildlife; it is not a countrywide density.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Realistically, almost nowhere with any guarantee. Named sites are
      Vashlovani, Tusheti, and Algeti. There is no tourist “almost guaranteed”
      sighting. This page does not colour the regional map.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A large cat with rosettes, a long tail, and no ear tufts. Lynx has a short
      tail, ear tufts, and is much smaller.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      In the Iranian series, adult males often measure 200–240 cm total length
      and 40–91 kg; females are smaller. There is no wild Georgian series here.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly medium and large ungulates. Diet has not been measured separately
      in Georgia. Livestock may be taken if wild prey is scarce.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      Mainly at night and at dusk. It does not migrate like many birds;
      individuals may move across borders.
  - question: Does it make a sound?
    answer: >-
      Yes — a territorial rasp. The clip on this page is from Botswana
      (xeno-canto XC1077461, subspecies P. p. pardus); there is no Georgian or
      Caucasian recording here.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous. It is a large predator. Do not approach. A series of
      attacks on people in Georgia is not documented on this page.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is CR on the Georgian Red List, hunting is prohibited, and it is
      on CITES Appendix I. The subspecies is Endangered globally; the species is
      Vulnerable.
