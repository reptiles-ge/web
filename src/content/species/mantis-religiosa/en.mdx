overview: |-
  The praying mantis (Mantis religiosa) is an insect, not a reptile.
  Its presence in Georgia has been confirmed. According to the IUCN, Georgia is part of its natural range. Ilia State University’s biodiversity database also lists it as a species found in Georgia. Observations recorded on this page report praying mantises in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Tbilisi, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Shida Kartli, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Adjara, Imereti and Abkhazia.
  You can recognize a praying mantis by its triangular, movable head, large eyes, long body and front legs, which it uses to catch prey. At rest, it folds these legs as if praying. It may be green, brownish, straw-colored or yellow-brown.
  Adult females are usually larger and heavier, while males are slimmer and more active.
habitat: >-
  The typical environment for the European mantis is warm, sunny, open places
  rich in vegetation: tall grass, shrubland, field edges, dry grassy areas, and
  gardens. It can also occur in semi-natural urban green spaces if there is
  enough vegetation and prey.

  In Georgia, this page marks 9 regions as the distribution area. This does not
  mean equal density everywhere; record counts also reflect observation effort.
diet: >-
  The European mantis is an ambush predator. It mainly catches other insects and
  small arthropods, holding them with its raptorial forelegs.

  A large individual can sometimes catch relatively large prey, although this
  page does not present a separate detailed study of the Georgian population's
  diet.

  Finding a mantis in a garden does not mean a pest species has appeared. It is a
  natural predator, but it should not be presented as a guaranteed pest-control
  tool.
behavior: >-
  A mantis often sits motionless on grass or a shrub and waits for prey to
  approach. The mobile head and large eyes help it watch its surroundings, while
  the forelegs fold quickly to catch prey.

  Its development proceeds without complete metamorphosis: a nymph hatches from
  the egg and resembles an adult, but does not yet have wings. The species
  overwinters as an egg mass, or ootheca.

  Adults are especially visible in the warm season across many parts of the
  broad range. A precise seasonal calendar for Georgia is not separately
  confirmed by the sources used on this page.
conservation: >-
  Globally, Mantis religiosa is assessed as Least Concern by the IUCN. That does
  not mean every local population is in the same condition.

  Changes to open habitats, intensive management, pesticide use, and direct
  killing can create local pressure on the species.

  The sources used on this page do not provide a quantitative population trend
  for Georgia or a separate regional protection assessment. We can therefore say
  that the species is broadly confirmed in Georgia, but its exact density within
  the country needs additional data.
interaction: |-
  A mantis is neither venomous nor poisonous to people and is not considered a health risk. If you pick one up, it may grip you with its front legs, scratch you or bite you, so there is no need to touch it.
  If you find a mantis in a garden or another green space, it is best to leave it where it is. Watch it from a distance and take a photo without touching it. There is no need to kill it just because it looks unusual.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a large insect with a long body, a movable triangular head and large eyes. It catches prey with its front legs. It can be green, brownish or straw-colored, so don’t rely on color alone to identify it.
  traits:
    - >-
      A praying mantis has a triangular, very mobile head and large, prominent eyes on the sides of its head. This often makes it look as though it is following a person or something else with its eyes.
    - >-
      Its front legs are adapted for catching prey and have strong spines. At rest, it keeps them folded in front of its body. When prey appears, it extends them very quickly and catches it.
    - >-
      The front part of the chest is elongated, and the body is long. Adults have developed wings. Females are usually bulkier and heavier, while males are relatively slimmer.
    - >-
      Color alone is not a reliable way to identify the species. In the wild, both green and brownish individuals occur, and they blend in well with their surroundings.
    - >-
      A young mantis looks like an adult, but is smaller. It has no wings yet, or only the beginnings of its wings are visible.

faq:
  - question: Is the European mantis found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Mantis religiosa is confirmed in Georgia. The IUCN lists Georgia in
      its native range, and the Ilia State University Biodiversity Database also
      lists it for Georgia.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Field records on this page confirm the European mantis in Kakheti, Kvemo
      Kartli, Tbilisi, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Shida Kartli, Samtskhe-Javakheti,
      Adjara, Imereti, and Abkhazia. It is most likely in warm, sunny, vegetated
      places: gardens, fields with tall grass, and shrubland.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Look for the mobile triangular head, large eyes, elongated body, and
      raptorial forelegs folded in a "praying" posture. Colour may be green,
      brownish, or straw-coloured, so do not rely on colour alone.
  - question: Is it venomous?
    answer: >-
      No. The European mantis is not venomous or poisonous to people. If handled,
      it may bite or grip with its spined forelegs, but it is not considered a
      medically significant danger.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It mainly catches other insects and small arthropods. The European mantis
      is an ambush predator and holds prey with fast, spined forelegs.
  - question: When is it most visible?
    answer: >-
      Adult mantises are mainly visible in the warm season. In many northern
      populations across the broad range, they are especially common in summer,
      although a precise seasonal calendar for Georgia is not separately
      confirmed by the sources used on this page.
  - question: Should I pick it up or move it?
    answer: >-
      No. There is no need to pick up a mantis. It is better to observe it in
      place or take a photo without touching it. Catching a wild animal rarely
      improves identification and may harm the animal.
