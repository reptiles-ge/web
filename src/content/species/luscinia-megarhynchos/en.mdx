commonName: Common nightingale
location: Breeding migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A small, plain brown songbird with a rusty tail and a powerful song. Confirmed
  in Georgia as a breeding migrant on the 2024 national bird checklist; it does
  not stay the winter.
overview: >-
  The common nightingale (Luscinia megarhynchos) is a small songbird in the
  family Muscicapidae, confirmed in Georgia as a breeding migrant. The usual
  Georgian name is ბულბული; the more precise name is სამხრეთული ბულბული.
  იადონი is also used for the genus Luscinia. The 2024 annotated checklist of
  the birds of Georgia codes it BB; M: a breeder that is absent in the
  non-breeding season, and also a bird of passage. Seasonality is classed as
  high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality is high (H), with photographs,
  ringing data, and sound recordings. Adults are about 15–16.5 cm long at
  species level, plain brown above with a rusty tail. It is not a threat to
  people. IUCN lists the species as Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns
  the same category nationally.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in the breeding season and on passage, not in winter.
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level breeding-migrant status but does not publish
  a regional gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly across Georgia, including
  treeless high plateaus or closed forest without undergrowth. Species-level
  habitat is dense scrub, woodland edge, river valleys, gardens, and similar
  cover, often near water. The nest is on or near the ground in dense cover. A
  separate national elevation band for Georgia is not published here. The atlas
  map is empty until named Georgian localities are gathered from sources that
  actually list them. Photographs on this page were not taken in Georgia; two
  show the Caucasian subspecies (L. m. africana) in Dagestan.
diet: >-
  No separate Georgian diet study is cited on this page. Across the range it
  takes insects and other small invertebrates on the ground and in leaf litter,
  and seasonally some berries. This is species-level diet, not a measured
  Georgian menu.
behavior: >-
  Active by day, but the song is also heard at night. The male sings; the female
  does not. The song is loud, with whistles, trills, and gurgles. A loud
  whistling crescendo is characteristic and is absent from the song of the
  thrush nightingale (Luscinia luscinia). Unpaired males sing regularly at
  night; after pairing, song is more a dawn and dusk signal. The bird often
  stays hidden in dense scrub and is heard more often than seen. The alarm call
  is frog-like. It is a long-distance migrant: it breeds in Europe and the
  western Palearctic and winters in sub-Saharan Africa. The 2024 checklist
  treats the species in Georgia as a breeder and migrant, not a winter visitor.
  The nest is a cup on or near the ground. At species level a clutch is often
  about 4–5 eggs; incubation is about two weeks, by the female. Those figures
  come from the wider range, not a Georgian nest sample. Exact arrival and
  departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated on this page.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Luscinia megarhynchos as Least Concern on the
  IUCN Red List. The global range is large. In the United Kingdom the species
  is on the red list because of range contraction; that is a British status, not
  a Georgian one. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Decree No.
  190). The 2024 Georgian checklist assigns LC globally and nationally. The
  Georgian population trend is not measured on this page. The EU Birds
  Directive does not apply in Georgia. Do not catch or keep wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous or poisonous and is not a threat to people. Wild birds must
  not be caught. Nests are near the ground; leave cover undisturbed.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A robin-sized plain brown bird with a rusty tail, a pale eye-ring, and a
    powerful song. In Georgia it is a breeding migrant on the 2024 checklist,
    not a winter bird. Usual confusion is with thrush nightingale and European
    robin.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 15–16.5 cm at species level, slightly larger than a European
      robin. In BTO ringing samples from the United Kingdom, adult mass averages
      about 21 g (often about 18–24 g). That is a British series, not a Georgian
      measurement. The body is compact, the tail relatively broad. The bill is
      straight. The sexes look similar.
    - >-
      Adults are plain warm brown above and paler, buff to whitish, below. The
      tail is rusty brown — the best visual mark. A pale eye-ring is often
      present. Field guides describe the Caucasian subspecies L. m. africana as
      paler above, with a stronger supercilium, than the western nominate form.
      Identification in Georgia is to species. Young birds are more mottled.
    - >-
      The thrush nightingale (Luscinia luscinia) is also a breeding migrant in
      Georgia on the 2024 checklist (BB; M), with photographs, ringing, and
      sound recordings. It is darker, more mottled on the breast, and less rusty
      on the tail; its song lacks this species’ loud whistling crescendo. The
      European robin (Erithacus rubecula) is a year-round resident on that
      checklist: it has an orange breast and face, not a plain brown body. The
      bluethroat (Luscinia svecica; often Cyanecula svecica in newer lists) is
      listed in Georgia as a breeding migrant and as a migrant by subspecies;
      males have a blue throat. This atlas has a page for the European robin; it
      does not yet have pages for thrush nightingale or bluethroat.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Do not pick it up. In Georgia in the warm
      months, a plain brown bird with a rusty tail singing from scrub, including
      at night, is this species; an orange breast is a robin, and a blue throat
      is a bluethroat.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by C. L. Brehm in 1831. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows IOC
    World Bird List and OSME Region List names: order Passeriformes, family
    Muscicapidae, genus Luscinia. Chats were formerly placed in Turdidae; NPLG
    Wiki Dictionaries still lists thrushes. Modern classification is in the Old
    World flycatchers.
  - >-
    Widely recognised subspecies are L. m. megarhynchos (western Europe, North
    Africa, Asia Minor), L. m. africana (the Caucasus and adjacent areas, from
    eastern Turkey to southwestern Iran), and L. m. golzii (Central Asia). The
    expected Caucasian form is africana. Field identification in Georgia is to
    species. No Georgian series assigned to subspecies is cited on this page.
  - >-
    The usual Georgian name is ბულბული. The more precise term is სამხრეთული
    ბულბული (Ilia State University dictionary; NPLG uses სამხრული ბულბული).
    იადონი is also used for the genus Luscinia. English names include common
    nightingale, rufous nightingale, and nightingale. The 2024 checklist uses
    Common Nightingale. The thrush nightingale (Luscinia luscinia) is a
    separate species.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes are BB; M, seasonality quality A, global and national
    Red List LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph), RD (ringing
    data), and SR (sound recording). That is country-level confirmation, not a
    regional map. The same list also codes Luscinia luscinia as BB; M. Some
    popular Georgian sources treat the thrush nightingale as passage only; the
    2024 checklist accepts it as a breeder too.
faq:
  - question: Is the common nightingale found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia accepts Luscinia
      megarhynchos as a confirmed breeder and migrant (BB; M). It is not present
      in the non-breeding season.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the warm months, in dense scrub, woodland edges, valleys, and gardens,
      often near water. This page has no municipality list and does not colour a
      regional map. Occurrence is confirmed at country level, not by region. It
      is heard more often than seen.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Plain brown back, rusty tail, pale eye-ring; the sexes look similar. The
      male’s song is loud, with whistles and trills, including a rising
      crescendo. The thrush nightingale lacks that crescendo and has a more
      mottled breast. The European robin has an orange breast.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 15–16.5 cm long at species level. In United Kingdom ringing samples,
      adult mass averages about 21 g. No separate measured Georgian series is
      cited on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly insects and other small invertebrates on the ground; seasonally
      some berries. Diet in Georgia is not studied separately on this page.
  - question: When is it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      As a breeding migrant: in the breeding season and on passage, not in

      for Georgia are not tabulated here. Song is strongest in the breeding

  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
