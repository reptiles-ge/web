description: >-
  A small, harmless newt of forest ponds and lakes in Georgia. A candidate

overview: >-
  The Caucasian smooth newt (Lissotriton lantzi) is a small, harmless newt of
  forest ponds and lakes in Georgia. Adults are usually about 7–11 cm long;
  breeding males may reach about 12 cm. It is not dangerous to people. The
  2026 Georgian checklist treats it as a candidate species: a geographically
  isolated lineage of the smooth newt (L. vulgaris) complex. For field use the
  practical point is simple: this is Georgia’s small, smooth-skinned pond newt
  — not the banded newt and not the crested newt. The checklist barcode is
  from Mtskemsi Lake (Tbilisi); a homologous sequence is from Bakuriani. The
  paper’s figure is Tabaruki / Mtskemsi. Older books listed it as Triturus
  vulgaris lantzi. Wolterstorff’s 1914 material came from Novorossiysk, Poti
  and Borjomi.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia this is a newt of still water in the forest belt. Look for it in
  fish-free or lightly stocked ponds, small lakes, ditches and slow water,
  often beside deciduous or mixed woodland. On land it hides under stones,
  logs and leaf litter. The 2026 checklist’s DNA record is Mtskemsi Lake
  (Tbilisi); Bakuriani is cited as a homologous sequence; the figure locality
  is Tabaruki / Mtskemsi. That is not the whole Georgian range. Tarkhnishvili
  1996 lists 17 Georgian localities, including Batumi, Sukhumi, Gagra,
  Pitsunda, Kutaisi, Bakuriani, Poti, Mukhuri, the Sakochavi lakes, Didi
  Mitarbi, the village of Tba, Kobuleti, Lagodekhi, Sataplia, Chiantba near
  Telavi, Satovle near Tbilisi and Ertatsminda on Trialeti. Some eastern
  populations were already treated as lost by then, for example near Kvareli.
  Skorinov et al. 2014 compile 42 localities in Georgia, from the Colchis
  lowland through Imereti, Borjomi–Bakuriani, Trialeti, Manglisi and Kakheti.
  This atlas marks Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti,
  Samtskhe — Javakheti, Shida Kartli, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli,
  Kakheti and Tbilisi. There is no separate record in the sources used here
  for Racha — Lechkhumi — Kvemo Svaneti. Elevation in Georgia runs from the
  coast to about 1,800 m (Bakuriani, Kakhisi Lake); higher records exist in
  the Russian Caucasus. The range continues in the North Caucasus. Old records
  exist for Armenia, Azerbaijan and north-eastern Turkey; recent confirmation
  there is scarce and some populations are treated as extinct.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. At species level,
  aquatic adults take small water invertebrates — insect larvae, crustaceans
  and worms; on land they take soil and litter invertebrates. Captive animals
  are often fed chironomid larvae. Do not treat that as a menu for every
  Georgian population.
behavior: >-
  It is in water when breeding; at other times it is often on land. Accounts
  describe it as more aquatic than the northern smooth newt. A full calendar
  for every Georgian population is not given here. In the north-western
  Caucasus, in captivity and in the field, courtship runs from late winter to
  early summer, often peaking in February–April; at higher elevation the
  season is later. Eggs are laid singly on water plants. Larvae grow in water
  and leave it after metamorphosis. Paedomorphosis — adults that keep larval
  traits, including gills — has been recorded in some populations. If you find
  one, watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up or move it between
  water bodies: that spreads disease. Skin glands produce a defensive
  secretion; wash your hands after any contact. It is not a medical risk to
  people.
conservation: >-
  Candidate species in the checklist. Species rank within the L. vulgaris
  complex still needs further tests. Pabijan et al. treat it as the oldest
  isolated lineage in the complex (late Miocene; isolation about 6.4 million
  years); that span may not be enough for full reproductive isolation.
  Wielstra et al. 2018 accept it as a species. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 note
  that Frost lists it as a species and Raffaëlli 2022 does not. ITIS, citing
  Amphibian Species of the World 6.2 (15 September 2025), treats the name as a
  junior synonym of L. vulgaris. Mars et al. 2025 recommend subspecies rank
  (L. v. lantzi), because full species status would leave L. vulgaris
  paraphyletic. IUCN does not assess L. lantzi separately; L. vulgaris is Least
  Concern. Skorinov et al. 2014 state that it is absent from the Georgian Red
  List. It is listed in the Red Data Book of Russia. Main threats are
  destruction or infilling of ponds, fish stocking, deforestation and the pet
  trade. Local decline in eastern Georgia was already described in the 1990s.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Georgia’s smallest newt. The skin is smooth or finely granular. There is
    no pale flank stripe. The male’s breeding crest is low. Do not confuse it
    with the banded or crested newt.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is slim and elongate. The skin is smooth or finely granular,
      not coarse like a crested newt. In water the tail is flattened side to
      side and is about as long as the body. Adults are usually about 7–11 cm
      in total length; breeding males are described up to about 12 cm.
      Separate Georgian measurements are not given on this page.
    - >-
      The back is olive, brownish or yellowish with small dark specks or
      spots. There is no silvery flank stripe — that is the main difference
      from the northern banded newt. The belly is yellow to orange. Males have
      large dark belly spots; females have very small ones.
    - >-
      In the breeding season the male grows a low, wavy or finely notched
      crest, not a tall comb. The tail tapers into a thin filament (up to
      about 7 mm). The toes have skin fringes. The female lacks this crest
      and is plainer, with finer spotting.
    - >-
      The northern banded newt (Ommatotriton ophryticus) is larger, has a pale
      flank stripe, an unspotted belly and a very high, notched male crest.
      Karelin’s crested newt (Triturus karelinii) is larger and coarser-skinned,
      with big dark belly spots and a high, notched crest. The Caucasian
      salamander (Mertensiella caucasica) has yellow spots, a round tail in
      cross-section and lives by streams, not in ponds.
    - >-
      The European smooth newt (L. vulgaris) does not occur in Georgia. In
      the field the Caucasian form looks similar; the reliable difference is
      mostly geography, not a single obvious field mark.
faq:
  - question: Does the Caucasian smooth newt occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is a candidate species in the 2026 checklist. The DNA barcode
      is from Mtskemsi Lake (Tbilisi); Bakuriani is also cited. The literature
      has many older and newer records from the west as far as Kakheti.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      At forest-belt ponds, small lakes and slow water — from the coast up to
      Trialeti and Bakuriani. The map marks several regions, not only Tbilisi.
      Present-day occupancy is not confirmed at every old site; decline near
      Kvareli was already noted in the 1990s.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and is harmless to people. Skin secretion can
      irritate. Do not pick it up. Wash your hands after contact.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A small, smooth-skinned newt without a pale flank stripe. Breeding
      males have a low crest and a thin tail filament. The banded newt has a
      flank stripe; the crested newt is larger and coarser-skinned.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adults are usually about 7–11 cm in total length; breeding males are
      described up to about 12 cm. Separate Georgian measurements are not
      given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      In water — small aquatic invertebrates. On land — soil invertebrates.
      There is no Georgian diet study on this page.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is in water when breeding, mostly in spring; at other times it is
      often on land. A full calendar for every Georgian population is not
      given here. At higher elevation the season is later.
  - question: Why is it a candidate species?
    answer: >-
      It is an isolated lineage in the L. vulgaris complex. Some authors
      treat it as a species, others as a subspecies or a synonym. The
      checklist keeps it as a candidate until rank is tested further.
