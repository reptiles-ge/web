description: >-
  A grey, hawk-like bird with a two-note call. Confirmed in Georgia as a
  breeding migrant on the 2024 national bird checklist; it does not stay the
  winter.
overview: >-
  The common cuckoo (Cuculus canorus) is a medium-sized bird in the cuckoo family (Cuculidae). It lays its eggs in other birds’ nests. Its breeding in Georgia is confirmed: it nests there but does not remain outside the breeding season. It is also seen during migration. Its presence in Georgia has been confirmed by a photograph, and the information about its seasonal movements is reliable. An adult is about 32–34 cm long. It is slate gray above and barred below. It poses no direct threat to people. The IUCN classifies it as Least Concern. Georgia’s 2024 bird checklist gives it the same status at the national level.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, look for this bird during the breeding season and migration, not in winter. The map on this page does not shade any municipalities. A 2024 list confirms that the species breeds in Georgia and migrates, but it does not provide records by region. Do not assume it is found equally throughout the country, including on high, treeless plateaus or in dense forests. The species is associated with open and partly open places: forest edges, farmland with trees, river valleys, reed beds, gardens and similar places where its host songbirds nest. No separate information has been published on the elevations at which it occurs in Georgia. diet: >-
  This page does not present a separate study of its diet in Georgia. In other parts of its range, adults eat various insects, especially hairy caterpillars that other birds often avoid. When visiting a nest, a female may also eat an egg or chick belonging to the bird whose nest it is. These are observations about the species in general, not findings from a study of its diet in Georgia.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day and is often heard before it is seen. The male’s song is
  the two-note “cuck-oo”, usually given from an open perch. The female has a
  different, bubbling call. It does not build a nest. It is an obligate brood
  parasite: the female lays in the nests of other birds, often removing one
  host egg. Species-level literature records many host species (commonly
  pipits, reed warblers, dunnocks, and wagtails in Europe); a published host
  inventory for Georgia is not compiled on this page. Several of those host
  groups do breed in Georgia, but that is not the same as a local host list.
  The chick typically ejects remaining eggs or young. Incubation is short,
  about 11–13 days at species level; a female may lay many eggs in different
  nests over a season (handbook figures often fall around 9–22). Adults leave
  the breeding grounds earlier than the young. The 2024 checklist treats the
  species as a breeder and a migrant in Georgia, not as a winter visitor.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Cuculus canorus as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List. The global range is huge; some European farmland populations have
  declined, including a well-documented decline in parts of the United Kingdom,
  but the global decline is not treated as fast enough for a higher threat
  category. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution
  No. 190). The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global and national Red
  List category LC. A Georgia-specific population trend is not quantified on
  this page. The EU Birds Directive does not apply in Georgia. Do not catch or
  keep wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not dangerous to people. A wild bird must not be caught.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a medium-sized grey bird with a long tail and a striped belly. Its call has two notes. Georgia’s 2024 bird list records it as a migratory bird that nests here, but does not list it as a winter bird. It is often mistaken for a sparrowhawk.
  traits:
    - >-
      This bird is about 32–34 cm long, with a wingspan of about 55–60 cm. It often weighs around 110–130 g; males are slightly heavier than females on average. It has a slim body, pointed wings and a long tail with feathers arranged in steps. An adult male is dark grey with a slate tint on its head, back and chest. Black and white bars begin sharply on its belly. Its eye, the ring around the eye, the base of its beak and its feet are yellow. A grey adult female looks similar, but often has a yellowish or brownish tint on its chest and the sides of its neck.
    - >-
      A rufous (“hepatic”) morph occurs in some adult females and more often in
      juveniles: reddish-brown upperparts with dark bars. Juveniles are more
      scaly or barred, often with a white nape patch and pale feather edges.
      Flight can look hawk-like. The call is the reliable field cue: males
      sing “cuck-oo”; sparrowhawks do not.
    - >-
      Georgia’s 2024 list records the sparrowhawk (Accipiter nisus) both as a year-round resident and as a migratory bird. It has broader, rounded wings and a shorter, striped tail. Its beak is hooked, and its eyes are bright yellow or orange. It does not make a “cuckoo” call. The Levant sparrowhawk (Accipiter brevipes) also nests in Georgia and is migratory. It is a hawk, not a cuckoo. The great spotted cuckoo (Clamator glandarius) is listed as an occasional visitor in the 2024 list, with its presence confirmed by photographs. It has a crest and white spots, is sandy brown rather than grey, and does not resemble a sparrowhawk. The Oriental cuckoo (Cuculus optatus) is absent from that list. These species do not yet have separate pages in this atlas.
    - >-
      It poses no direct threat to people. Do not pick it up. In Georgia in late spring, the gray, striped bird calling “goo-goo” from a treetop is this species, not a sparrowhawk.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the
    IOC World Bird List and the OSME Region List for names: order
    Cuculiformes, family Cuculidae, genus Cuculus.
  - >-
    Widely recognised subspecies include C. c. canorus (Europe east through
    Turkey and much of northern Asia), C. c. bangsi (Iberia and north-west
    Africa), C. c. subtelephonus (Central Asia), and C. c. bakeri (Himalayan
    region and nearby). The nominate form is the one whose mapped range
    includes Turkey and therefore the expected form for the Caucasus. Field
    identification is to species. A measured Georgian specimen series assigned
    to subspecies is not given on this page.
  - >-
    Georgian is გუგული; ჩვეულებრივი გუგული is also used. SABUKO lists the
    family as გუგულისებრნი. English names in use are common cuckoo, European
    cuckoo, and Eurasian cuckoo. The 2024 checklist uses Common Cuckoo.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: BB; M, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    confirmation, not a regional map. Great spotted cuckoo is listed separately
    as casual (Cas), with photographs, and national Red List NE.
faq:
  - question: Is the common cuckoo found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed breeding bird and migrant (BB; M). It is absent in the
      non-breeding season.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the warmer months, in open and semi-open country with trees or
      reedbeds where small songbirds nest. This page has no named municipality
      list and does not colour the regional map. Presence is confirmed at
      country level, not mapped region by region here.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Slate-grey upperparts, barred belly, long tail, pointed wings, yellow
      eye-ring. Males sing a two-note “cuck-oo”. A sparrowhawk has a hooked
      bill, broader wings, and does not make that call.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 32–34 cm long at species level, with a wingspan of about 55–60 cm.
      A separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly insects, especially hairy caterpillars. Diet has not been studied
      separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      As a breeding migrant: present in the breeding season and on passage,
      absent in winter, according to the 2024 checklist. Exact arrival and
      departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated on this page.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. BB on the 2024 checklist means it is a confirmed breeder. It does
      not build its own nest; it lays in the nests of other birds. Which host
      species are used in Georgia has not been compiled here.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. It faces little threat worldwide and is also listed in the same category (LC) on the 2024 national list. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Do not catch wild birds.
