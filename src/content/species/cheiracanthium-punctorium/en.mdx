overview: >-
  The European yellow sac spider (Cheiracanthium punctorium) is a spider in the
  family Cheiracanthiidae. The shorter English name “yellow sac spider” is also
  used, but it can refer to several Cheiracanthium species. This page uses the
  scientific name when precision matters.

  The species is confirmed in Georgia. Caucasian Spiders lists Georgian records
  from Tbilisi, Poti, Bakuriani, Sighnaghi, Baghdati, Rokiti, Chokhatauri,
  Betania, south of Kutaisi, and near Atsriskhevi. These records confirm
  occurrence, but they do not show that the species is evenly distributed in all
  Georgian regions.

  Female body length is about 10–15 mm; male body length is 7.5–12 mm, not
  including the legs. Useful field marks include a pale yellowish-green abdomen,
  greenish-brown to orange-red front body, strong chelicerae, and an elongated
  first pair of legs. Colour alone is not enough for a secure identification.

  Cheiracanthium punctorium can bite through human skin, especially if it is
  caught, pressed, or accidentally trapped. Sources describe immediate burning
  pain, local redness, and swelling; necrotic skin reactions are not expected in
  the cited summaries. Do not handle the spider. If symptoms are strong or
  generalised, call 112.
habitat: >-
  At species level, Cheiracanthium punctorium lives mainly in the herb and shrub
  layer of warm, open habitats. European sources associate it with tall grass,
  meadows, fallow land, forest edges, roadsides, and similar open places; it can
  also occur in less-used damp meadows.

  The Georgian records used here include both western and eastern Georgia: Poti,
  south of Kutaisi, Baghdati and Rokiti, Chokhatauri, Tbilisi and Betania,
  Bakuriani, Sighnaghi, and the Atsriskhevi area. The map marks only regions
  supported by those named localities.

  These records do not provide a complete Georgian elevation range. General
  European habitat descriptions should not be copied directly to every habitat in
  Georgia.
diet: >-
  The species is a predator and does not build a round capture web. It hunts
  actively, mostly at night, in grass and low vegetation, subduing small
  arthropods with venom.

  The diet of the Georgian population has not been studied separately in full.
  In general, prey can include insects and other small invertebrates. The species
  has an ecological role as a small arthropod predator, but it should not be
  treated as a deliberate pest-control tool.
behavior: >-
  Cheiracanthium punctorium is mainly nocturnal. During the day it may stay in a
  silk retreat in herbaceous vegetation, shrubs, or under stones. It is not an
  orb-weaver sitting in the centre of a large round web.

  European sources describe conspicuous brood sacs in tall grass. Females defend
  these sacs, so bite risk increases when a person handles or presses the spider
  or its retreat. This is general species biology; a separate seasonal calendar
  for Georgia is not established on this page.
conservation: >-
  World Spider Catalog treats Cheiracanthium punctorium (Villers, 1789) as the
  accepted name. The species has a broad Palearctic range: Europe, Turkey, the
  Caucasus, European Russia to southern Siberia, Iran, and Central Asia.

  The sources used here do not give a quantified population trend for Georgia.
  The species is not listed by name in Georgia's 2014 Red List. That does not
  mean changes to open grassland habitats are locally irrelevant; it only means
  this page does not have reliable data for assigning a national threat category.
interaction: >-
  Cheiracanthium punctorium uses venom to subdue prey. Contact with people is
  usually accidental: picking up the spider, trying to catch it, trapping it in
  clothing, or pressing it in grass or under stones can lead to a bite.

  A bite can be sharply painful. Spiders of Europe and the Swiss study describe
  burning pain that may intensify within minutes and last for several hours, with
  possible local redness and swelling. Necrotic skin injury is not expected in
  those sources. If pain is severe, swelling increases, breathing becomes
  difficult, the face or throat swells, or the general condition worsens, call
  [112](tel:112). Do not cut, suck, or burn the bite. This is educational
  information, not a medical protocol.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium-sized pale yellowish-green spider with strong chelicerae and an
    elongated first pair of legs. It does not build a large round capture web.
    Field identification should use several traits together; secure species-level
    identification may require an adult specimen and specialist examination.
  traits:
    - >-
      Female body length is 10–15 mm; male body length is 7.5–12 mm. Legs are
      not included in that measurement. This is species-level information, not a
      separate Georgian measurement series.
    - >-
      The front body may be greenish-brown or orange-red. The abdomen is often
      pale yellowish-green; a darker spear-shaped mark may be present but can
      also be faint or absent.
    - >-
      The chelicerae are strong and long, with reddish or orange basal parts and
      darker tips. The first pair of legs is elongated, and the tips of the legs
      can look darker.
    - >-
      The species does not build a large orb web. A spider sitting in the centre
      of a round web is more likely the [European garden spider](araneus-diadematus)
      or [Argiope](argiope-bruennichi), not Cheiracanthium punctorium.
    - >-
      In Georgia it can be confused with other pale, long-legged spiders,
      especially species resembling Cheiracanthium or Clubiona. A photo is not
      always enough; specialist confirmation may be needed.
