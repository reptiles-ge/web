overview: >-
  The European garden spider (Araneus diadematus), also called the cross spider
  or cross orb-weaver, is an orb-weaving spider in the family Araneidae. In
  Georgian, the word ჯვრიანა also appears in the name of another animal, so this
  page is specifically about the spider.

  The species is confirmed in Georgia. A published specimen is known from
  Mitarbi near Bakuriani, from a mountain meadow at about 1,500 m. The species is
  also included in the updated spider checklist of Lagodekhi Reserve.

  The main identification mark is the white cross-shaped pattern on the upper
  side of the abdomen. Body colour is quite variable: it may be yellowish, brown,
  reddish, or almost black. Females are much larger than males: female body
  length is about 10–22.5 mm, male body length 4–11 mm.

  It uses venom to subdue prey. It is not considered a medically significant
  species for people, and bites are rare. The species has not been globally
  assessed by the IUCN and is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  The European garden spider builds a large, round web in gardens, shrubland,
  forest edges, meadows, and other vegetated places. The web is often stretched
  between plants, branches, or fences.

  The most precise published Georgian record presented here is from Mitarbi near
  Bakuriani, from a mountain meadow at about 1,500 m. The species is also
  confirmed in the spider list of Lagodekhi Reserve.

  These data are enough to confirm that the species occurs in Georgia, but not
  enough to build an exact distribution map for every region of the country.
  Therefore, do not assume that the European garden spider is evenly distributed
  across all Georgian habitats and elevation belts.
diet: >-
  The European garden spider catches insects and other small arthropods in its
  web. It secures prey with silk and uses venom to subdue it.

  The diet of the Georgian population has not been studied separately in full.
  In general, it is a sit-and-wait predator that uses its web to catch flying
  animals or small animals moving through vegetation.
behavior: >-
  Adult females often sit in the centre of the web and sense prey through web
  vibrations. When disturbed, the spider may hide or shake the web. It does not
  try to attack people.

  European data show adults as especially conspicuous from summer into autumn. A
  full seasonal calendar for Georgia is not available, although the published
  Mitarbi specimen was collected on 21 July.

  Females lay eggs in a silk sac. Young spiders disperse in the following season;
  this is general species biology, not data studied separately for the Georgian
  population.
conservation: >-
  The current scientific name of the species is Araneus diadematus. Its native
  range is broad and includes Europe, Turkey, the Middle East, the Caucasus,
  much of Russia, Iran, Central Asia, China, and Japan. It is considered
  introduced in North America.

  The IUCN has not globally assessed the European garden spider, and it is not
  included in Georgia's 2014 Red List. The sources used on this page do not
  define a quantified population trend or a special national protection status
  for Georgia.

  There is no need to catch a wild spider or move it elsewhere. If the web is not
  creating a problem, it is better to leave it in place.
interaction: >-
  The European garden spider has venom, although it uses it to subdue prey. For
  people, its bite is usually not a serious medical risk. Bites are rare and may
  mainly happen when the spider is picked up, pressed, or accidentally trapped in
  clothing.

  There is no need to handle the spider. If a bite happens, wash the area with
  soap and water and watch your condition. If strong pain, increasing swelling,
  breathing difficulty, or worsening general condition occurs, call 112. This is
  educational information, not a medical protocol.

    - >-

      European garden spider is the white cross-shaped pattern on the upper side
      of the abdomen; it has neither Argiope-like bands nor the side lobes of the
