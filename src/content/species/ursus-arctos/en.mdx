commonName: Brown bear
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A large omnivore with a shoulder hump, rounded ears, and long front claws. It
  is a native resident in Georgia and is on the national Red List.
overview: >-
  The brown bear (Ursus arctos) is a mammal in the bear family (Ursidae) and a
  native, year-round resident in Georgia. At species level, head-body length
  reaches about 1–2.8 m. Mass is highly variable and may range from tens of
  kilograms to several hundred kilograms. Southern Eurasian mountain populations
  are usually much lighter than, for example, the large coastal Alaskan bears. No
  separate large Georgian measurement series is presented on this page. The risk
  to people is linked to the animal's size and strength, not venom. IUCN lists
  the species globally as Least Concern, while Georgia's 2014 Red List treats it
  as Endangered (EN).
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, the brown bear is mainly associated with mountain and forested
  environments, especially where regular human disturbance is lower. Historical
  data indicate that the species disappeared or declined strongly in parts of
  the lowland forest and other easily accessible areas. Its main modern range
  includes the Greater Caucasus, the Lesser Caucasus, and the far east of the
  country, including Vashlovani National Park and its semi-arid surroundings. So
  the brown bear should not be linked only with high mountain forest. The
  records and monitoring data used on this page include Borjomi-Kharagauli,
  Tusheti, Algeti, Mtirala, Kintrishi, Machakhela, Vashlovani, and other places.
  These are specific confirmed localities, not a complete municipal map of
  Georgia. Do not assume the same density across the whole country. A single
  precisely defined elevation range for Georgia is not presented in the sources
  used on this page.
diet: >-
  The brown bear is an omnivore. A separate, complete quantitative diet study
  across Georgia is not presented in the sources used on this page. At species
  level it feeds on herbaceous plants, roots, berries, nuts, chestnuts and other
  fruit or mast, as well as insects, carrion, and sometimes ungulates. The
  Borjomi-Kharagauli and Trialeti data link autumn bear concentrations with
  fruiting and mast trees, including chestnut. In conflict areas it may damage
  beehives, crops, or livestock. These are examples of local conflict, not a
  full description of its diet.
behavior: >-
  The brown bear often moves at dusk and at night, especially near people. In
  quiet, remote forests it may also appear by day. In winter, when food is
  scarce, it rests in a den and enters a long sleep, though it can wake. The
  exact calendar of winter activity has not been established the same way for
  every Georgian population, especially in milder areas such as Vashlovani.
  Pregnancy involves delayed implantation. Cubs are usually born in the den in
  winter and stay with the female for about two years. These are general
  species-level data, not separate figures counted for Georgia. A bear leaves a
  five-toed track. Long claw marks are visible on the front foot, while the hind
  footprint looks more like a human foot than a wolf print. The audio recording
  used on this page is from Finland, not Georgia.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists the brown bear globally as Least Concern, although the condition of
  different regional populations varies strongly. Georgia's 2014 Red List places
  the species in the EN category because of its small, fragmented population.
  Hunting is not allowed. An older 2010 estimate put at least about 450
  individuals in Georgia across about 34,000 km² of habitat. The 2014 estimate
  for Borjomi-Kharagauli listed 38–47 individuals. In 2022–2024 samples from
  several protected areas, 228 unique genotypes were identified, but this is not
  Georgia's total number because the data come from selected areas and much of
  the Greater Caucasus was not included in that sampling. The main named threats
  are illegal hunting, habitat fragmentation, and conflict with people.
interaction: >-
  The brown bear is not venomous, but it is a large and powerful animal. Risk
  can arise during a sudden encounter, especially if cubs or food are nearby. Do
  not approach or feed bears, and do not let a dog run toward one. When moving
  on trails, it is wise to make noise so you do not surprise a bear. Store camp
  food securely. In some villages, damage to beehives and livestock is a real
  problem, especially around Borjomi-Kharagauli, but this does not mean bears
  hunt people. As with any wild mammal, it can theoretically carry rabies. Do
  not touch wild animals by hand.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large, stocky mammal with a pronounced muscular shoulder hump, rounded
    ears, a short tail, and long front claws. No other bear species occurs
    naturally in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level, head-body length is about 1–2.8 m, the tail 6–21 cm,
      and shoulder height often about 90–150 cm. Mass varies widely, and males
      are usually larger than females. A separate large measurement series for
      the Georgian population is not presented in the sources used on this page.
      The face profile is often slightly dished, the ears are small and rounded,
      and the short tail almost disappears in the fur.
    - >-
      Fur colour is highly variable, from pale cream-sandy to dark brown or
      almost black. Some individuals also have a paler patch on the throat or
      chest. The pronounced hump at the shoulders is a muscular structure and an
      important identification feature. The front claws are longer than the hind
      claws and do not retract like a cat's.
    - >-
      No other bear species occurs naturally in Georgia. Polar bear and American
      black bear are not part of Georgia's fauna. A wolf is much slimmer and
      more dog-like, with longer legs and a bushy tail. Wild boar tracks are
      cloven, while a bear leaves a five-toed track. A small bear cub may look
      like a dog from a distance, so approaching it is especially dangerous: the
      female may be nearby.
    - >-
      The brown bear is not venomous. Signs of its presence may include a large
      five-toed print with long claw marks, droppings full of berries or plant
      remains, and dug-up ground. If you meet a bear or fresh bear sign, do not
      approach the animal.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758. The accepted name is Ursus arctos. Kingdom
    Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Carnivora, suborder
    Caniformia, family Ursidae, genus Ursus.
  - >-
    Several subspecies names have been used in the Caucasus, including
    U. a. syriacus, caucasicus, dinniki, and lasistanicus. Murtskhvaladze and
    co-authors (2010), using mitochondrial and microsatellite data, conclude
    that Caucasian bears belong to the nominal species Ursus arctos. Maternal
    lineages differ between the Greater and Lesser Caucasus; male movement,
    likely via the Likhi Ridge, maintains gene flow. Field identification is to
    species.
  - >-
    In Georgian the name is მურა დათვი; often simply დათვი. In English, brown
    bear; inland North American populations are often called grizzly — the same
    species, a different geographic name.
  - >-
    It is in Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s 2002 annotated mammal list. Eliava
    et al. 2007 include it in national biodiversity data. Lortkipanidze 2010
    confirms the modern range in the South Caucasus. That is country-level
    confirmation plus the named points above, not a complete regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the brown bear found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The brown bear is native to Georgia and lives here year-round. It is
      included in the country's mammal checklists and in Georgia's Red List.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, the brown bear is found mainly in mountain forests where
      regular human disturbance is lower. It also lives in the semi-arid
      landscape around Vashlovani. Confirmed places used on this page include
      Borjomi-Kharagauli, Mtirala, Kintrishi, Machakhela, Algeti, Vashlovani,
      and Tusheti. The Greater Caucasus is also part of its range, although this
      page does not map every municipality. A specific photo or record does not
      mean a bear sighting is guaranteed at that site.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A large body, pronounced shoulder hump, rounded ears, short tail, and long
      front claws are the main identification features of the brown bear. No
      other bear species occurs naturally in Georgia. A wolf is slimmer and more
      dog-like.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-

  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      The brown bear is an omnivore. It feeds on plants, berries, chestnuts and

  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      The brown bear is a year-round resident in Georgia and does not make
      regular seasonal migrations like many birds. In winter it rests for a long
      time in a den, although the exact months are not the same for every
      Georgian population. In summer and autumn its signs may appear more often
      because of active feeding and movement.
  - question: Can you hear it?
    answer: >-
      The brown bear makes several sounds, but near people it often moves
      quietly. The audio recording used on this page is from Finland, not
      Georgia; no Georgian sound recording is presented here.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous, but it is a large and powerful animal. Do not get
      close to it, especially if it is a female with cubs. Do not feed bears and
      keep your distance.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, the brown bear has EN status on the Red List and hunting it
      is not allowed. Globally, IUCN lists the species as Least Concern.
