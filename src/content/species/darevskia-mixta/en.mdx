overview: >-
  The Mixed Rock Lizard (Darevskia mixta) is a small rock lizard that, in Georgia, lives only in moist, forested valleys in the west, not in Adjara. Its body, including the head but not the tail, is often about 5–6 cm long. It is not venomous and is not dangerous to people. Recently it has been seen at Sairme, Chkhorotsku and Baniskhevi, in Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti, Imereti and Samtskhe-Javakheti. This does not mean it lives only in those three places, but you should not assume it occurs across all of Georgia either. The 2026 Georgian checklist lists it as a confirmed species, not a candidate. Globally it is considered near threatened, but it is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List. It belongs to the true lizard family (Lacertidae), and the species was described from a specimen collected at Abastumani. English sources often call it the Ajarian lizard or Mixed rock lizard. Because it does not occur in Adjara, the Georgian checklist proposes the name Georgian lizard.
habitat: |-
  It lives in moist forest valleys, among cliffs and rocks. There it warms itself and shelters in crevices. What matters to it is how moist and warm the spot where it lives is. It avoids dry or overly warm places and does not live in open, dry steppe. It has not been found in lowlands or below about 250–280 m.

  According to a 2015 study, in Georgia it occurs in the eastern part of the Meskheti Range. It also lives on the southern slope of the Greater Caucasus, between the Rioni and Khobi valleys. Of the 26 sites named in that study, samples came from Zekari Pass, the Borjomi and Rioni valleys and the Samegrelo Range. An image of it from Sairme was published in 2026. DNA data exist for samples from Sairme and Chkhorotsku, and data from a Baniskhevi sample have also been mentioned.

  Information on its distribution is moderately reliable. Its presence in Adjara, the Colchian Lowland and the dry plains of the east is not confirmed. The 2015 study also failed to confirm it in Adjara or west of the Abastumani Valley. Repeated searches on the Likhi Range did not find it, although another rock lizard (D. obscura / rudis s.l.) lives there. One old specimen from Giresun, Turkey, was probably misidentified. Later records that give no exact location are not treated as confirmed. Its presence in other regions has not been confirmed so far, but that does not mean every suitable valley has been checked.
diet: >-
  It probably eats small animals without a backbone, as other rock lizards do. It catches its prey with its mouth and has no venom.
behavior: >-
  It is a rock lizard that is active during the day. It warms itself on stones, shelters in cracks and defends its territory. It lays eggs. On the maternal side, it is an ancestor of two other species, the Armenian Rock Lizard (D. armeniaca) and Dahl's Rock Lizard (D. dahli). These are separate species that reproduce without males. They are more likely to be found in southern and eastern Georgia, and not necessarily in the same western valley. If you catch a Mixed Rock Lizard, its tail may come off or it may bite. So watch it, but do not pick it up; leave it where it is.
conservation: >-
  In a 2009 international assessment, the Mixed Rock Lizard was rated “Near Threatened”, but that assessment is now out of date. It lives in a small area and is tied to moist forest valleys. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190), and it is not on the CITES list either. Georgia’s 2026 checklist treats its presence as biologically confirmed. Do not catch wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and poses no risk to human health. If you pick it up, it may bite, and its tail may come off. So leave it where you found it. Do not keep a wild animal at home.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This small rock lizard lives in damp valleys in the west but is not found in Adjara. In the wild, you cannot identify it by colour alone. So look at its scales and size as well, and note where you saw the lizard.
  traits:
    - >-
      This lizard lives on rocks and stones, not on the forest floor. From its nose to the base of its tail, it often measures about 4.9–6.3 cm. In the breeding season, males often have an emerald-green back and dark sides. This colouring is typical of it, but colour alone cannot be used to tell the species apart.
    - >-
      One large scale on the temple is an almost decisive clue for identifying this species. It sits between the scale in the middle of the temple and the scale by the ear. Rarely, the Caucasian Rock Lizard (D. caucasica) may have a similar large scale too. So where the ranges of the two species meet, this scale alone is not enough to tell which species you are looking at. The plates in front of the vent are often paired, but on the Greater Caucasus there is more often just one. Do not pick up the lizard to count its scales.
    - >-
      The closest relative of the Mixed Rock Lizard is Clark's Rock Lizard (D. clarkorum), but that species lives apart from it, in the southwest, for example in the Charnali Valley. The Mixed Rock Lizard does not occur in Adjara. There, in Shuakhevi, adjarica, which belongs to the parvula group, may occur. In the Greater Caucasus, the Mixed Rock Lizard and the Caucasian Rock Lizard (D. caucasica) live together only in a narrow strip. Part of their DNA is similar, and you cannot tell them apart by color.
    - >-
      Derjugin's Lizard (D. derjugini) may also occur in the same valley, but it lives on the forest floor. Its head is not flattened, and it is not found on vertical rock faces. mixta was once thought to be a hybrid of derjugini and saxicola; it is now considered a separate species. Another rock lizard species also lives in Samegrelo, for example D. brauneri. You cannot tell these lizards apart by color alone. The drawings of the Sairme lizard show the head scales and the plates in front of the anal opening instead.
    - >-
      The Mixed Rock Lizard (Darevskia mixta) lives only in western Georgia, outside Adjara. Recently it has been found in Sairme, Chkhorotsku and Baniskhevi. The 2026 list treats it as a confirmed species, not a possible one. Clark's Rock Lizard (D. clarkorum) lives separately from it, in the southwest, for example in the Charnali Gorge. So when you try to identify one, pay more attention to where you saw it than to its colour.
faq:
  - question: Is the mixed rock lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 list treats its presence in Georgia as biologically confirmed. There is an image of it from Sairme, and DNA data from Sairme, Chkhorotsku and Baniskhevi. It is found only in western Georgia, though, and not in Adjara.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It lives on cliffs and rocks in damp forest valleys. It occurs in the eastern part of the Meskheti Range, in the Borjomi Valley and on the southern slope of the Greater Caucasus between the Rioni and the Khobi. It is known from Sairme, Chkhorotsku, Baniskhevi and Abastumani. Do not look for it in Adjara, in the lowlands or on the Likhi Range.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Two clues are more reliable than colour: where you saw the lizard, and whether it has a single large scale at the temple that reaches the ear plate. It is a small rock lizard. Males often have a green back and dark sides, but colour alone makes it hard to tell apart. When identifying it, compare it with Clark's Rock Lizard (D. clarkorum) in Adjara, the Caucasian Rock Lizard (D. caucasica) in the Greater Caucasus, and Derjugin's Lizard (D. derjugini), which lives on the ground.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. If you catch it, it may bite you, and its tail may come off. So do not pick it up, and leave it where you found it.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      The head and body together are often about 5–6 cm long. Published measurements put this length at about 4.9–6.3 cm. The tail is not included in this figure, so the whole lizard is longer.
  - question: Is this the Adjarian rock lizard?
    answer: >-
      No. The Mixed Rock Lizard (Darevskia mixta) does not occur in Adjara. In Shuakhevi, in Adjara, there is adjarica, which may belong to the parvula group. That is why the English name Ajarian lizard is misleading. The Georgian checklist suggests the name Georgian lizard instead. So if you see a rock lizard in Adjara, do not take it for this species.
  - question: Is it a candidate species?
    answer: >-
      No. It was once thought to be a hybrid, a cross between two species, but it is now regarded as a separate species. The species list also marks it as biologically confirmed. Its range meets that of the Caucasian Rock Lizard (D. caucasica) only in a small area.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is active during the day. It is likely to be seen in damp valleys during the warmer months.
