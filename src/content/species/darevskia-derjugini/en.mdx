description: >-
  A small, non-venomous, ground-dwelling forest Darevskia. Confirmed in western
  Georgia and the eastern forest belt; colour does not identify the species.
overview: |-
  The Artvin lizard (Darevskia derjugini) is a small forest lizard that lives on the ground. In Georgia, it is found in the western part of the country and the forested belt of the east. It belongs to the family of true lizards (Lacertidae) and is harmless to people.
  Its body is usually about 4–6.5 cm long, and its tail is often nearly twice as long. The Artvin lizard is not found throughout Georgia. It lives more often in wooded, damp places than on dry plains or open steppes.
  The Artvin lizard is classified as Near Threatened.
habitat: |-
  The Artvin lizard is found mainly in forests and at forest edges, on the ground among grass, fallen leaves and roots. It can also be seen beside forest roads, in cleared areas and along rivers and streams.
  In Georgia, it is found in the west and in the forested zone of the east. It has been seen at different elevations, from the lowlands up to about 1,800 metres. This does not mean it is equally common everywhere within that range.
diet: >-
  The diet of the Artvin lizard in Georgia has not been fully studied on its own. Available information suggests that it mainly eats small animals without backbones, including insects and spiders.
behavior: |-
  The Artvin lizard is active during the day. In warm weather, it basks in the sun at forest edges and in open areas. When threatened, it runs away quickly and hides.
  It lays eggs. The species is often reported to lay 4–8 eggs at a time, but this page has no separate figures for how many eggs it lays in Georgia.
  Data from other countries suggest that it becomes ready to reproduce after several years. This timing should not be applied directly to lizards living in Georgia.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists Derjugin's lizard as Near Threatened. Loss and fragmentation of
  forest habitats are considered major threats.

  The species is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red List. Locally, it may be
  affected by logging, road construction, and changes to forest edges.

  Several subspecies have been described within the species, though their
  classification differs between sources. For this page, the main practical
  issue is accurate identification of Darevskia derjugini itself and confirmed
  distribution.
interaction: >-
  The Artvin lizard is not venomous and poses no risk to human health. If you catch one, it may bite or shed its tail to defend itself. Do not pick it up — take a photo and leave it where it is.

  summary: >-
    It is a small forest lizard that lives on the ground. Its head is less flat than those of Darevskia species that live on rocks. Colour alone makes it hard to identify; its body features, the kind of environment it lives in and where it is found also matter.
  traits:
    -  |-
      The Artvin lizard has a less flattened head than many Darevskia rock lizards. The scales on its back are rounded. They are mostly smooth, though some have a slightly raised ridge down the middle.
      Some distinguishing features require a closer look, such as the number of tiny openings on its thighs. You do not need to catch the lizard to check them.
    -  |-
      The back may be dark brown, olive green or brownish. Pale stripes often run along the sides, and some lizards also have faint pale spots there.
      Males often have a greenish belly and may have blue spots on their sides. Females more often have a yellowish or whitish belly. The rear part of the tail is often turquoise or blue in young lizards.
      Their colour varies, so the species cannot be identified with certainty by colour alone.
    -  |-
      The Artvin lizard may resemble the meadow lizard (Darevskia praticola), the Pontic meadow lizard (Darevskia pontica), and several rock-dwelling species of Darevskia.
      The Artvin lizard moves mainly on the forest floor. The rock-dwelling species are more often found near rocks and have flatter heads. The sand lizard (Lacerta agilis) is larger than the Artvin lizard, while the Colchian slow worm (Anguis colchica) has no legs at all.
    -  |-
      The species is listed as the “Caucasian forest lizard” in the 2026 Georgian list. It is also called the “Artvin lizard.” This name refers to Artvin in northeastern Turkey, which is considered the species’ type locality.
      Older sources also call the species Lacerta derjugini. Its current scientific name is Darevskia derjugini.
    -  |-
      The Artvin lizard has been confirmed in Georgia. Recent records report it from Lagodekhi, Sioni, Mulakhi, Racha, Baniskhevi and Kintrishi.
      It is not found throughout the country. It is less likely to be seen on dry plains or open, grassy steppes.

    answer: >-
      Yes. Derjugin's lizard is a confirmed species in Georgia. It occurs in
      western Georgia and in the eastern forest belt, but it is not distributed

  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Look for Derjugin's lizard at forest edges, along forest roads, in
      clearings, and near streams and rivers — mainly on the ground among grass
      and roots.

      Modern records are known from Lagodekhi, Sioni, Mulakhi, Racha, Baniskhevi,
      and Kintrishi. Encounters in dry plains and open steppe are less likely.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. Derjugin's lizard has no venom and poses no medical danger to people.
      When threatened it usually flees. If caught, it may bite, so do not pick it
      up.
  - question: How do I tell it from a rock lizard or a meadow lizard?
    answer: >-
      The Artvin lizard is found mainly on the ground in wooded areas. Its head is less flat than that of Darevskia species that live on rocks. Rock lizards move directly on rocks more often. Color alone is not a reliable way to tell them apart.

      If the lizard is moving on a vertical rock face and has a noticeably flat head, it may be another Darevskia species rather than an Artvin lizard.
