commonName: Steppe ratsnake
location: Dry eastern Georgia
description: >-
  The Steppe ratsnake is a non-venomous, patterned snake with a round pupil. It lives mainly in the dry areas of eastern Georgia and is harmless to people.
overview: >-
  The steppe ratsnake (Elaphe dione) is a patterned, non-venomous snake and harmless to people. It belongs to the family Colubridae and is usually about 0.7–1.2 m long; this size applies to the species as a whole. Its range stretches from the Caucasus to the Pacific Ocean. In Georgia it needs a dry climate and lives in stony or grassy places, mainly in the east: in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. In Kakheti it has recently been confirmed at Kvareli and Kachreti: at Kvareli by DNA, at Kachreti by photographs of its head. By 2021 much more had become known about this snake. It also became clear that suitable places for it exist in western Georgia too. In Europe it faces a low risk of extinction. Georgia's 2026 checklist treats it as a confirmed species, but its distribution is still known only with medium accuracy. So don't expect to find it all over the country.
habitat: |-
  The Steppe ratsnake needs a dry climate. In Georgia it lives on stony or grassy ground, mainly in the east: Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. In Kakheti it has been found at Kvareli and Kachreti. Observations shared on social media and citizen-science platforms showed that, like several other eastern snakes, it has also been seen in suitable places in western Georgia.

  Across its whole range it lives in steppe, shrubland, open woodland, rocky places and altered landscapes too, from sea level to high mountains. It has been confirmed at about 3,520 m in Sichuan, but that is not in Georgia. Outside Georgia it often hides in rodent burrows, under stones or in crevices.
diet: >-
  Ratsnakes of the genus Elaphe, which includes the Steppe ratsnake, usually eat rodents, small birds, nestlings and eggs. They may also eat lizards. In the Volga basin, rodents have been described as the Steppe ratsnake's main prey. Like other members of its genus, it climbs shrubs and trees well. It has no venom: it wraps its body around its prey and suffocates it.
behavior: |-
  It is active in the warm season. Like its close relatives, it often moves about by day, and depending on the weather it may also appear in the evening. It lays eggs. In the Volga basin, 17 females kept in a terrarium each laid 5–14 eggs between early July and mid-August. These figures come from Volga snakes, not Georgian ones.

  When threatened, it usually flees. It has no venom, but a large snake can bite hard, so do not pick it up. In the same dry parts of the east you may also meet the Urartian ratsnake (Elaphe urartica), which has larger markings. Confusion with the Levantine viper, however, is dangerous: the Levantine viper has a vertical pupil and is venomous.
conservation: >-
  Across Europe, the Steppe ratsnake faces a low risk of extinction: its range is wide and it adapts well to different surroundings. It is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List. Georgia's 2026 checklist treats it as a confirmed species, but its distribution is still known only with medium accuracy. Knowing exactly where snakes live matters when deciding which species need protection most. That is because a species' threat assessment also depends on the size of its range. Locally, it is threatened by changes to its habitat, deaths on roads, and killing when people mistake it for a venomous snake. It eats rodents and is useful in that way, but that is no reason to pick it up.

  summary: >-
    The steppe ratsnake is a non-venomous, patterned snake with a round pupil. In Georgia, the best clue is its fine crosswise blotches, often with four lengthwise stripes as well.   traits:
    - >-
      A medium-length ratsnake, not especially heavy-bodied. Snakes of this species are usually about 0.7–1.2 m long. The head is moderately distinct from the neck, and the pupil is round. A dark stripe often runs through the eye. Do not pick the snake up to count its scales.
    - >-
      The ground colour is grey, brownish or yellowish. Dark crossbars mark the back, and four paler stripes often run along its length. The head also carries a dark pattern, while the belly is pale and often finely spotted. Young snakes have the same pattern, but it stands out more sharply against the background.
    - >-
      Several other snakes look like the Steppe ratsnake. The Urartian ratsnake (Elaphe urartica) is stouter, with a dark head and larger, rounded blotches. It lacks the fine crosswise pattern and the four stripes. The Spotted Whip Snake (Hemorrhois ravergieri) is slimmer and faster. Its blotches are often diamond-shaped and may join into stripes on the tail. The Red-bellied racer (Dolichophis schmidti) is faster too. Adults are often plain reddish with a red belly. Dahl's whip snake (Platyceps najadum) is slimmer. Instead of a fine pattern along the whole back, it has eye-like spots on the neck. So to tell them apart, look at the pattern on the back first.
    - >-
      The Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetinus) is venomous. Its pupil is narrow and vertical, its head is broad, and its scales are rough and ridged. The European cat snake (Telescopus fallax) also has a vertical pupil and is active at night. The smooth snake is a small snake with smooth scales. If a patterned snake has a vertical pupil, it is not a steppe ratsnake: it may be a Levantine viper, another viper or a European cat snake. The steppe ratsnake has no venom, but its bite can be painful, so do not pick it up.
    - >-
      In Georgia, the Steppe ratsnake occurs in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. In recent years it has been recorded at two named sites, Kvareli and Kachreti, both in Kakheti. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as a confirmed species, though its distribution is known only with medium accuracy. Do not expect it throughout the country.

faq:
  - question: Is the steppe ratsnake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as a confirmed species. However, it does not occur throughout the country, so don't look for it everywhere.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Mainly in the dry east of Georgia. In Kakheti it has been seen at Kvareli and Kachreti, and it also occurs in Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. There are suitable places for it in western Georgia too, but no specific western locality has been named. So its main area is still the dry east.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      The Steppe ratsnake is non-venomous: it wraps its body around its prey and squeezes it to death. When threatened, it usually flees. A large snake can still bite, so do not pick it up. A vertical pupil, on the other hand, may belong to a Levantine viper, another viper or a European cat snake.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Look for fine crosswise blotches on a grey or brownish background, often with four lengthwise stripes as well. A dark stripe runs through the eye, and the pupil is round. The Urartian ratsnake has large, rounded blotches and a dark head. The Levantine viper has a vertical pupil. So a snake with a vertical pupil is not a Steppe ratsnake.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      It is usually about 0.7–1.2 m long. This figure applies to the species as a whole.
  - question: How does it differ from the Urartian ratsnake?
    answer: >-
      By pattern and build. The steppe ratsnake has a finer pattern and often four stripes. The Urartian ratsnake is stouter, with a dark head and larger, rounded blotches. Both are harmless and may live side by side in the dry east. To tell them apart, look at the back pattern and the colour of the head.
  - question: What does it eat, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      It lays eggs. In the Volga basin, clutches of 5–14 eggs were counted, but that figure comes from snakes there, not from Georgia. In the Volga basin its main prey is rodents. Other ratsnakes in its genus eat rodents, small birds and eggs.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is active in the warm season and often moves about by day. At this time it may turn up in dry rocky or grassy places.
