overview: >-
  The Alpine Rock Lizard (Darevskia alpina) is a rock lizard of the high mountains of the western Caucasus. It may have arisen from interbreeding between two groups of rock lizards, caucasica and saxicola. Georgia’s 2026 checklist includes it as a candidate but says little about it. The checklist gives no list of sightings in Georgia, no specific location and no DNA data for identifying the species. Other international publications place it in the western Caucasus, including Abkhazia and western Georgia. Those reports do not come from the 2026 checklist. It was first described in 1967 as a subspecies of the Caucasian Rock Lizard. The place named in that first description is not in Georgia: it is Terskol, in Kabardino-Balkaria, Russia, at about 2,200 m. Its Georgian name points to Abkhazia, while the English name alpine rock lizard and the Russian “alpine lizard” point to altitude. It has no venom and is not dangerous to people.
habitat: >-
  This high-mountain rock lizard lives in the western Greater Caucasus, west of the Baksan Valley, at roughly 1,650 m and above. Its range covers only the Western Caucasus: the high-mountain districts of Russia, Abkhazia and western Georgia. This is the range described in published works, not localities confirmed in Georgia. A 2013 paper linked specimens from 1912 to Upper Bachiani / Upper Ksani, at about 2,296 m. That site is cut off from the species’ known western range. Suitable habitat alone cannot tell us which region of Georgia it lives in.
diet: >-
  Rock lizards of the genus Darevskia usually catch small prey: insects, spiders and other small animals without a backbone. They hunt on rocks, among fallen stones and along the edges of meadows.
behavior: >-
  It is active during the day. In warm weather it can be seen on rocks and high in the mountains, including below alpine meadows. High in the mountains it is probably active for only a short part of the year. It lays eggs. Do not take a lizard from the wild.
conservation: >-
  Globally, the Alpine Rock Lizard is classed as Vulnerable. It was assessed this way in 2009, and a 2024 assessment gives the same category. Of the Darevskia species, Georgia’s 2014 Red List includes D. clarkorum, D. dahli and D. mixta, but not D. alpina. A 2013 assessment says that its habitats across the Caucasus are deteriorating because of human activity and climate change. Georgia’s 2026 checklist lists it as a candidate for recognition as a separate species. It may have arisen through interbreeding between other species, so more evidence is needed to recognize it as a separate species.

  traits:
    - >-
      In the shape of its body and head, it is a typical rock lizard: a flattened body, a long tail and clearly visible legs. Do not identify the species by its colour in a photo alone.
    - >-
      In appearance, it falls between Brauner's Rock Lizard (D. brauneri) and the Caucasian Rock Lizard (D. caucasica). Brauner's Rock Lizard belongs to the saxicola group. The other species in this group also have almost no simple outward features that let you tell them apart. So do not rely on appearance alone to identify the species.
    - >-
      The Caucasian Rock Lizard (D. caucasica) is confirmed on the list. In Georgia, the places named for it are Truso (Zakagori), Tbatana and Lebarde. Brauner's Rock Lizard is so far listed only as a possibility, with Mestia (Manshura), Martvili and Ertso Lake given for it. The two rock lizards may look alike in color, so do not rely on color alone to tell them apart.
    - >-
      The Dagestani rock lizard (D. daghestanica) belongs to the caucasica group. Along the upper Terek River, their ranges join without a gap. The Ajarian Rock Lizard (D. adjarica), by contrast, is listed for southwestern Georgia, a different area. You can't tell these pairs apart by color either.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. If you do not catch it, it poses almost no risk to people. Watch it in the wild, but do not pick it up.
