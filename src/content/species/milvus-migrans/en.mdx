commonName: Black kite
location: Year-round resident, migrant and winter visitor in Georgia
description: >-
  A medium-sized bird of prey with a forked tail, not an eagle. Confirmed in
  Georgia on the 2024 national bird checklist as a year-round resident, a
  passage migrant, and a winter visitor. It passes the Batumi bottleneck in
  large numbers in autumn.
overview: >-
  The black kite (Milvus migrans) is confirmed in Georgia as a year-round
  resident, passage migrant, and winter visitor; autumn passage at Batumi is
  massive. It belongs to the family Accipitridae. The 2024 annotated checklist
  of the birds of Georgia codes it YR-R; M; WV: a breeding resident, a bird of
  passage, and a winter visitor. Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A).
  Occurrence quality is high (H), with photographs. Abuladze (2013) uses the
  Georgian name ძერა; this atlas uses შავი ძერა as the page title. The European
  form is about 48–58 cm long, with a wingspan of about 130–155 cm. It is not a
  threat to people. IUCN lists the species as Least Concern. The 2024 checklist
  assigns the same category nationally.
habitat: >-
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level resident, migrant, and winter-visitor status
  but does not publish a regional gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly
  across Georgia, including treeless high plateaus or alpine meadows. Abuladze
  (2013) names breeding habitat as deciduous floodplain forest on large rivers:
  in the west, Rioni, Enguri, Khobi, Bzipi, and Kelasuri; in the east, Mtkvari,
  Alazani, and Iori. Nesting elevation in that source is up to about 800 m in
  the Lesser Caucasus and about 1,000 m in the Greater Caucasus. Named nests
  (historical): Armazi near Mtskheta (1974, cliff), Vere gorge west of Tbilisi
  (1976, cliff), Okumi floodplain in Abkhazia (1983), a grey-heron colony in
  Kobuleti district, Adjara (1981 and 1988; not found there in 1991–2012), and
  Rioni floodplain below Vani, Imereti (1978–1980). In winter Abuladze treats it
  as the most numerous wintering raptor, mainly in the western Kolkheti Lowland
  and Black Sea coast, including the Paliastomi basin and the lower Rioni; it is
  scarcer in the east. The Batumi Raptor Count has monitored autumn passage
  since 2008 from watchpoints at Sakhalvasho and Shuamta, north of Batumi in
  Adjara. Those figures describe passage, not a breeding-distribution map.
  Photographs on this page were not taken in Georgia.
diet: >-
  Abuladze’s 1970s material from western Georgian floodplains (Rioni, Enguri,
  Khobi) and Gardabani Reserve (Mtkvari floodplain) shows a broad diet: hunting
  and scavenging. Prey remains (about 170) and pellets (n = 232) from 19 nests,
  plus about 60 observed attacks: mammals and birds at least three-quarters of
  prey frequency and more than 95% of prey biomass; amphibians and reptiles are
  frequent in the breeding season but a small share of biomass. Among reptiles,
  grass snake (Natrix natrix) and dice snake (Natrix tessellata) were the usual
  species. Also large insects, fish, frogs, small and medium birds, and rodents;
  remains of domestic fowl were found under nests several times. In winter it
  often feeds at dumps and on carrion. At species level it is an opportunistic
  hunter-scavenger.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. In flight the tail is forked — the main field mark. The
  wings are long and angled; compared with a buzzard it is more agile and often
  flaps, not only glides. The call is a sharp whistle, often with a rapid
  whinny. The nest is of sticks, often lined with rubbish (rags, wire, plastic);
  44 of 47 nests in Abuladze were in old trees, mostly 10–20 m up. Eggs are laid
  in the second and third decades of April, mostly 15–25 April; of 27 complete
  clutches, 21 had two eggs, five had three, one had four. The female incubates
  about 32 (±3) days. Hatching was recorded from 11 May to 2 June; young fledge
  after about 45 (±3) days, sometimes up to 55. First fledging in western
  Georgia was on the Rioni floodplain between 4 and 12 July, about a week later
  in the east. The 2024 checklist treats the species as a breeding resident in
  Georgia. In autumn the Batumi bottleneck is a mass-passage site.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Milvus migrans as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List (2021). The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global and national
  Red List category LC. The species is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List
  (Government Resolution No. 190); long-legged buzzard (Buteo rufinus rufinus)
  is listed there as VU, not this kite. The European Red List of Birds gives
  Georgia 240–2,500 breeding pairs (2013–2017, partial method) and a very wide
  short-term interval; that is uncertainty, not a precise census. Abuladze
  (2013) estimated 400–500 breeding pairs at the time, down from 900–1,100 in
  the mid-1970s; Galvez, Gavashelishvili and Javakhishvili (2005) gave 100–300
  pairs. A complete 2020s national breeding census is not on this page. Passage
  at Batumi increased through 2008–2019 at about 10% per year (Hoekstra et al.
  2020; Vansteelant et al. 2020); that is a passage trend, not a breeding count.
  Abuladze inferred a decline in passage relative to early BRC years; later
  counts do not support that. Documented pressures include illegal shooting in
  the Batumi bottleneck and, in Abuladze, hunting, electrocution, and loss of
  floodplain forest. Accipitridae is on CITES Appendix II. Do not catch or keep
  wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people if left alone. The talons are strong; wild birds
  must not be captured.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium-sized raptor with a forked tail and dark brown plumage. In Georgia
    it is a resident, migrant, and winter visitor on the 2024 checklist. It is
    not an eagle. Usual confusion is with common buzzard, European honey-buzzard,
    and red kite.
  traits:
    - >-
      The European form is about 48–58 cm long, wingspan about 130–155 cm, mass
      often 560–940 g; females are generally larger than males. A separate
      measured series from Georgia is not given on this page. The head is
      relatively pale, the bill black with a yellow cere, the legs yellow. Back
      and wings are dark brown; the underwing often shows a pale “window” at the
      base of the primaries. The tail is forked in flight and barred. Sexes are
      alike in the field. Juveniles have a shallower fork.
    - >-
      The subspecies expected in Georgia is M. m. migrans. Abuladze (2013) names
      this form as the only one in the country. Yellow-billed kite (Milvus
      aegyptius) is often split by IOC and is not on the Georgian list.
      Black-eared lineatus is an eastern Palearctic form; field identification
      on this page is to species.
    - >-
      Common buzzard (Buteo buteo) has a page in this atlas: broader wings, a
      short rounded tail, often a dark carpal patch, no fork. European
      honey-buzzard (Pernis apivorus) also passes Batumi in large numbers: a
      small pigeon-like head, a long barred tail that is not constantly forked;
      this atlas does not yet have a separate page. Red kite (Milvus milvus) is
      rare on the 2024 checklist (ND); Abuladze treats it as an occasional
      visitor and notes confusion with pale immature black kites. Red kite has a
      deeper fork, a rufous body, and a white wing patch. Golden eagle (Aquila
      chrysaetos) is much larger, with a more protruding head; this atlas has a
      page for that species. Griffon vulture (Gyps fulvus) is a large scavenger
      with an almost bare head; this atlas has a page for that species too.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Leave it alone. A medium dark raptor with a
      forked tail over Batumi in autumn is more likely this species than an
      eagle or a buzzard.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Boddaert in 1783 as Falco migrans. The 2024 Georgian checklist
    follows the OSME Region List for names: order Accipitriformes, family
    Accipitridae, genus Milvus. IOC World Bird List treats Milvus migrans as
    this species; the English name is Black Kite, the checklist uses Eurasian
    Black Kite. An old synonym is M. korschun, which NPLG also mentions.
  - >-
    Georgian is შავი ძერა; Abuladze and NPLG mainly use ძერა. Red kite (Milvus
    milvus) is a separate species and a scarce visitor in Georgia, not the taxon
    of this page.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R; M; WV, data quality A, global and national Red
    List LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is
    country-level confirmation plus the named Batumi counts and Abuladze
    localities, not a complete regional map.
  - >-
    Hoekstra et al. (2020): about 60,000 at Batumi in 2008, over 240,000 in 2019;
    2008–2019 mean about 149,404, peak day 38,140. BRC autumn reports: 2022 —
    347,943; 2023 — 44,591 in one day; 2025 — over 350,000 in the season. Full
    spring counts in 2019, 2020 and 2022 averaged 239,649 ± 22,547. Those are
    passage figures, not a breeding population.
faq:
  - question: Is the black kite found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      year-round resident, migrant, and winter visitor (YR-R; M; WV), with
      photographs.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In floodplain forest and near wetlands where it is resident. The most
      concentrated autumn passage is documented in the Batumi bottleneck from
      the Sakhalvasho and Shuamta watchpoints. In winter Abuladze names mainly
      the western Georgian lowland. This page does not map every region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Medium size, dark brown plumage, a forked tail in flight, long angled
      wings, often a pale patch on the underwing. A buzzard’s tail is rounded.
      Eagles are larger.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      The European form is about 48–58 cm long, wingspan about 130–155 cm. Mass
      is often 560–940 g. A separate measured series from Georgia is not given
      on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Live prey and carrion: rodents, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, insects,
      and refuse. Abuladze’s Georgian sample is from the 1970s.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year, according to the 2024 checklist. Abuladze places the spring peak
      in the first week of April and autumn peaks in the second decade of
      September and late September to the first week of October. At Batumi the
      autumn mass is often mid-September. Exact arrival dates for the whole
      country are not tabulated on this page.
  - question: Is it an eagle or a red kite?
    answer: >-
      Neither. This is the black kite (Milvus migrans). Eagles belong to other
      genera. Red kite (Milvus milvus) is a separate, scarce species in Georgia.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless if left alone. It is globally Least Concern and is coded LC
      nationally on the 2024 checklist. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
      Do not catch wild birds.
