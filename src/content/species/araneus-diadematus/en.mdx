overview: >-
  The European garden spider (Araneus diadematus), also called the cross spider
  or cross orb-weaver, is an orb-weaving spider in the family Araneidae. In
  Georgian this page uses ჩვეულებრივი ჯვრიანა for the spider; this should not
  be confused with the amphibian [Caucasian parsley frog](/en/amphibians/pelodytes-caucasicus),
  which also has a Georgian name containing ჯვრიანა.

  The species is confirmed in Georgia. The CaBOL paper lists a female specimen
  from Mitarbi near Bakuriani at 1500 m, in montane meadow. Otto & Japoshvili
  2018 also include the species in the updated checklist of Lagodekhi Reserve,
  citing the older Mcheidze 1964 record and their study.

  The easiest field mark is the white cross-shaped pattern on the upper side of
  the abdomen. Body colour is variable, from yellowish to brown, reddish, or
  almost black. Females are usually much larger than males: female body length is
  10–22.5 mm, male body length 4–11 mm.

  The spider has venom for subduing prey. It is not considered medically
  significant for people; bites are rare and usually linked to handling or
  accidental pressure. The species has not been globally assessed by the IUCN and
  is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  The European garden spider builds a large round orb web in gardens, shrubland,
  forest edges, meadows, and other vegetated places. The web is often stretched
  between plants, fences, or branches.

  The most precise Georgian record used here is Mitarbi near Bakuriani, 1500 m,
  in montane meadow. The updated Lagodekhi Reserve checklist also confirms the
  species. These data confirm occurrence in Georgia, but they are not enough to
  draw a complete municipal or all-region distribution map.

  Do not assume equal occurrence in every Georgian habitat and elevation belt.
  Realistic places to look are gardens, forest edges, grassy plots, and shrubland
  with enough vegetation for web supports.
diet: >-
  The European garden spider catches insects and other small arthropods in its
  web. It secures prey with silk and uses venom to subdue it.

  The diet of the Georgian population has not been studied separately here. The
  safest species-level summary is that it is a sit-and-wait predator taking
  flying or plant-moving insects that enter the web.
behavior: >-
  Adult females often sit in the centre of the web and detect prey through web
  vibrations. When disturbed, the spider may hide or shake the web. It does not
  try to attack people.

  European data show adults most conspicuously from summer into autumn. A full
  month-by-month Georgian calendar is not available on this page, but the
  published Mitarbi specimen was collected on 21 July.

  Females lay eggs in a silk sac. Young spiders disperse in the following season;
  this is general biology, not a separately quantified Georgian dataset.
conservation: >-
  World Spider Catalog treats Araneus diadematus Clerck, 1757 as the accepted
  name. The range is broad: Europe, Turkey, the Middle East, the Caucasus, Russia
  from Europe to the Far East, Iran, Central Asia, China, and Japan; it is
  introduced in North America.

  The species has not been globally assessed by the IUCN. It is not included in
  Georgia's 2014 Red List. The sources used here do not provide a quantified
  Georgian population trend or a special national protection status.

  Do not collect wild spiders or move them elsewhere. Remove a web only when it
  directly blocks a path or an everyday-use space.
interaction: >-
  The European garden spider has venom, but uses it to subdue prey. For people,
  its bite is usually not a serious medical risk. Bites are rare and mainly may
  happen when the spider is picked up, pressed, or accidentally trapped in
  clothing.

  There is no need to handle the spider. If a bite happens, wash the area with
  soap and water and watch symptoms. If strong pain, increasing swelling,
  breathing difficulty, or worsening general condition occurs, call 112. This is
  educational information, not a medical protocol.

  summary: >-
    A medium to large orb-weaving spider with variable colour and a clear white
    cross-shaped mark on the upper abdomen. The legs are ringed, and females are
    larger than males.
  traits:
    - >-
      Female body length is 10–22.5 mm; male body length is 4–11 mm. Legs are not
      included in this measurement. The female has a larger, rounder abdomen; the
      male is smaller and slimmer.
    - >-
      Colour is highly variable: pale yellowish, brown, reddish, or almost black.
      Despite this variation, the white cross-shaped dorsal pattern is usually
      visible and is the main field mark.
    - >-
      The legs are distinctly ringed. The abdomen appears slightly angular at the
      front and is widest in the front third.
    - >-
      The web is large, round, and orb-shaped. The spider often sits in the centre
      of the web in gardens, forest edges, and vegetated plots.
    - >-
      The [wasp spider](argiope-bruennichi) has bold yellow-and-black cross-bands
      and often a white zigzag in the web. The [lobed argiope](argiope-lobata)
      has obvious side lobes on the abdomen. The European garden spider has a
      white cross on the abdomen, not Argiope-like bands or side lobes.
