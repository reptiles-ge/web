description: >-
  A small harmless snake with a dark collar. Confirmed in Georgia in the

overview: >-
  The collared eirenis (Eirenis collaris) is a small snake found in southeastern Georgia. It belongs to the colubrid family (Colubridae). The 2026 Georgian list treats its presence in Georgia as confirmed. The subspecies found here is E. c. collaris. A snake mentioned in the list is from Taribana in Dedoplistskaro Municipality. Adults of this species are usually less than 40 cm long. The longest measured snake was about 40 cm. It has no venom and is harmless to people. The IUCN considers it at low risk of extinction (Least Concern). It is often confused with the gentle eirenis (Eirenis modestus), which the same list associates with eastern and central Georgia. Do not expect to find the collared eirenis in every part of Georgia.
habitat: |-
  A 2026 list places it in southeastern Georgia. The specimen shown in the figure is from Taribana, Dedoplistskaro. This atlas’s map marks Kakheti, Tbilisi and Kvemo Kartli. These markings are based on the wording of the list, the Taribana specimen and photos on the site from the Yagluja Hills; they are not a complete list of municipalities. Confidence in the distribution shown on the map is moderate. No specific location in western Georgia or Colchis is named here.

  In general, this species lives in dry, rocky or semi-desert areas, on soft soil containing stones and sand, and on grassy slopes in oak woods. It is often found under a stone or mound of earth, or in a crevice; it is sometimes found alongside blind snakes. The range of elevations at which it occurs in Georgia has not been published separately. Taribana and Yagluja are associated with lowlands and foothills. Published accounts of the species give an elevation of up to about 1,700 metres above sea level, but this should not be taken as an elevation recorded in Georgia.

  Its range includes eastern Turkey, the South Caucasus, Dagestan, Iraq and western Iran. The material used for the original description of the species came from Beshbarmag on the Caspian coast (Azerbaijan), not from Georgia.
diet: >-
  There is no separate study from Georgia on this species’ diet. Reports of what it eats include scorpions, centipedes, worms, beetles and other insects, as well as spiders and small lizards. It mainly eats animals without backbones that live under stones. It has no venom and catches prey with its mouth.
behavior: >-
  It is secretive. In heat it often stays under stones or soil. Hourly and
  seasonal calendars have not been published for Georgia; dated photographs on
  this atlas are from May and July. At species level it lays eggs: literature
  cites 4–8 eggs in summer; hatchlings emerge in August–September at about
  10–13 cm. Mating season and clutch size in Georgia are not measured here.
  Overwintering sites in Georgia are not described separately; species-level
  accounts mention communal hibernation underground. When threatened it usually
  flees. Do not pick it up — it is not venomous, but even a small snake can
  bite. In the same dry southeast you may also meet the ring-headed dwarf snake,
  the smooth snake, the worm snake, and rarely the European cat snake — pupil,
  size, and neck pattern separate them.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists it as Least Concern — the range is wide and no specific global
  threat is singled out. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List; the snakes listed
  there are Dinnik’s viper and the Caucasus viper. The 2026 checklist treats it
  as biologically confirmed; distribution accuracy is scored medium. The
  Georgian picture is still narrow: the named locality is Taribana, and the
  southeastern range is a checklist summary, not a complete map. Local threats
  include habitat change and killing after confusion with a venomous snake. Do
  not pick it up.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a small, slender, nonvenomous snake with round pupils and a dark collar around its neck. Its back is olive or light brown. It is neither a viper nor a worm-like blind snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      Its body is slender and short. It looks more like a small snake than a large racer snake. The head is not clearly distinct from the neck, the tip of the snout is rounded, and the pupil is round. The scales on its back are smooth. Around the middle of its body, it often has 15 rows of scales, sometimes 15–17. The quiet eirenis often has 17–18 rows there. Do not pick up a snake to count its scales. Adults are usually less than 40 cm long. The longest measured snake was about 40 cm.
    - >-
      The back is olive or light brown; each scale often has a pale center and a dark edge. A dark, collar-like band on the neck narrows toward the underside. This is where its Georgian name comes from. Sometimes a faint band runs across the head, with two spots on the back of the head. The belly is yellowish white. The Turkish subspecies E. c. macrospilotus has a row of dark spots on its back. The subspecies listed for Georgia is E. c. collaris.
    - >-
      The modest eirenis (Eirenis modestus) looks similar to this species but is larger, though it is usually under 70 cm long. In adults, the collar-like band around the neck often fades. The modest eirenis is listed in the east and central areas, while this species is listed in the southeast (Dedoplistskaro, Taribana). The smooth snake (Coronella austriaca) has a thicker body and two rows of small spots along its back. It is more often found at forest edges and in damp places. The blind snake (Xerotyphlops vermicularis) looks like a worm and has small eyes. The cat snake (Telescopus fallax) has vertical pupils.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. Its pupil is round. A pupil shaped like a vertical slit points to a viper or a gyurza. If you encounter it, keep your distance and do not pick it up: even a small nonvenomous snake can be easily injured, and an unfamiliar snake may be venomous.
    - >-
      The 2026 list includes it as a confirmed species. The subspecies named here has the same name as the species: E. c. collaris. The specimen mentioned is from Taribana, in Dedoplistskaro Municipality. In Georgia, it is found in the southeast, but not throughout the country. It is often confused with the meek eirenis (Eirenis modestus), which the list associates with eastern and central Georgia.

    The Georgian name საყელოიანი ეირენისი refers to the dark neck band; English

faq:
  - question: Does the collared dwarf snake occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically confirmed in
      southeastern Georgia. The named locality is Taribana, Dedoplistskaro. Do
      not look for it across the whole country.
  - question: Where might I encounter it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      On dry stony ground, often under stones, in the southeast. This atlas marks
      Kakheti, Tbilisi, and Kvemo Kartli. The named scientific locality is
      Taribana; a named photograph is from the Iagluji Hills. There is no named
      western or Colchic point here. Distribution confidence is medium.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. When threatened it usually flees. Even a small snake
      can bite — do not pick it up. A vertical pupil points to a viper or the
      blunt-nosed viper.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A small slender snake, round pupil, smooth scales, olive or light-brown
      back, dark collar on the neck. In the ring-headed dwarf snake the collar
      is often weaker in adults and the checklist range is east–central. The
      worm snake is worm-like. Do not pick it up to count scales.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adults of this species are usually less than 40 cm long. The greatest measured length is about 40 cm. This page does not give a separate length measurement for Georgia.
  - question: What does it eat, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      Based on what is known about the species, it lays 4–8 eggs in summer. What it eats in Georgia has not been studied separately. In general, it eats mainly insects and other animals without backbones that live under rocks. It occasionally eats a small lizard too.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      No separate hour-by-hour calendar has been published for Georgia. It may be found under stones in dry places during the warm season. The photos in this atlas were taken in May and July.
  - question: How does it differ from the ring-headed dwarf snake?
    answer: >-
      Both are small, nonvenomous Eirenis snakes. The collared one has a more distinct collar and is usually shorter than the quiet one. The collared one is listed in the southeast, while the quiet one is listed in the east and center. Around the middle of the body, the collared one often has 15 rows of scales, compared with 17–18 for the quiet one. This is not a feature you should pick up a snake to check.
