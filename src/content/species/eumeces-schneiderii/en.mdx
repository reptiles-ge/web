overview: >-
  Schneider's skink (Eumeces schneiderii) is a large, non-venomous skink confirmed in Georgia in the far south-east of Kakheti. Georgia's 2026 checklist confirms that it truly occurs in the country: the images of the skink come from Vashlovani National Park, and the DNA-checked specimens from Chachuna Managed Reserve. It is not a snake and poses no danger to people. Georgian skinks belong to the Caucasus–Iran form E. s. princeps. Some sources treat this form as a separate species, Novoeumeces princeps, but the Georgian checklist keeps it as a subspecies of Eumeces schneiderii.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, Schneider's Skink is confirmed from dry south-eastern Kakheti, in the country's far south-east. Its home is the Vashlovani–Chachuna area, a land of dry semi-desert and rocky, shrubby ground. Images of the species come from Vashlovani National Park, and DNA-tested specimens from Chachuna Managed Reserve. Both places are in Kakheti, but this does not mean the skink lives throughout Kakheti or across Georgia.
diet: >-
  Schneider's Skink probably feeds on small invertebrates. This is an assumption based on what most skinks eat.
behavior: >-
  Schneider's Skink lays eggs. In the wild, you can tell it by its strong, elongated body, smooth shiny scales, well-developed legs and visible eyelids. Do not pick up a wild lizard: a photo from above and from the side is enough, then leave the animal where it is.
conservation: >-
  In Georgia, Schneider's skink has a small confirmed range in the country's south-east. That is why habitat change, the breaking up of dry open landscapes and accidental killing are practical concerns for it. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats its presence in Georgia as firmly confirmed. Georgian skinks (E. s. princeps) differ sharply from E. s. pavimentatus in one stretch of DNA. Their classification may change in the future, but the Georgian checklist does not yet split them off as a separate species.
interaction: >-
  Schneider's Skink is harmless to people: it has no venom and does not try to attack. Even so, do not catch it or move it somewhere else. If you take a photo, the most useful views are the side of the body, the colouring of the back and the head. Along with the photo, note the exact spot where you saw it.

  traits:
    - >-
      It has a long, strong body with smooth, shiny scales. The legs are fully developed, and the toes are clearly visible. So it is neither a legless lizard nor a snake.
    - >-
      Older descriptions give it an olive-brown back, sometimes with golden-yellow spots or streaks. A pale yellowish stripe often runs along the side. Colour varies, so do not rely on colour alone to identify it.
    - >-
      Including the tail, it may reach roughly 35–37 cm in length. This figure applies to the species across its wide range and is not based on separate measurements of animals in Georgia.
    - >-
      The [Asian Snake-Eyed Skink](ablepharus-pannonicus) is much smaller. Its eyelids are fused and transparent, so it looks as if it cannot blink. Schneider's Skink, by contrast, has an ordinary eye ring and a much sturdier body. To tell the two apart, look at size and the eye.
    - >-
      The [Snake-Eyed Lizard](ophisops-elegans) is also found in dry eastern Georgia. Its “snake-like” eye may confuse you, but it belongs to the true lizard family: it is slimmer and faster, and its scales are different. The [Caucasian Agama](paralaudakia-caucasia) is a broader, flat-headed rock lizard and does not resemble the smooth, tube-round body of Schneider's Skink. So don't judge by the eye alone: look at the body shape and the scales.
    - >-
      In Georgia, Schneider's Skink is confirmed from Vashlovani and Chachuna. If you see a similar animal in western Georgia, high in the mountains or in humid Colchic forest, check especially carefully whether it really is this species.
