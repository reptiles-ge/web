commonName: Golden jackal
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A medium-sized dog-like predator with yellowish-grey fur and a relatively
  short tail. It is a native resident in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The golden jackal (Canis aureus) is a member of the dog family. It is native
  and resident in Georgia, especially common in the lowlands. Head–body length is
  often about 70–85 cm and mass often about 7–15 kg; its tail is shorter than a
  fox’s.
  IUCN assesses the species globally as Least Concern (LC).
habitat: >-
  Golden jackals live in both eastern and western Georgia and are especially
  common in the lowlands, including the Colchian lowlands. Shakarashvili and
  co-authors (2020) report sightings at higher elevations in recent decades, up
  to 2,000 m; that does not make such elevations typical habitat. Near Tbilisi,
  camera traps at Ponichala Nature Reserve recorded the jackal more often than
  any other mammal studied there in 2020–2021 (Kalandarishvili, 2025). A
  photograph from Lagodekhi Protected Areas is dated 2025. The map's regional
  table shows only records entered on this page, not the species' full range in
  Georgia. The species uses varied habitats, including scrub, farmland and
  woodland edges where food and cover are available.
diet: >-
  The golden jackal is a flexible omnivore, eating small mammals, birds, insects,
  fruit, carrion and available scraps. Its diet changes with place and season. In
  a review of European studies, remains of domestic animals and large wild
  mammals came mainly from scavenging; finding such remains does not by itself
  show that a jackal killed the animal. The sources on this page do not measure
  the proportions of foods eaten by jackals in Georgia.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at dusk and at night; it can also be seen by day. At
  Ponichala camera traps (2020–2021) detections were more frequent in the
  morning, about 6 a.m. to noon, than during the afternoon dog peak — that is
  one site, not a national timetable. It often lives as a pair or small family
  group. It marks territory by howling; the call sits between a fox bark and a
  wolf howl and is also used in surveys in Georgia. There is no Georgian audio
  recording on this page; the clip is xeno-canto XC1134910 (Stanislas Wroza). It
  dens in a burrow it digs or takes over. Gestation lasts about 63 days; litters
  usually have several pups, but their number varies. This page has no separate
  breeding data for Georgia. Tracks resemble a dog’s and are larger than a fox’s.
conservation: >-
  The global IUCN assessment (2018) lists the golden jackal as Least Concern
  (LC). Eliava and co-authors (2007) also list it as LC in Georgia; this page
  does not treat it as a threatened taxon on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. It appears
  on the list of hunting species; current seasons and rules should be checked in
  official sources. Shakarashvili and co-authors (2020) report an increase in
  abundance in recent decades, but that does not establish a current national
  population size.
interaction: >-
  A golden jackal is not venomous, but it can bite and rabies is a risk. Do not
  handle a wild animal; keep dogs vaccinated against rabies. After a bite or
  scratch, wash the wound promptly with soap and water and seek medical care
  urgently. Seeing a jackal by day does not, by itself, indicate rabies.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium-sized canid with yellowish-grey or sandy fur, often a darker patch
    across the back, and a relatively short tail that usually has a dark tip. It
    lives year-round in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level head–body is often about 70–85 cm, tail about 20–30 cm,
      shoulder height often about 40–50 cm, mass often about 7–15 kg. Males are
      usually slightly larger than females. There is no separate measured
      Georgian series on this page. The muzzle is broader than a fox’s, the
      ears are erect, and the legs are relatively long.
    - >-
      Colour is yellowish-grey, sandy, or pale brown; the back often has a
      darker “saddle” of mixed black and white hairs. The belly is paler. The
      tail is bushy, typically with a dark tip, not white. Winter fur is longer
      and darker. The African “golden jackal” is a different species (African
      golden wolf, Canis lupaster) and does not occur in Georgia.
    - >-
      The [red fox](vulpes-vulpes) (Vulpes vulpes) also occurs in Georgia: finer
      muzzle, longer bushy tail often with a white tip, black ear backs, and black
      lower legs. The [wolf](canis-lupus) (Canis lupus) is much larger,
      longer-legged, and heavier in the head. A free-ranging dog can
      match size and tracks; ear shape, tail carriage, and voice do not always
      decide. The raccoon dog (Nyctereutes procyonoides) is introduced in
      Georgia: shorter-legged, with a masked face, and has no separate profile
      here yet.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. A bite or saliva can carry rabies risk. Do not handle
      it, especially if an animal approaches without fear, seems disoriented, or
      is aggressive. Daytime activity can also be normal.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758. The accepted name is Canis aureus. Order
    Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Canidae, genus Canis. The type
    locality in the literature is tied to Iran / “the East”, not Georgia.
  - >-
    Since 2015, African populations once treated as the same species are
    classified separately (Canis lupaster). Georgian animals belong to Eurasian
    Canis aureus.
  - >-
    Caucasus and European literature often cites subspecies C. a. moreoticus;
    C. a. caucasica also appears historically. Subspecies boundaries are
    disputed; do not assign a Georgian individual to a subspecies in the field
    with confidence.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual names are ტურა and ჩვეულებრივი ტურა. In English:
    golden jackal; also common jackal, Asiatic jackal, Eurasian golden jackal.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as native (2018 global assessment). It is in
    Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s 2002 annotated mammal list. Eliava et al.
    2007 list it as LC. Shakarashvili et al. 2020 treat most western Georgian
    samples as recent migrants from the east of the country.
faq:
  - question: Is the golden jackal found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. IUCN lists it as native in Georgia. It is on national mammal
      checklists and on the list of hunting species.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Golden jackals live in eastern and western Georgia, especially in the
      lowlands. Research documents them at Ponichala Nature Reserve, and there is
      a photo record from Lagodekhi Protected Areas. This page's map shows the
      records entered here, not their full range.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Look for yellowish-grey fur, an often darker patch across the back, and a
      relatively short, dark-tipped tail. A fox's tail is longer and often
      white-tipped; a wolf is much larger. Colour alone is not enough.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      At species level head–body is often about 70–85 cm, tail about 20–30 cm,
      mass often about 7–15 kg. There is no separate measured Georgian series
      on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It is an omnivore that eats small mammals, birds, insects, fruit and
      carrion. Diet varies with place and season; remains of a domestic animal do
      not always mean the jackal killed it.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      Mainly at dusk and at night; it can also appear by day. It is resident
      and does not migrate like many birds. Howling is often heard.
  - question: Can you hear it?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Territorial howling carries a long way. The clip on this page is
      xeno-canto XC1134910 (Stanislas Wroza); there is no Georgian audio recording
      here.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It can bite, and rabies is a risk. Do not handle it. After a bite or
      scratch, wash the wound promptly with soap and water and seek medical care
      urgently. Daytime activity alone is not a sign of rabies.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      It is globally Least Concern and is not treated on this page as a
      threatened taxon on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. It is a hunting species;
      season and quota are set by official rules.
