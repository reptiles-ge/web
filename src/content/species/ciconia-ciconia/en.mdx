overview: >-
  The white stork (Ciconia ciconia) is a breeding migrant in Georgia. On this
  page’s map table, distribution is confirmed in Samtskhe–Javakheti, Kvemo
  Kartli, and Adjara. Shida Kartli and Abkhazia have only isolated records and
  are not treated as distribution. The 2024 census confirmed breeding in
  Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli; confirmed status in Adjara does not mean
  that census found breeding there. Globally it is Least Concern (LC), but
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List classifies it as Vulnerable (VU, D1) under the name
  ლაკლაკი.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia during the breeding season, look for white storks in settlements
  of Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli, near mountain grasslands, subalpine
  lakes and wetlands. The distribution map also confirms Adjara alongside those
  two regions. Shida Kartli and Abkhazia are recorded only in the table and are
  not treated as distribution. Breeding pairs were found in 43 of 59 settlements
  surveyed in 2024. Every counted nest was in a settlement, on a roof, pole or
  other support.
diet: >-
  In open meadows and wet areas, it catches invertebrates, amphibians,
  reptiles, small mammals and fish. It walks with its head angled down and
  grabs prey with its bill. These are general features of the species; the
  sources cited here do not include a separate study of its diet in Georgia.
behavior: >-
  Active by day, it often soars high on rising warm air. In flight it extends
  its neck rather than tucking it in like a heron. Its most noticeable sound
  at the nest is bill-clattering. Both adults build or repair the nest,
  incubate the eggs and regurgitate food for the young into the nest. In
  Georgia’s 2024 census, most nests had two or three nestlings. Many migratory
  populations winter in Africa, but these sources do not establish the precise
  route of Georgia’s breeding birds. The recording on this page was made outside
  Georgia.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Ciconia ciconia as Least Concern globally.
  Georgia’s current 2014 Red List classifies it as Vulnerable (VU, D1: small
  population) under the name ლაკლაკი. The 2024 bird checklist gives a national
  assessment of LC, but this reflects a draft
  update and does not replace the legal 2014 listing. The 2024 census counted
  202 occupied nests, compared with 105 known in 2018–2020. These totals are
  not directly comparable: the 2024 survey added 36 settlements. Among 23
  settlements surveyed in both periods, a 40% increase was not statistically
  significant. The study identifies possible future wind-energy development
  in Samtskhe–Javakheti as a risk. Collision and electrocution on power lines
  are known risks for the species, but these sources give no separate casualty
  total for Georgia.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people. In Georgia it nests in
  villages, so close contact with people is normal. The bill is strong — do not
  approach, feed, or pick up nestlings. Moving a nest or taking it off a line
  is specialist work, not a casual intervention.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large white bird with black wing tips, a long red bill and red legs. In
    Georgia it is a breeding migrant on the 2024 checklist, nesting in two
    southern regions. The usual confusion is black stork and great white egret.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 100–115 cm at species level; wingspan is often given around
      155–215 cm. Mass is often about 2.3–4.5 kg. A separate measured series
      from Georgia is not given on this page. The body and neck are white; the
      flight feathers are black and obvious in the air. Adults have a red bill
      and red legs. Skin around the eye is dark. Females are on average a
      little smaller than males; sex is not reliably told by size in the field.
    - >-
      Juveniles have a dark or brownish-black bill, not clean red; the legs are
      also darker. That is the most useful age mark at close range. Flight
      silhouette: long outstretched neck, long legs trailing, broad wings with
      a black trailing edge. A heron tucks the neck in flight.
    - >-
      Black stork (Ciconia nigra) is also a breeding migrant on the 2024
      checklist (BB; M); the checklist codes it NT nationally, while the 2014
      Red List codes it VU under the Georgian name ყარყატი. The back, neck and
      wings are dark, the belly white; it nests in forest and river valleys,
      not on village roofs. Great white egret (Ardea alba) is all white, without
      black flight feathers, with a yellow bill and an S-shaped neck. Great
      white and Dalmatian pelicans are much larger, with a pouched bill.
      Oriental stork (Ciconia boyciana) is not on the 2024 checklist. This atlas
      does not yet have a separate black stork page.
    - >-
      Georgian names clash. The 2014 list calls C. ciconia ლაკლაკი and C. nigra
      ყარყატი. This page uses თეთრი ყარყატი as the main name because it states
      the colour; ლაკლაკი is the official alternative. Bare „ყარყატი“ in legal
      text means the black species. It is not a threat to people. A white
      black-winged bird with a red bill on a village pole is this species.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758, originally as Ardea ciconia. The 2024
    Georgian checklist follows the IOC World Bird List and the OSME Region List
    for names: order Ciconiiformes, family Ciconiidae, genus Ciconia. It is
    entered as Ciconia ciconia ciconia.
  - >-
    Widely recognised subspecies are C. c. ciconia (Europe, north-west Africa,
    western Asia) and C. c. asiatica (Central Asia). The nominate form is the
    one the 2024 checklist assigns to Georgia. Field identification is to
    species.
  - >-
    Georgian is თეთრი ყარყატი; the official 2014 Red List name is ლაკლაკი.
    English is white stork; the 2024 checklist uses Western White Stork to
    separate it from Ciconia boyciana.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: BB; M, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC (the national figure reflects a draft update), occurrence quality H,
    evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    status. The map on this page takes regions from the records table and treats
    only confirmed status as distribution. Breeding localities come from the
    separate 2024 census — Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli — not from a
    checklist gazetteer. Black stork is listed separately as BB; M.
faq:
  - question: Is the white stork found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed breeding bird and passage migrant (BB; M).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The map table confirms distribution in Samtskhe–Javakheti, Kvemo Kartli,
      and Adjara. Shida Kartli and Abkhazia have only isolated records and are
      not treated as distribution. For breeding, the 2024 census names
      settlements in Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli; it counted 202
      occupied nests in 43 settlements.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Large white body, black flight feathers, long red bill and legs. The neck
      is stretched out in flight. A black stork has a dark back. A great white
      egret lacks black wing tips and tucks the neck in flight.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 100–115 cm long at species level, with a wingspan often given
      around 155–215 cm. A separate measured series from Georgia is not given
      on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Prey it can catch in open, often wet ground: invertebrates, amphibians,
      and small vertebrates. Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia on
      this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the breeding season and on passage. The 2024 checklist does not
      classify it as a winter visitor; exact arrival and departure dates are
      not given here.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. BB on the 2024 checklist means it is a confirmed breeder. The 2024
      census found breeding only in Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli, on
      supports in settlements.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. It is globally Least Concern. The 2014 Red List lists it
      as VU (ლაკლაკი). The 2024 checklist’s national LC reflects a draft
      reassessment. Do not disturb wild birds or nests.
