location: Breeding migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A small migratory dove with chestnut-and-black scaly upperparts and a
  black-and-white striped neck patch. Confirmed in Georgia as a breeding
  migrant on the 2024 national bird checklist; Vulnerable globally and
  nationally on that list.
overview: >-
  The European turtle dove (Streptopelia turtur) is a small dove (family
  Columbidae) confirmed in Georgia as a breeding migrant. The 2024 annotated
  checklist of the birds of Georgia codes it BB; M: a breeder absent in the
  non-breeding season, and also a bird of passage. The Iliauni SPA country
  checklist also codes it BB, M. Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code
  A). Occurrence quality is high (H), with photographs and checklist evidence.
  Adults are about 26–28 cm long at species level. The usual Georgian name is
  გვრიტი; ჩვეულებრივი გვრიტი is also used in official documents. It is not a
  threat to people. IUCN lists the species as Vulnerable. The 2024 checklist
  assigns global and national category VU. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List
  (Government Resolution No. 190).
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in the breeding season and on spring and autumn
  passage, not in winter on the 2024 checklist. Species-level habitat is open
  and semi-open country: woodland edge, scrub, tall hedges, orchards, farmland
  with weeds and seed, and river valleys with bushes. Nests are usually a thin
  platform of twigs in scrub or a low tree. A named Georgian locality is Batumi
  (Adjara): Iliauni SPA N 16 lists it only as a migrant (M), not as a breeder
  at that site. The CMS action-plan background notes that the Batumi Raptor
  Count recorded hundreds of passage birds in September–October 2016. Iliauni
  SPA N 17 (Kolkheti; Guria–Samegrelo) also lists it as M — that is site-level
  passage, not a map of those whole regions. The 2024 checklist confirms
  country-level breeding-migrant status but does not publish a regional
  gazetteer. The atlas map colours only Adjara because of the named Batumi
  record; do not assume it occurs only there, and do not assume even occurrence
  on treeless high plateaus or in dense closed forest. A separate national
  elevation band for Georgia is not published here.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range
  adults feed mainly on seeds, especially small weed and cereal seeds taken on
  the ground; some invertebrates are also taken. Nestlings are fed crop milk
  (a nutritious secretion from the crop of both parents). That is species-level
  biology, not a measured Georgian menu.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. The song is a soft, rhythmic purring “turr-turr”, often
  given from a perch in scrub or a tree; that call is the origin of the English
  name “turtle”. The recording on this page is xeno-canto XC1153227 (Arjun
  Dutta, Fen Drayton Lakes RSPB, Cambridgeshire; CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). It is song,
  not a Georgian recording. Flight is fast and direct, with a dark wedge-shaped tail
  bordered white. Courtship includes display flights and bowing. The nest is a
  flimsy twig platform. Species-level clutches are usually two white eggs;
  incubation is about 13–14 days and both parents sit; young leave the nest
  after about two weeks — those figures are from the wider range, not a
  Georgian nest sample. Northern and temperate populations are long-distance
  migrants wintering mainly in the Sahel belt of Africa. The Black Sea–Eastern
  Mediterranean flyway is one documented route for eastern birds; Georgia sits
  on that broader corridor. The 2016 Batumi autumn count is named passage
  evidence, not a claim that every coastal watch site is a proven hotspot.
  Exact arrival and departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated here.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Streptopelia turtur as Vulnerable on the
  IUCN Red List because of a rapid, large-scale decline in European breeding
  populations. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global and national
  category VU. The species is not listed on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government
  Resolution No. 190) among the bird taxa in that decree. A later Ministry
  order on practical measures for threatened species (Order No. 2-804) names
  ჩვეულებრივი გვრიტი (Streptopelia turtur) with international and national
  status VU and lists pressures including unregulated or excessive harvest,
  especially on migration corridors, possible competition or hybridisation with
  Eurasian collared dove, destruction of shelterbelts, and burning of grain
  fields. Those are policy statements, not a national census. Streptopelia
  turtur is not on the Environmental Supervision Department’s published hunting
  season list; Eurasian collared dove (Streptopelia decaocto) is listed there
  from the fourth Saturday of August to 15 February, with a daily limit of 5 —
  check the live DES notice. The CMS background document cites a Georgian bag
  estimate for 1966–1970 (about 19–60 thousand) and 2015 season rules; those
  are historical figures, not the current DES list. Species-level drivers of
  decline elsewhere include agricultural intensification and loss of seed-rich
  foraging habitat, disease (including trichomonosis in some populations), and
  hunting along migration and wintering routes. A current pair count for
  Georgia is not given on this page. Do not catch or shoot wild turtle doves.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people. It is not venomous. Do not catch, keep, or
  hunt wild birds. Leave nests alone. On the current DES season list this
  species is not a game bird; do not confuse it with collared dove, which is
  listed separately.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small migratory dove with chestnut-and-black scaly upperparts, a
    black-and-white striped neck patch, and a purring “turr-turr” song. In
    Georgia it is a breeding migrant on the 2024 checklist, Vulnerable globally
    and nationally on that list. The usual confusion is Eurasian collared dove.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 26–28 cm and wingspan about 47–53 cm at species level. Mass
      is often given around 100–170 g. A separate measured series from Georgia
      is not given on this page. The body is slender for a dove. Upperparts are
      mottled chestnut and black (a scaly or tortoiseshell look). The head and
      underparts are blue-grey to pinkish-grey; the breast often shows a soft
      pink wash. Each side of the neck has a patch of fine black-and-white
      stripes. The eye has a reddish orbital ring in adults. The tail is dark
      with a clear white tip and sides, obvious in flight and when the bird
      fans the tail.
    - >-
      Juveniles are duller and lack the neat neck patch until they moult.
      Sexes are similar in the field. The song is a soft, repeated purr —
      “turr-turr” — not the three-note coo of collared dove. Birds often feed
      on the ground in open patches and flush with a sharp wing rattle.
    - >-
      Eurasian collared dove (Streptopelia decaocto) is a year-round resident
      and migrant in Georgia on the 2024 checklist (YR-R; M). It is paler,
      more uniform grey-buff, with a single black half-collar on the nape, not
      a striped side-neck patch, and it lacks the chestnut-black scaly back.
      Oriental turtle dove / rufous turtle dove (Streptopelia orientalis meena)
      is listed as a migrant only (M), with photographic evidence; it is
      larger and darker, with heavy scaling on the underparts and a different
      neck pattern — treat uncertain autumn birds carefully. Laughing dove
      (Spilopelia senegalensis) is coded as introduced and year-round resident
      (INT; YR-R): smaller, warmer pink-brown, with a spotted neck patch, not
      black-and-white stripes. Common woodpigeon (Columba palumbus) is much
      larger, with a white wing bar and neck patch of a different shape — see
      that species page in this atlas. This atlas does not yet have separate
      pages for collared dove, oriental turtle dove, or laughing dove.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. A small dove with a scaly chestnut back and
      a purring song in Georgia in late spring or summer is this species, not
      the pale collared dove of towns and villages.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Columba turtur. It is now placed in
    Streptopelia (Bonaparte, 1855). The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the IOC
    World Bird List and the OSME Region List for names: order Columbiformes,
    family Columbidae, genus Streptopelia. English checklist name is European
    Turtle Dove.
  - >-
    Four subspecies are widely recognised: S. t. turtur (Europe east through
    Asia Minor toward western Siberia), S. t. arenicola (North Africa and from
    Iraq–Iran east into Central Asia), plus more local Saharan and Nile forms
    (hoggara, rufescens). Handbook ranges place both turtur and arenicola in
    the broader Caucasus–Near East region. Field identification in Georgia is
    to species. A measured Georgian specimen series assigned to subspecies is
    not given on this page.
  - >-
    Georgian is გვრიტი; ჩვეულებრივი გვრიტი appears in official conservation
    documents. English names in use are European turtle dove, European
    turtle-dove, and turtle dove. Russian is обыкновенная горлица.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: BB; M, data quality A, global and national Red List
    VU, occurrence quality H, evidence PH; CH (photograph and checklist). The
    Iliauni SPA country checklist also codes BB, M. That is country-level
    confirmation, not a complete regional map. Collared dove, oriental turtle
    dove, and laughing dove are listed as separate species on the same
    checklist.
faq:
  - question: Is the European turtle dove found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed breeding bird and migrant (BB; M). It is absent in the
      non-breeding season.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the warmer months in open and semi-open country with scrub, hedges,
      woodland edge, or farmland with seed. A named passage locality is Batumi
      (Adjara); Iliauni also lists it as a migrant on Kolkheti SPA
      (Guria–Samegrelo). This page has no complete municipality list. Presence
      is confirmed at country level; the map colours only Adjara because of the
      named Batumi record.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Chestnut-and-black scaly upperparts, black-and-white striped neck patch,
      white-edged dark tail, and a soft purring “turr-turr” song. Collared dove
      is paler with a single black nape collar and no scaly chestnut back.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 26–28 cm long at species level, with a wingspan of about 47–53 cm.
      A separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly seeds taken on the ground; chicks are fed crop milk. Diet has not
      been studied separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      As a breeding migrant: present in the breeding season and on passage,
      absent in winter, according to the 2024 checklist. A September–October
      2016 count at Batumi documents autumn passage. Exact arrival and
      departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated on this page.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. BB on the 2024 checklist means it is a confirmed breeder. Named nest
      sites inside Georgia are not listed on this page. The Batumi and Kolkheti
      SPA lists code this species there only as a migrant (M).
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless to people. It is globally Vulnerable and coded VU
      nationally on the 2024 checklist. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List
      (Decree No. 190). It is not on the current DES hunting-season list;
      collared dove is listed separately. Do not catch or hunt wild turtle
      doves.
