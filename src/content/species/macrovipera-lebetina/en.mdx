commonName: Levantine viper
location: Eastern Georgia
description: >-
  Eastern Georgia’s high-risk venomous viper — dorsal blotches alone are not
  enough to identify the Levantine viper.
overview: |-
  The gyurza (Macrovipera lebetinus) is a large [venomous viper](/venomous-snakes) found mainly in the dry and semi-dry parts of eastern Georgia. It cannot be [reliably identified](/snakes/shxamiani-gvelis-amocnoba) by its back markings or size alone; do not approach it. This atlas uses the name Macrovipera lebetinus; some sources also use Macrovipera lebetina or Vipera lebetina.
habitat: |-
  The gyurza is found in dry, stony and rocky places. In Georgia, it lives in dry areas along the lower reaches of the Iori, Mtkvari and Alazani rivers, in Shiraki and Gareji, and in the dry foothills of [Kvemo Kartli](/regions/kvemo-kartli). It is rare near [Tbilisi](/regions/tbilisi) and Rustavi. This atlas’s map marks the gyurza in [Kakheti](/regions/kakheti), Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. Confirmed locations include Kvareli, the area around Nekresi Monastery, the Alazani Valley and the Colorful Desert.
  As of 2026, the area where the gyurza is found had expanded over recent decades from Georgia’s far southeast to the Alazani Valley and Tbilisi. The map has only isolated records for Tbilisi. Records associated with Rustavi mostly concern dry areas of Kvemo Kartli along the Mtkvari. For Tbilisi, this atlas lists the Tbilisi Botanical Garden, Tabori, Nadzaladevi and Saburtalo; toward Rustavi, it lists Rustavi, Gardabani and the Jandari Reservoir. Such a record only confirms that the gyurza could be found in a particular place in a particular year. It does not mean the snake is widespread or frequently seen in every part of the city.
  The gyurza shelters in cracks in rocks, beneath stones and in rodent burrows; it sometimes enters structures. During intense summer heat, it may appear near water. In Georgia, it is found up to about 1,500 metres in elevation. Its natural presence in western Georgia has not been confirmed. The high mountains of the Greater Caucasus and the Colchic forest are not places where it usually lives.
diet: >-
  Adult gyurzas mainly eat rodents, but they also catch small birds, lizards and other small animals with backbones. They usually hunt by lying in wait. Sometimes they climb bushes or trees.
behavior: |-
  In the South Caucasus, the gyurza is usually active from April to the end of October, although some sources say it may appear as early as late March. When you might encounter it depends on the weather and daytime temperature: in spring and autumn, it often moves around during the day, while in the summer heat it is more active at dusk and at night. In very hot weather, an encounter is also more likely near water. It spends the winter in rock crevices or burrows.
  The gyurza lays eggs, unlike other vipers found in Georgia. It usually mates in April and May. In summer, it lays about 15–20 eggs. The young hatch after about 35–45 days and are about 23–25 cm long.
  The gyurza does not seek out people and usually defends itself when it feels threatened. If you encounter one, keep a safe distance and do not try to catch it.
conservation: |-
  A 2021 assessment found that the gyurza faces a low risk of extinction as a species. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List, although older Georgian encyclopedias sometimes associate it with the Red Book. The vipers listed there include [Dinnik’s viper](vipera-dinniki) and the [Caucasian viper](vipera-kaznakovi).
  The gyurza is threatened by changes to its habitat, expanding settlements, excessive livestock grazing, and people killing it deliberately. In dry areas, it hunts rodents and is an important part of the local food chain.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The gyurza is a thick-bodied viper. It has a broad, triangular head and vertically elongated pupils. A raised ridge runs down the middle of each scale. If only its spotted back is visible from a distance, it may resemble a Urartu racer or a multicolored racer.
  traits:
    - >-
      The gyurza has a thick, heavy body. Its broad, triangular head is clearly distinct from its neck. It has a blunt nose and no horn. Gyurzas are usually 70–90 cm long, though large ones can reach 1.5 meters. Reports of gyurzas longer than 2 meters are disputed.
    - >-
      The pupil looks like a vertical slit. Each scale on the back has a prominent raised line down the middle, making the back look rough. The scales on the head, including above the eyes, are mostly small.
    - >-
      It may be gray, sand-colored, or brownish. Its back often has dark spots or bands running across it. These sometimes form a zigzag pattern. Its belly is lighter and often dotted with small dark spots. Some have a more even color.
    - >-
      The [Urartu racer](elaphe-urartica) has a thick body and large, rounded dark patches on its back. The dark-spotted pattern of the [variegated racer](hemorrhois-ravergieri) can also resemble a gyurza’s, especially when the snake’s head is out of view. Both racers have round pupils; a gyurza’s are vertical. Do not approach a snake to look at its pupils.
    - >-
      The [steppe viper](vipera-renardi) is also found in eastern Georgia, but it is much smaller than the gyurza. It is usually about 40–60 cm long. The [nose-horned viper](vipera-transcaucasiana) has a horn on its nose, making it easy to recognize. It is found in more mountainous areas. [Dinnik’s viper](vipera-dinniki) lives in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus and is not found where the gyurza usually lives.
faq:
  - question: Is the Levantine viper found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. As of 2026, the gyurza has been confirmed in Georgia. It is found mainly in the dry and semi-dry areas of eastern Georgia and is not found throughout the country.
  - question: Are “gyurza”, “giurza” and გიუზა the same snake?
    answer: >-
      Yes, “gyuza” is a common alternative spelling of “gyurza.” In this atlas, the species Macrovipera lebetinus is called “gyurza.”
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, the gyurza is found mainly along the lower reaches of the Iori, Mtkvari and Alazani rivers, in Shiraki and Gareji, and in the dry foothills of Kvemo Kartli. It is rare around Tbilisi and Rustavi. This atlas’s map marks it in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. Its presence has also been confirmed in Kvareli, near Nekresi Monastery and in the Colorful Desert. It is not known to occur naturally in western Georgia.
  - question: Does the Levantine viper occur in Tbilisi or Rustavi?
    answer: >-
      Yes, but rarely — mostly in dry, open, or rocky places. In Tbilisi, the gyurza has been recorded in the Tbilisi Botanical Garden, on Tabor, and in Nadzaladevi and Saburtalo. Toward Rustavi, records list Rustavi, Gardabani, and Jandari Reservoir. These records only confirm that the gyurza was recorded in those places in specific years. They do not mean it is often found in every part of the city.
  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      The gyurza is a venomous snake. Among snakes found in Georgia, its bite is considered one of the most serious threats to human health. It is a large snake and may inject a large amount of venom when it bites. Its venom can cause bleeding, interfere with blood clotting and damage the area around the bite. The gyurza does not usually attack people. It bites when it feels threatened — if someone gets too close, steps on it or tries to catch it. If bitten, call 112 immediately.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
      If someone is bitten by a gyurza, call 112 immediately. Have the person lie down and reassure them. Try to keep them as still as possible. Do not cut the bite, suck out the venom or tie anything tightly around the bitten area. The person needs treatment in a hospital. If medicine to counter the venom is needed, only a doctor should choose and give it.
  - question: When is the Levantine viper active?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia and the South Caucasus, the gyurza is usually active from April through the end of October. It has also been spotted in late March. In spring and autumn, it often moves around during the day. In the summer heat, it is more likely to appear at dusk or at night. Its activity at any given time also depends on the weather, temperature and location.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      The gyurza is a large, thick-bodied snake. It has a broad, triangular head and vertically elongated pupils. Its scales have a raised line down the middle. It has no horn on its snout. Urartu and multicolored racers have round pupils. Do not identify a snake by the spots on its back alone, and do not approach it.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      The gyurza is usually 70–90 cm long, though large ones can reach 1.5 metres. In Georgia, it is the [largest viper](/snakes/didi-gvelebi). Reports that it can grow longer than 2 metres are disputed.
  - question: Does it lay eggs or give live birth?
    answer: >-
      The gyurza lays eggs, while Vipera vipers found in Georgia give birth to live young. Gyurzas usually mate in April–May and lay about 15–20 eggs in summer.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Watch the snake from a safe distance and leave it where it is. Do not try to catch or kill it. If you give it room and do not disturb it, the gyurza will usually move away from you on its own.
