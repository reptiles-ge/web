commonName: "Sand Lizard"
location: "Western Georgia; east above 600–800 m"
description: >-
  A harmless lacertid. Widespread in western Georgia; in the east only above
  about 600–800 m.
overview: >-
  The sand lizard (Lacerta agilis) is a harmless lizard confirmed in Georgia —
  widespread in the west, and in the east only above about 600–800 m. It belongs
  to the true lizard family (Lacertidae). It has no venom and is not dangerous
  to people. Adults at species level usually have a snout–vent length of about
  7–9 cm; total length is often about 18–25 cm. This page has no measured
  Georgian series. IUCN lists it as Least Concern. It is not on Georgia’s 2014
  Red List. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed.
  Linnaeus described it in 1758. The range is broad in western Eurasia; Georgia
  sits near the south-eastern edge, not at the centre.
habitat: >-
  It occurs in Georgia. Do not map it to the whole country or to the eastern
  lowlands. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 place it throughout western Georgia; in
  the east only above about 600–800 m. Distribution confidence is medium. The
  checklist figure is from Ivlita (Akhaltsikhe Municipality, Samtskhe–Javakheti).
  Barcodes are from Tskruti (Samtskhe–Javakheti) and Tianeti (Mtskheta–Mtianeti).
  This atlas map marks western regions plus those two eastern highland regions.
  It does not fill Tbilisi, Kvemo Kartli or the Kakheti lowland from that
  elevation rule. IUCN places subspecies grusinica on the Black Sea coast, in
  the Colchic lowland and in Adjara, and ioriensis (spelled iorensis in IUCN)
  in the upper Iori valley. Doronin et al. 2019 found it on the northern edge
  of Tianeti at about 1,119–1,185 m and not more than 2 km away. At species
  level the habitat is meadow, shrub, woodland edge, traditional farmland and,
  in the west, coastal and Colchic lowland — not only the sandy dunes of
  north-west Europe. Look for it in open sun with nearby cover (grass, bushes,
  stone piles). On bare vertical rock, Darevskia is more likely.
diet: >-
  This page has no Georgian stomach-content study. At species level it eats
  small invertebrates — beetles, spiders, flying insects and similar prey. That
  is a general diet, not a menu for every Georgian site. Do not treat it as
  garden pest control.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day: it basks and hunts. IUCN, at species level, has animals
  leaving winter shelter around March–April in the south and in mountains. In
  the Caucasus, Doronin et al. 2019 note peak activity of green Lacerta in May.
  These are literature windows, not a calendar for every Georgian site. It lays
  eggs. At species level a female lays one or two clutches a year of about
  4–14 eggs; incubation is about 50–55 days; hatchlings of the first clutch
  appear in July–August, a second clutch in September–October (Agasyan et al.
  2010). Georgian clutch size is not published here. If caught, the tail may
  break off; it may also bite. Watch it and leave it where it is.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assessed it as Least Concern in 2009 (Agasyan et al. 2010; population
  trend decreasing, but the global range is wide). It is not on Georgia’s 2014
  Red List (Resolution No. 190). Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat it as
  biologically confirmed. It is listed on Appendix II of the Bern Convention;
  that framework does not automatically apply EU Habitats Directive rules in
  Georgia. Local status differs: the checklist describes a wide western range,
  while the Iori-valley form is very restricted. Doronin et al. 2019 suggest a
  single population near Tianeti of fewer than 1,000 animals — a local estimate,
  not a national species status. Species-level threats include habitat
  conversion, intensive agriculture and road mortality; Georgia-specific
  pressure is not measured here. It is not on CITES. Do not catch wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and poses no medical risk. If handled it may bite; the
  tail may break off. Leave it in place. Do not keep wild animals.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium, stocky ground lacertid. Males in spring often have green flanks;
    females are brownish with dark spots that have pale centres. Compare the
    striped lizard and Caucasian green lizard — colour alone is not enough.
  traits:
    - >-
      A robust lacertid with well-developed limbs — not a Darevskia rock lizard
      and not a skink. The head is relatively short and deep compared with the
      large green lizards. The collar scales are serrated. In the field, size,
      pattern, habitat and elevation matter more than scale counts. Do not pick
      the lizard up to count scales.
    - >-
      Colour changes with sex and season. Breeding males become green on the
      flanks, sometimes more extensively. Females are usually brown or grey,
      often with dark dorsal spots that have pale centres (ocelli). Juveniles
      resemble females, often with stronger stripes or spots. Caucasian animals
      can be greener than western European ones — so a green adult is not
      automatically a striped lizard or a Caucasian green lizard.
    - >-
      The striped lizard (Lacerta strigata) is a semiarid-ground animal. It is
      not described as elevation-restricted in the east. Near Tianeti in 2018
      both were found at one site; L. strigata was more numerous. Juveniles of
      both can be striped. Do not identify by colour alone.
    - >-
      The Caucasian green lizard (Lacerta media) is a larger green lacertid,
      mainly in eastern semiarid country and sporadically in the north-west.
      The checklist treats it as a candidate species. Where both occur, compare
      size and build; green adults are easily confused.
    - >-
      The Artvin lizard (Darevskia derjugini) is smaller, lives on the forest
      floor and is common in the west. Rock lizards (Darevskia) are usually on
      rock or walls. None of these lizards is venomous.
faq:
  - question: Does the sand lizard occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. Widespread
      in the west; in the east above about 600–800 m.
  - question: Where might I find it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the west — meadow, woodland edge, shrub, coast and Colchic lowland. In
      the east — at elevation, not on the Kakheti or Tbilisi lowland. Named
      points are Ivlita, Tskruti and Tianeti.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A medium, stocky lacertid. Males in spring often have green flanks;
      females are brownish with dark spots that have pale centres. Colour alone
      does not separate it from the striped lizard or Caucasian green lizard.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. If handled it may bite; the tail may break off.
      Leave it where it is.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Head–body length at species level is usually about 7–9 cm; total length
      is often about 18–25 cm. This page has no measured Georgian series.
  - question: How does it differ from the striped lizard?
    answer: >-
      In the east L. agilis is tied to elevation; L. strigata is a semiarid
      lacertid. Near Tianeti they can occur together. Juveniles of both can be
      striped.
  - question: Which subspecies occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The checklist names three: grusinica in the west, brevicaudata in the
      south and centre, ioriensis in the east (Iori / Tianeti). For field
      identification the species is enough; COI does not fully split the
      subspecies.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      By day. At species level emergence in the south and in mountains is
      around March–April; peak activity of green Lacerta in the Caucasus is
      described in May. That is not a calendar for every site.
