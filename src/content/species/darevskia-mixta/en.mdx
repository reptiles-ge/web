overview: >-
  The mixed rock lizard (Darevskia mixta) occurs in Georgia as a harmless rock
  lizard of humid western forest gorges, but not in Adjara. It belongs to the
  true lizards (Lacertidae). It has no venom and is not dangerous to people.
  The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed, not as a
  candidate. The type locality is Abastumani. Snout–vent length is often about
  5–6 cm. IUCN lists it as Near Threatened. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red
  List. Do not assume it throughout the country: named modern points are
  Sairme, Tchkhorotskhu and Baniskhevi. This atlas map marks Samegrelo — Zemo
  Svaneti, Imereti and Samtskhe — Javakheti from those records; it does not
  mean the species is limited to three points. English names include Ajarian
  lizard and mixed rock lizard; the checklist prefers Georgian lizard because
  the species is absent from Adjara.
habitat: >-
  It occurs in Georgia. Do not assume it in Adjara, the Colchis lowlands, or
  the dry eastern plains. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 figure a specimen from
  Sairme; COI barcodes are from Sairme and Tchkhorotskhu; a Baniskhevi sequence
  is named in the account. Distribution confidence is medium. Gabelaia and
  co-authors (2015) place the range in the eastern Meskheti Range and on the
  southern slope of the Greater Caucasus between the Rioni and Khobi valleys;
  their 26 named sites include the Zekari Pass, Borjomi Gorge, the Rioni
  valley and the Samegrelo Range. They did not find it in the lowlands or below
  about 250–280 m. Habitat is humid forest gorge with rock and stone used for
  basking and shelter — not open dry steppe. Humidity and temperature in the
  microhabitat matter; it avoids dry or overly warm sites (Tarkhnishvili 2012).
  Repeated surveys of the Likhi Range did not confirm it, though another rock
  lizard (D. obscura / rudis s.l.) occurs there. Surveys in Adjara and west of
  the Abastumani Gorge in Gabelaia et al. 2015 also failed to confirm it. A
  single old Giresun (Turkey) specimen is treated as a likely misidentification;
  later reports without exact sites are not treated as confirmed on this page.
  This atlas does not currently map other administrative regions; that does
  not mean every suitable gorge has been surveyed.
diet: >-
  This page has no Georgian stomach-content study for the species. As a rock
  lizard it is expected to take small invertebrates, but the menu is not
  measured here. It has no venom; prey is seized in the mouth.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. It is a rock lizard: it basks on stones, hides in
  crevices and is territorial. A Georgian hourly or seasonal calendar is not
  on this page. It lays eggs. It is the matrilineal ancestor of the
  parthenogens Armenian rock lizard (D. armeniaca) and Dahl’s lizard (D. dahli);
  those are different taxa and are more likely in southern or eastern Georgia,
  not automatically in the same western gorge. If grabbed, the tail may break
  and the lizard may bite. Watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it
  up.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assessed it as Near Threatened in 2009 (Tuniyev and co-authors). That
  assessment is old; the range is narrow and tied to humid forest gorges. It
  is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190). Tarkhnishvili et al.
  2026 treat it as biologically confirmed. A Georgian population trend is not
  measured here. It is not listed on CITES. Do not take wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and poses no medical risk. If handled it may bite; the
  tail may break. Leave it where it is. Do not keep wild animals.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small rock lizard of western humid gorges. Colour is not enough. Field ID
    needs scalation, size and range. It is absent from Adjara.
  traits:
    - >-
      It lives on rock and stone, not as a ground forest lizard. Snout–vent
      length is often about 4.9–6.3 cm (Darevsky 1967; Gabelaia et al. 2015).
      Breeding males often have an emerald-green back and dark flanks. That is
      typical appearance and does not separate species by colour.
    - >-
      A nearly diagnostic trait is one large scale (the post-central temporal,
      PCT) between the central temporal scale and the ear (tympanal) scale.
      Preanal plates are often paired; an unpaired preanal is more common in
      Greater Caucasus animals. Do not pick the lizard up to count scales.
      Rarely D. caucasica can show a similar PCT — scalation alone is not
      enough in a contact zone.
    - >-
      Clark's rock lizard (D. clarkorum) is the closest relative but is
      geographically isolated — in the southwest, e.g. the Charnali Gorge;
      the Adjara candidate adjarica is in the parvula complex at Shuakhevi.
      mixta is absent from Adjara. It has a narrow contact zone with the
      Caucasian lizard (D. caucasica) in the Greater Caucasus; mtDNA is close —
      colour will not tell them apart.
    - >-
      Derjugin’s lizard (D. derjugini) may occur in the same gorge but lives
      on the ground in forest, with a less flattened head — not on a rock
      face. mixta was once treated as a hybrid of derjugini and saxicola; it
      is now a separate species. Samegrelo also has other rock lizards (e.g.
      D. brauneri); colour will not separate those either. Checklist figures
      show head scalation and preanal plates (Sairme).
    - >-
      The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed, not as
      a candidate. It is endemic to western Georgia excluding Adjara. Named
      modern points are Sairme, Tchkhorotskhu and Baniskhevi. mixta is absent
      from Adjara. Clark's rock lizard (D. clarkorum) is geographically
      isolated — in the southwest, e.g. the Charnali valley.

faq:
  - question: Is the mixed rock lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is biologically confirmed in the 2026 checklist. It is endemic to
      western Georgia, excluding Adjara. Figure: Sairme. Barcodes: Sairme and
      Tchkhorotskhu; also Baniskhevi.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In humid forest gorges on rock and stone — eastern Meskheti Range,
      Borjomi Gorge, and the southern Greater Caucasus slope between the Rioni
      and Khobi. Named points include Sairme, Tchkhorotskhu, Baniskhevi and
      Abastumani. Do not look in Adjara, the lowlands or the Likhi Range.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      It is a small rock lizard. Males often have a green back and dark flanks.
      Colour is not enough. Range and one large scale between the temple and
      the ear plate are more reliable. Compare it with clarkorum in Adjara,
      caucasica in the Greater Caucasus, and ground-dwelling derjugini.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. If grabbed it may bite; the tail may break. Leave
      it where it is.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Snout–vent length is often about 5–6 cm (about 4.9–6.3 cm in the
      literature). Total length including the tail is not measured on this
      page.
  - question: Is this the Adjarian rock lizard?
    answer: >-
      No. mixta is absent from Adjara. adjarica is a candidate in the parvula
      complex at Shuakhevi. The English name Ajarian lizard is misleading; the
      checklist prefers Georgian lizard.
  - question: Is it a candidate species?
    answer: >-
      No. It is biologically confirmed in the checklist. It was once treated as
      a hybrid; it is now a species with a narrow contact zone with caucasica.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      By day. A full Georgian seasonal calendar is not on this page. In humid
      gorges it is expected in the warm season.
