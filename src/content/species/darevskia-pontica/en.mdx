commonName: "Pontic Meadow Lizard"
location: "Abkhazia — Gagra (type locality); northwestern Pontic populations"
description: >-
  The Pontic Meadow Lizard is a harmless lizard of the Black Sea region of northwestern Georgia, and a candidate species on the checklist. You cannot identify it by colour alone.
overview: >-
  The Pontic Meadow Lizard (Darevskia pontica) occurs in northwestern Georgia, in the Black Sea region. It lives on the ground, has no venom and is not dangerous to people. It belongs to the true lizard family (Lacertidae). A 2026 Georgian list treats it as a possible separate species and distinguishes it from the Meadow Lizard (Darevskia praticola). However, it has not yet been shown that the two groups are clearly separated genetically. It has no separate international threat assessment; in 2009, the Meadow Lizard group was assessed as near threatened. The Pontic Meadow Lizard is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. The lizard used to describe the species is recorded as coming from Gagra, on the Black Sea coast of Abkhazia, not from eastern Georgia. This does not mean the species occurs only in Gagra.
habitat: |-
  It lives in meadows, along forest edges and in deciduous forest, on the ground among grass and leaves. Unlike rock lizards (Darevskia), it does not live on sheer rock faces. It occurs in Georgia: the 2026 list assigns the groups in the Black Sea area of northwestern Georgia to Darevskia pontica. Its presence on the eastern lowlands or in the high mountains of Javakheti should not be assumed. Information on its distribution is of moderate reliability, and not every meadow in Colchis has been surveyed.

  It was described in 1918 under the name Lacerta praticola pontica. The description gives Gagra as the locality; in 2016 this was refined to the area around Gagra in Abkhazia. The first description also included unusual lizards found at Ananuri in the Aragvi valley. In 2016 they were assigned to praticola in its usual sense, not to pontica. In 2011 an adult male (ZISP 22853), collected in June 1911, was chosen as the main reference specimen for the description.
diet: >-
  Lizards in the Meadow Lizard group probably eat small animals: insects, spiders and similar prey. They have no venom and catch their prey with their jaws.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day and lives on the ground. It warms itself on stones or by roadsides and hides in grass, among leaves and roots. In the northwestern Caucasus, in Russia's Krasnodar Krai, it usually lays eggs in June. It lays once a season, often 1–6 eggs at a time. Animals measured there often have a head-and-body length of about 4.2–6.2 cm, and females are usually larger than males. If you catch one, it may bite you or its tail may come off. So do not pick it up: watch it and leave it where it is.
conservation: >-
  The Pontic Meadow Lizard (D. pontica) has no separate international threat assessment. In 2009, the Meadow Lizard (Darevskia praticola) was assessed internationally as Near Threatened, but that assessment did not treat pontica as a separate species. The 2026 list includes D. pontica as a possible separate species, but more evidence is still needed that it is truly distinct from D. praticola. The Pontic Meadow Lizard is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. That list includes other Darevskia species, such as clarkorum, dahli and mixta. It is not listed under CITES either. Do not catch wild lizards.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The Pontic Meadow Lizard is small and lives on the ground in the northwestern part of the Black Sea region. In the wild, you cannot tell it apart from other Darevskia lizards by color alone. So look at its scales as well, and keep in mind how it lives and where you saw it.
  traits:
    - >-
      This is not a rock lizard (Darevskia): it has short legs and lives on the ground, in meadows and at forest edges. Its back is often brownish gray with dark spots, and a broad dark stripe often runs along it. This look is typical for it, but color alone is not enough to tell species apart.
    - >-
      The head and body, from the tip of the snout to the base of the tail, are often about 4–6 cm long. This figure comes from animals studied in the northwestern Caucasus. It does not include the tail, so the whole lizard is longer.
    - >-
      pontica often has six pairs of plates under its chin. Three of those pairs meet along the middle of the chin. praticola often has five pairs, two of which meet along the middle. Some animals in eastern Georgia, including on the Tsiv–Gombori Ridge, also have six pairs. This feature can help distinguish the species, but it is not enough on its own to identify an individual animal.
    - >-
      Telling the Pontic Meadow Lizard (D. pontica) from the Meadow Lizard (D. praticola) reliably by back colour is hard. Lizards living in eastern and central Georgia (Sioni, Tushurebi) are treated as the Meadow Lizard, while the groups in the northwestern Black Sea region are treated as pontica. Sources disagree on whether they are separate species or subspecies. Where you saw the lizard will help you more than the colour of its back.
    - >-
      Derjugin's lizard (D. derjugini) has also been confirmed in western Georgia. It lives on the ground in forests and is more often found deep in the woods or beside streams. The Sand Lizard (Lacerta agilis) is larger in the west. It belongs to a different genus and is not a small Darevskia. So do not rely on color alone to identify a lizard.
faq:
  - question: Is the Pontic meadow lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 list treats it as a possible separate species. The lizard used to describe the species is recorded as coming from Gagra, in Abkhazia.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In northwestern Georgia’s Black Sea region, in Abkhazia. The place named in this region is Gagra. It lives in meadows, at forest edges and among leaves, not only on rocks. But living in meadows and at forest edges does not mean it is found throughout Georgia.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Color alone will not tell you. The Pontic Meadow Lizard is small and lives on the ground. Compare it with the eastern Meadow Lizard (D. praticola), the western Derjugin's Lizard (D. derjugini) and the larger L. agilis. Where you saw the lizard and the plates on its chin are more reliable clues than the color of its back.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No, it has no venom. If you catch it, it may bite you, and its tail may come off. So don't catch it: leave it where you found it.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Often about 4–6 cm, not counting the tail. That is the head-and-body length of lizards measured in the northwestern Caucasus. With the tail, the whole lizard is longer than that.
  - question: Is it the same as the meadow lizard?
    answer: >-
      The name pontica is used for lizards from the northwestern part of the Black Sea region, and praticola for those living in eastern and central Georgia. The pontica lizards are considered a possible separate species, although some databases treat them as a subspecies. The two cannot be told apart by color. So don't rely on color: where you saw the lizard tells you more.
  - question: Why is it a candidate species?
    answer: >-
      There is no agreement on this: some treat it as a separate species, others as a subspecies. A 2011 work treats it as a species. A 2016 study, however, groups the Caucasian pontica with praticola, and some databases keep it as a subspecies. The 2026 Georgian checklist does not yet consider it proven that it is truly separate from the Meadow Lizard.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      During the day. In the northwestern Caucasus, its eggs have usually been seen in June. That timing applies only to that region.
