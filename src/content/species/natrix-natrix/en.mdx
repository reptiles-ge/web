description: >-
  Non-venomous grass snake in Georgia — yellow collar; not a viper.
overview: >-
  The grass snake (Natrix natrix) is a non-venomous, semi-aquatic snake. Its
  best-known mark is the yellow, white, or cream collar behind the head, though
  this mark can be weak in dark individuals. In western Georgia it is often
  confused with the Caucasus viper, and near water with the dice snake (Natrix
  tessellata). The 2026 Georgian checklist treats the species as biologically
  confirmed. On this page, only regions whose status is confirmed in the
  records-by-region table count as distribution: Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo
  Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti,
  Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti, and Tbilisi. For this species, one record is enough.
  Georgian
  populations belong to the subspecies Natrix natrix scutata. The striped form
  formerly called N. n. persa is now treated within the same subspecies. The
  former "big-headed grass snake" (Natrix megalocephala), once considered a
  separate species, is no longer treated as separate in modern classification.
  IUCN lists the grass snake as Least Concern, and it is not included in
  Georgia's 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  On this page, only regions whose status is confirmed in the records-by-region
  table count as distribution: Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Guria,
  Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli,
  Kakheti, and Tbilisi. For this species, one record is enough. It
  prefers marshes, floodplains, lakes, streams, damp forests, and riverside
  meadows. The 2026 checklist includes a specific eastern record from Tushurebi
  village in Tianeti Municipality, in Mtskheta — Mtianeti. Compared with the
  dice snake, it often chooses cooler and shadier
  settings. In Colchic forest it has also been recorded climbing trees, including
  to bask and to hunt tree frogs. It may occur in gardens, parks, and farmland if
  there are enough amphibians and damp refuges. The sources used on this page do
  not define one exact maximum elevation for its distribution in Georgia.
diet: >-
  It feeds mainly on amphibians — frogs and toads. It also catches fish, though
  it depends on fish less than the dice snake does. It does not constrict its
  prey and swallows it alive. It hunts both in water and on land, and sometimes
  moves quite far from water while searching for toads.
behavior: >-
  It is active in the warm season and moves mostly by day. In very hot weather
  it may also become active in the evening or at night. In the western lowlands,
  the active season is longer than in high mountain areas, but no single seasonal
  calendar for Georgia is published. It lays eggs. At species level, mating
  occurs in spring, and in summer the female usually lays about 8–40 eggs in a
  warm, moist place such as rotting vegetation or compost. Young hatch in late
  summer or early autumn. These data apply to the species as a whole, not
  specifically to Georgia's population. When threatened it may release a strong
  cloacal secretion, flatten its head, or feign death. It does not chase people
  and usually avoids conflict. Do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  The IUCN Red List assesses the grass snake as Least Concern. It is not included
  in Georgia's 2014 Red List. The species is also listed in Appendix III of the
  Bern Convention. Local threats may include loss of wetlands and other damp
  habitats, water pollution, road mortality, and killing after confusion with
  venomous snakes. In the humid west, the species is locally common.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This nonvenomous snake lives both in water and on land. The easiest way to recognize it is by the yellow or pale “collar” behind its head and its round pupils. In western Georgia, it may be mistaken for a Caucasian viper, and near water, for a water grass snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      Behind its head, it often has a yellow, white or cream-colored “collar”: two pale, crescent-shaped patches with a dark area behind them. This is one of the common grass snake’s most distinctive features. In very dark grass snakes, the collar may be barely visible or not visible at all.
    - >-
      The back may be greenish, grey, or brownish. The dorsal scales have a small
      central ridge. Very dark, almost black individuals occur in Colchis. Some
      grass snakes have two pale lengthwise stripes on the back — this form was
      formerly known as "persa" and has also been recorded near Paliastomi. The
      pupil is round.
    - >-
      In Georgia, the grass snake is a medium-sized snake and is usually about
      70–120 cm long. Females are larger than males. In other parts of Europe,
      individuals longer than 1.5 m occur rarely, but that should not be treated
      as typical for Georgia. The head is only slightly distinct from the body and
      does not have the broad, sharply triangular shape typical of vipers.
    - >-
      The dice snake (Natrix tessellata) lacks the pale collar behind the head
      and often has checkered blotches on the back. It is more closely tied to
      open water and feeds mainly on fish. The grass snake more often hunts
      amphibians and also occurs in damp, shady settings. In Georgia, the dice
      snake may be more visible in semi-arid areas.
    - >-
      The Caucasus viper is venomous, has a shorter and heavier body, and its
      pupil is vertical. It lacks the pale collar typical of the grass snake. The
      smooth snake has smooth scales and no collar. The glass lizard is not a
      snake at all — it has movable eyelids and ear openings. Do not pick up an
      unknown snake.

faq:
  - question: Is the grass snake venomous?
    answer: >-
      No. The grass snake has no venom and is not dangerous to people. When
      threatened it may release a strong-smelling secretion, flatten its head, or
      feign death. Do not pick it up, especially in western Georgia, where from a
      distance it can be confused with the Caucasus viper.
  - question: How does it differ from the dice snake?
    answer: >-
      The grass snake often has a pale collar behind the head and more often
      occurs in damp, shady settings. It feeds mainly on amphibians. The dice
      snake lacks the collar and often has checkered blotches on the back. It is
      more closely tied to water and feeds mainly on fish.
  - question: How can I tell it from a viper?
    answer: >-
      Look for the yellow, white, or cream collar behind the head, a round pupil,
      and a relatively slender body. In Colchis the grass snake may be very dark,
      almost black. The Caucasus viper lacks the collar, has a shorter and
      heavier body, and has a vertical pupil. Do not rely on only one trait to
      identify an unknown snake.
  - question: Is the grass snake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The grass snake is a confirmed species in Georgia. The 2026 Georgian
      checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. Distribution on this page is
      Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe —
      Javakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti, and Tbilisi.

  - question: What does it eat?

  - question: Does it lay eggs or give live birth?
    answer: >-
      It lays eggs. At species level, mating occurs in spring, and in summer the
      female usually lays about 8–40 eggs in a warm, moist place. Young hatch in
      late summer or early autumn. Separate fecundity data for Georgia's
      population are not given in the sources used on this page.
  - question: What should I do if one comes into the yard?
    answer: >-
      Keep your distance and do not pick it up. The grass snake is non-venomous
      and not dangerous to people. If you are not sure of the species, do not
      approach the snake and calmly give it space to leave on its own.
