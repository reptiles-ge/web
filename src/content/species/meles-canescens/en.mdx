commonName: Caucasian badger
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A stocky, short-legged mustelid with a black-and-white striped head and a
  grizzled grey back. It is a resident native species in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian badger (Meles canescens) is a mammal in the weasel family
  (Mustelidae). In Georgian it is მაჩვი, often also ევროპული მაჩვი (“European
  badger”). National checklists, the hunting list, and the 2016 IUCN assessment
  still write Meles meles. Since 2013, specialist taxonomy treats Transcaucasian,
  Anatolian, and Near Eastern populations as a separate species, Meles
  canescens. The ASM Mammal Diversity Database lists Georgia in that range. It
  has no venom. The main human risk is a bite if it is handled, not poison.
  Globally, Meles meles in the lumped sense is Least Concern. Meles canescens
  has no separate IUCN assessment. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in forest, woodland edge, scrub, farmland, and
  foothills where it can dig a sett. The National Geographic Georgia / National
  Museum map calls it eurytopic — in almost all biotopes, rarely in the alpine
  belt — and gives elevation to about 2,700 m; that figure is from that map, not
  a separate measured belt on this page. This page does not map administrative
  regions. Named Georgian localities include Vashlovani (photograph on this
  page, 6 June 2014); Lagodekhi, Batsara, and Kintrishi (the same map, as recent
  sightings); Ajameti Managed Reserve and Sataplia (Agency of Protected Areas
  texts); and the Iori floodplain hunting farm (management plan, listed as
  Meles meles leptorynchus). The map says it occurs over almost the whole
  country. That is a country-level summary, not a municipality list. Do not
  assume even density across Georgia.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. At genus level it is an
  omnivore: in north-west Europe earthworms are often important; in
  Mediterranean and drier country it takes more insects, fruit, small
  vertebrates, and crops. Western Anatolian papers list fruit, insects, and
  earthworms. The realistic picture in Georgia is the same opportunistic menu at
  forest and farmland edges — but the local share has not been measured here.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at night and at dusk. It can also be seen by day,
  especially where disturbance is low. A daytime sighting does not by itself
  mean disease. It lives in a sett — a complex burrow with several entrances
  and spoil heaps. A sett may be used for generations. At species-group level,
  Meles often has delayed implantation: mating in spring–summer, cubs usually in
  late winter or early spring, often several young in a litter. Those figures
  come from the wider range, not a Georgian den sample. The print is broad, with
  five toes; long digging claws show on the forefoot. The track photograph on
  this page is from Vayots Dzor, Armenia, 18 August 2026, not a Georgian series.
conservation: >-
  Kranz and co-authors assess Meles meles as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List
  (2016); that assessment includes populations now often called Meles
  canescens. Meles canescens has no separate IUCN status (MDD: Not Evaluated).
  It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution No. 190). The
  national map gives national status NE and describes the population as
  “small”; that wording is from the map, not a separate census on this page. It
  is listed as a hunting species under the Georgian name მაჩვი; the official
  Latin line is written Martes meles — a nomenclatural error; the animal meant
  is the badger, not a marten. Quotas and seasons are set by agency documents
  and are not copied here as current numbers. Local pressures can include
  hunting, road mortality, and conflict over crops and hives; a national trend
  is not measured on this page. Do not handle wild animals.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom. If handled or cornered it can bite. It has a strong jaw and
  long claws. Any wild mammal can in principle carry rabies; Georgian sources
  more often name fox, jackal, wolf, and dog as the main sources. Do not feed
  or handle it. Keep dogs vaccinated.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A stocky, short-legged animal with a black-and-white striped head, a
    grizzled grey back, and a short tail. It is resident in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is low and broad, the legs short, the head wedge-shaped, the ears
      small with white tips. Abramov and Puzachenko treat Meles canescens as
      smaller than Meles meles. Anatolian samples in the literature are given
      about 73–84 cm head–body and about 5–9 kg; those are regional figures, not
      a measured Georgian series. Northern European Meles meles is often heavier
      in autumn — do not transfer that weight to Georgia.
    - >-
      A white midline stripe runs from the muzzle over the forehead; two black
      stripes pass through the eyes and ears. The back and sides are
      grizzled grey; the legs and often the belly are darker. Heptner describes
      the Transcaucasian form as having a dirtier-grey back than large northern
      Meles meles. In the field, identification to badger is by the facial mask;
      living Meles meles and Meles canescens cannot be told apart with
      confidence.
    - >-
      The least weasel is much smaller and elongated, without the black-and-white
      face. Beech marten and pine marten are slimmer, with a bushy tail, and are
      more typically in trees; this atlas does not yet have pages for them. The
      red fox is dog-like, with a slender muzzle and a long tail. The honey
      badger (Mellivora capensis) is not accepted here as part of Georgia’s
      fauna.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. Do not handle it. A short-legged grey animal with a
      striped face at a woodland edge or on a road is this species, not a
      marten.
facts:
  - >-
    Blanford described Meles canescens in 1875 from Iran (Abadeh). Abramov and
    Puzachenko in 2013, using cranial morphometrics in agreement with molecular
    data, recognise four parapatric species: Meles meles (Europe), Meles
    canescens (Southwest Asia and the mountains of Middle Asia), Meles leucurus,
    and Meles anakuma. The name used on this page for the Georgian population is
    Meles canescens. Order Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Mustelidae,
    genus Meles.
  - >-
    Satunin described Meles meles minor in 1905, type locality Borjomi, Georgia.
    That name is now treated as a synonym of Meles canescens. Other historical
    names include Meles urartuorum and Meles meles ponticus. Official Georgian
    documents still write Meles meles. Do not assign a field individual to a
    subspecies.
  - >-
    The boundary between Meles meles sensu stricto and Meles canescens lies in
    the North Caucasus and is not sharply defined; hybridisation has also been
    mentioned. For Georgia this page follows canescens as the accepted
    Transcaucasian name and does not claim that northern Meles meles is
    confirmed in Georgia.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as native for Meles meles (lumped concept). It is in
    Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s 2002 annotated list and in Eliava et al.
    2007. That is country-level confirmation plus named localities, not a full
    regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the badger found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is on national mammal checklists. IUCN lists Georgia in the native
      range of Meles meles. Under current taxonomy the Georgian population is
      Meles canescens.
  - question: Why do some sources say Meles meles and others Meles canescens?
    answer: >-
      Until 2013 these populations were treated as one species. Transcaucasia,
      Anatolia, and the Near East are now split as a separate species. Official
      Georgian texts still use Meles meles. In the field it is the same
      striped-face badger.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In forest, woodland edge, scrub, and farmland where there is a sett. Named
      sites include Vashlovani (photo, 2014), Lagodekhi, Batsara, Kintrishi,
      Ajameti, Sataplia, and the Iori floodplain hunting farm. This page does
      not map every region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Black-and-white facial stripes, a stocky short-legged body, a grizzled
      grey back, and a short tail. A weasel is small and has no mask. A marten
      is slimmer with a bushy tail.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      The Southwest Asian form is smaller than European Meles meles. Anatolian
      samples are given about 73–84 cm head–body. There is no separate measured
      Georgian series on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It is an omnivore: insects, earthworms, fruit, small vertebrates, sometimes
      crops. Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      Mainly at night and at dusk. It can also appear by day. It is resident and
      does not migrate like many birds.
  - question: Is it dangerous? Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous. It can bite if handled. Do not handle it. Globally,
      lumped Meles meles is Least Concern. It is not on the 2014 Red List. It is
      a hunting species; season and quota are set by official rules.
