overview: >-
  Brown marmorated stink bug (Halyomorpha halys) is an insect in the stink bug
  family Pentatomidae. Georgian official sources use the names აზიური ფაროსანა
  and აზიური ბაღლინჯო. The accepted scientific name is Halyomorpha halys.

  In Georgia this is not a native wildlife species. EPPO reports the first
  Georgian records from 2016, in Khobi municipality and Pitsunda. Georgia's
  National Food Agency links the 2016 spread with damage to hazelnut crops and
  highlights western Georgian outbreak areas in 2017.

  For a reader in Georgia the useful answer is simple: this is an invasive,
  plant-feeding agricultural pest, especially relevant around hazelnut orchards,
  fruit trees, vegetable plots, gardens, and houses. It does not bite people or
  pets and is not known as a vector of human or animal diseases, but adults may
  enter buildings in autumn to overwinter.

  Adults are about 12-17 mm long. The body is broad, shield-shaped, brown and
  marbled; useful field marks are the pale bands on the antennae, legs, and
  outer edge of the abdomen.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, brown marmorated stink bug is mainly tied to agricultural and
  settled landscapes: hazelnut orchards, fruit gardens, vegetable plots, yards,
  garden edges, shrubs, and trees that provide host plants. It is not a native
  conservation-priority forest or steppe species; it is an invasive pest.

  Confirmed Georgian evidence starts in the west. EPPO names Khobi municipality
  and Pitsunda, while National Food Agency material highlights Samegrelo, Guria,
  Samtredia, and Gali. A 2019-2020 hazelnut-orchard survey also covered western
  Georgian regions. This supports mapping named western records, not claiming
  that the insect is equally common in every Georgian region.

  The sources used here do not provide a precise Georgian elevation range. In
  practical terms, encounters are most expected in lowland and foothill
  agricultural areas where host plants and overwintering shelters are available.
diet: >-
  Brown marmorated stink bug is a polyphagous plant feeder. Adults and nymphs
  pierce plant tissues with sucking mouthparts and feed from leaves, shoots,
  fruit, seeds, and other soft plant parts.

  Georgian official material lists hazelnut, pome and stone fruits, berries,
  vegetables, sunflower, and maize, with examples including peach, nectarine,
  apple, pear, raspberry, tomato, and pepper. International risk analysis also
  treats it as a pest with a very broad host range.

  Feeding often leaves depressed, discoloured, or corky damage on fruit. This is
  an agricultural damage issue, not a biting or attacking behaviour toward
  people.
behavior: >-
  In Georgia, National Food Agency material places feeding from late April-May
  through late autumn. Eggs are laid in clusters on the underside of leaves;
  nymphs hatch after a few days, pass through five instars, and then become
  adults.

  NCDC material notes observations of two full generations in western Georgia.
  That is a western Georgian observation and should not be read as an exact
  calendar for every microclimate in the country.

  In autumn, adults seek dry, protected overwintering places. They may enter
  houses, attics, window and door gaps, wall cracks, tree hollows, and leaf
  litter. When disturbed or crushed, they release a strong odour.
conservation: >-
  Conservation is not the main frame for this species; control and accurate
  identification are. Brown marmorated stink bug is an invasive pest in Georgia
  that damages cultivated plants and has been especially important for hazelnut
  production.

  Georgian Red List or IUCN conservation status is not the relevant working
  frame in the sources used for this page. The purpose here is to separate
  confirmed distribution evidence, useful identification, and safe response.

  Chemical control should follow official agricultural advice, registered
  products, and label directions. For occasional individuals indoors, safe
  mechanical removal is often more appropriate than improvised pesticide use.
interaction: >-
  Brown marmorated stink bug is not venomous or poisonous to people. According
  to NCDC guidance, it does not bite people or pets and does not spread human or
  animal diseases.

  The odorous secretion released when it is disturbed can be unpleasant; some
  people may be sensitive or allergic to it, but NCDC does not describe this as
  life-threatening. Crushing the insect is not useful because the smell becomes
  stronger and can remain on surfaces.

  Indoors, remove it without direct handling or crushing: collect it with paper
  or a container and place it in soapy water, or use a vacuum if it can be
  cleaned afterward. Use pesticides only according to label, registration, and
  safety rules.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A broad, shield-shaped, brown-marbled stink bug. The most useful field marks
    are the pale bands on the antennae, alternating pale-dark blocks along the
    edge of the abdomen, and the wide mosaic-patterned body.
  traits:
    - >-
      Adults are about 12-17 mm long. The body is broad, slightly flattened, and
      shield-shaped from above.
    - >-
      Colour alone is not enough: individuals may look grey-brown or darker
      brown. The pale antennal bands and alternating marks on the outer abdomen
      are more reliable.
    - >-
      Pale bands are also visible on the legs. The shoulder corners are not
      strongly spined, so a generic “brown stink bug” impression should not be
      treated as final identification.
    - >-
      Nymphs are smaller than adults and change appearance through development.
      Newly hatched nymphs may be reddish; later stages become darker with
      whitish-brown markings.
    - >-
      It can be confused with other stink bugs in Georgia. For confirmation,
      photograph the insect sharply from above so the antennae, abdomen edge,
      and body shape are visible.
