commonName: Dahl's whip snake
location: Eastern and southern Georgia
description: >-
  Non-venomous Dahl’s whip snake in Georgia — extremely slender; not a viper.
overview: |-
  The slender whip snake (Platyceps najadum) is a nonvenomous, very slender snake. In Georgia, it is mainly associated with dry, rocky places in the east and south. It belongs to the family Colubridae.
  Adults are usually about 70–100 cm long and rarely exceed 1 metre. A 2026 Georgian list confirms the species’ presence; a modern specimen named in the list is from Tmogvi, in Aspindza Municipality.
  According to a 2021 Georgian study, the species is associated with a dry climate and is found mainly in rocky and grassy areas of eastern Georgia. It is classified as Least Concern.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, Dahl’s whip snake may be encountered on dry rocky slopes, near
  cliffs, in scrub, at woodland edges, in dry meadows, and around agricultural
  settings. It uses rock cracks and cavities as shelter; in winter it has also
  been recorded in human structures.

  The 2021 study places the species in a dry-climate group and links it
  especially with eastern rocky and grassy landscapes. The named modern record
  is from Tmogvi. Older museum material also names Tbilisi and Borjomi.

  Some historical records also exist from western Georgia, but old museum labels
  cannot replace a precise modern distribution map. This page presents no
  specific modern records from Guria or Imereti.

  Across the species’ wider range it has been described up to about 2,000 m,
  although a separate complete elevation range for Georgia is not available.
diet: >-
  The diet of the Georgian population has not been studied separately. In other
  parts of the species’ range, Dahl’s whip snake mainly hunts lizards, although
  it also catches small mammals, birds, and other snakes.

  It does not use venom and relies on speed to catch prey. This food list
  describes the species’ general biology, not a separately measured diet in
  Georgia.
behavior: >-
  In other parts of its range, Dahl’s whip snake is active by day. It moves
  quickly and often basks. Its hourly activity in Georgia has not been studied
  separately.

  When threatened, it mainly tries to flee. If cornered, it may raise the front
  of the body and gape.

  It lays eggs. Species literature often cites 3–16 eggs, although the mating
  period and egg number for the Georgian population have not been published
  separately.

  In 2026, a Dahl’s whip snake in Georgia was recorded in a shared winter
  shelter with 133 red-bellied racers and one dice snake. The shelter was in a
  reinforced-concrete septic tank. This is an interesting specific case and does
  not mean the species usually always overwinters with other snakes.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists Dahl’s whip snake as Least Concern. It is not included in Georgia’s
  2014 Red List.

  European populations are protected under Annex II of the Bern Convention and
  Annex IV of the EU Habitats Directive, although the EU legal regime does not
  automatically apply in Georgia.

  In the 2026 Georgian checklist the species is biologically confirmed, and its
  distribution precision is rated medium.
interaction: |-
  The olive-colored racer is not venomous and does not pose a medical danger to people. Do not pick one up — a large snake may bite in self-defense, and the bite can be painful.
  If the black part of a snake’s eye is vertically elongated, it is not an olive-colored racer. Watch an unfamiliar snake from a safe distance, leave it a way through, and take photos from afar.

facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1831 (Eichwald), originally as Tyria najadum; the type
    locality is Baku, not Georgia. Older literature often used Coluber najadum
    or Zamenis dahlii. The modern genus Platyceps became standard after Schätti
    & Monsch 2004. Caucasian animals belong to the nominotypical subspecies
    (P. n. najadum).
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist specimen and COI barcode are from Tmogvi (Aspindza;
    CaBOL-ID 1027410). The same paper notes about 6.65% COI difference between
    Georgian and Armenian sequences — a reason to revisit taxonomy, not a
    completed species split.
  - >-
    The Georgian name reflects the olive–buckthorn dorsal tone. The English
    name whip snake refers to the extremely slender, whip-like body. Neck
    ocelli are the main field mark.
faq:
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      No. Dahl’s whip snake has no venom and does not pose a medical threat to
      people. When threatened it mainly tries to flee, although it may bite if
      picked up.

      If the snake has a vertical pupil, it is not Dahl’s whip snake. In that
      case, do not approach to identify it and give the snake room.
  - question: Is Dahl’s whip snake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. In the 2026 Georgian checklist, Dahl’s whip snake is listed as a
      biologically confirmed species. The named modern record is from around
      Tmogvi. In Georgia it is not evenly distributed across the whole country.
  - question: Where might I encounter it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Dahl’s whip snake is mainly associated with dry, rocky or grassy habitats
      in eastern and southern Georgia. The named modern record is from around
      Tmogvi, in Samtskhe–Javakheti.

      Older material also names Tbilisi and Borjomi. Historical museum records
      exist from western Georgia, but they do not replace a precise modern
      distribution map. This page presents no specific modern records from Guria
      or Imereti.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A very slender, whip-like body, round pupil, smooth scales, and dark spots
      with pale edges on the neck and front of the body are especially helpful.
      The rear of the body is often almost plain.

      The spotted whip snake has blotches over a much larger part of the back. A
      vertical pupil rules out Dahl’s whip snake, although you should not rely on
      only one feature to identify an unknown snake.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adult Dahl’s whip snakes are usually about 70–100 cm long and rarely
      exceed 1 m. Literature from the species’ wider range also describes
      maximum sizes of about 1.35–1.40 m.

      A separately confirmed maximum length for Georgia is not presented in the
      sources used on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Its diet in Georgia has not been studied separately. In other parts of the
      range it mainly hunts lizards, although it also catches small mammals,
      birds, and other snakes. It has no venom and relies on speed to catch prey.
  - question: When is it active, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      In other parts of its range it is active by day. A precise daily activity
      calendar for Georgia has not been published separately.

      It lays eggs; species-level literature often cites 3–16 eggs. In Georgia,
      an individual has also been recorded sheltering in winter in a human-made
      structure with other non-venomous snakes.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Observe from a safe distance, take a photo, and give the snake room. Do
      not pick it up.

      If the pupil is vertical, it is not Dahl’s whip snake. A vertical pupil can
      occur in venomous vipers as well as other species, so species
      identification should not be based only on this feature.
