commonName: Wasp spider
location: Western and eastern lowlands
description: >-
  A yellow-and-black banded orb-weaver. It is native in Georgia and is not a
  medical risk to people.
overview: >-
  The wasp spider (Argiope bruennichi) is an orb-weaving spider in the family
  Araneidae. The accepted name is Argiope bruennichi (Scopoli, 1772); the original
  combination was Aranea brünnichii. In Georgian it is არგიოპა; the Ilia State
  University biodiversity database uses ბრუნიქის არგიოპა. The English name refers
  to the yellow and black bands on the abdomen, which resemble a wasp. Otto 2022
  lists the species for Georgia and calls it very abundant in the Caucasus
  ecoregion. The Ilia database treats it as native and writes that it is widely
  distributed in Georgia. That is a country-level assessment, not even density
  in all twelve regions. At species level the female body is about 10–22 mm, the
  male about 7–8 mm. It has venom for insect prey; it is not medically
  significant for people. IUCN has not assessed the species. It is not on
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List; the arachnid on that list is the fen raft spider
  (Dolomedes plantarius).
habitat: >-
  Look for it in low grass and humid meadow, where the female builds an orb web
  close to the ground. Nentwig et al. give species-level habitat as grass of
  humid meadows near the ground. That is not a measured Georgian elevation belt.
  This atlas colours regions only from named records. Otto 2022 compiles, among
  others, Tbilisi (Mcheidze 1964; Pkhakadze 2006), Gamarjveba east of Tbilisi
  (Otto 2006 and 2010; Kvemo Kartli), David Gareja / Sagarejo (Stoev and Lazarov,
  20 July 2006; Kakheti), Adjara (Moritz; also Batumi, Kobuleti, Chakvi, Khulo —
  Mcheidze 1964), Kakheti localities Telavi, Gurjaani, Sighnaghi, Lagodekhi and
  Shiraki (Mcheidze 1964, 1997), Guria (Ozurgeti, Chokhatauri, Lanchkhuti,
  Bakhvi), Imereti (Terjola, Tkibuli, south of Kutaisi, Baghdati, Zestaponi),
  Samtskhe–Javakheti (Akhaltsikhe, Aspindza), Poti in Samegrelo, Abkhazia
  (Sukhumi, New Athos, Bichvinta–Miusera; Mcheidze 1964; Spassky 1937; Kovblyuk
  et al. 2011), and the Tskhinvali / Eredvi area (Ponomarev and Komarov 2015;
  Shida Kartli on this atlas map). Racha–Lechkhumi–Kvemo Svaneti and
  Mtskheta–Mtianeti are not coloured from a named record on this page. “Widely
  distributed” in the Ilia database does not mean it occurs in every high-mountain
  belt. Photos on this page were not taken in Georgia.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. At species level the
  female takes flying and jumping insects in the orb web — wasps, flies,
  grasshoppers and other large invertebrates that hit the silk. Prey is wrapped,
  then bitten. That is the general trophic niche, not a measured Georgian menu.
  Do not treat it as biological control in a garden.
behavior: >-
  By day the female often sits in the web centre, head down. The hub often has a
  white zigzag of silk — a stabilimentum; its function is not fully settled
  (prey attraction, predator warning, or making the web visible). At species
  level adult females are most obvious in the second half of summer. A named
  Georgian date is 20 July 2006 at David Gareja. A full national calendar is not
  measured here. Eggs are in a brown papery sac; they overwinter, and young
  emerge in spring. The male is much smaller and often waits at the female’s web
  for her last moult. The female may eat the male during mating; that is
  species-level behaviour, not a Georgian sample. Do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  EUNIS and the Ilia database give IUCN status NE (Not Evaluated). The Ilia
  database assigns national LC*; that is their assessment, not Government
  Resolution No. 190 of 2014. The official Red List arachnid is Dolomedes
  plantarius (VU), not this species. In northern Europe the species has expanded
  its range; Krehenwinkel and Tautz 2013 treat the Caucasus as a genetic
  diversity source, including Georgian samples from Gamarjveba, Adjara and David
  Gareja. A Georgian population trend is not measured on this page. Do not catch
  wild spiders.
interaction: >-
  The spider has venom for prey. It is not a medical risk to people: bites are
  rare and usually only local pain or redness. This is not a viper bite. An
  allergic reaction to any bite is possible; if breathing, the face, or general
  condition worsens, call 112. Do not handle it. A web in garden grass in summer
  is typical; destroy it only if it blocks a path — the spider is not a pest.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large orb-weaver with yellow and black cross-bands on the abdomen and often
    a white zigzag in the web hub. The female is much larger than the male.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level female body length is about 10–22 mm (Nentwig et al.);
      male about 7–8 mm. Legs are not included. There is no separate Georgian
      measurement series on this page.
    - >-
      The female cephalothorax (prosoma) is silvery and shiny. The abdomen
      (opisthosoma) is dorsally banded white or yellow on a black or dark ground
      — the wasp-like pattern. The underside is dark with yellow longitudinal
      stripes and spots. Legs are yellowish with brown rings. The male is more
      uniformly silver-grey or yellowish, with weaker bands.
    - >-
      The web is an orb, usually in grass near the ground. The hub often has a
      white zigzag (stabilimentum). An adult female often sits head-down in the
      centre. That is the fastest field cue if you can see the spider.
    - >-
      The same genus in Georgia includes Argiope lobata (Otto 2022 checklist).
      That species has lateral lobes on the abdomen and a more silvery or spotted
      pattern, not wasp yellow-and-black bands. Garden orb-weavers such as
      Araneus have a different mark, often a cross, and not these bands. Do not
      confuse it with a true wasp: the spider has eight legs, sits in a web, and
      has no wings.
    - >-
      This atlas has a separate profile for the lobed argiope (Argiope lobata).
facts:
  - >-
    The accepted name is Argiope bruennichi (Scopoli, 1772). The World Spider
    Catalog treats the species as accepted. Range: Europe, North Africa, Turkey,
    the Middle East, Russia (Europe to the Far East), the Caucasus, Iran,
    Central Asia to China, Korea, Japan.
  - >-
    Otto 2022 lists 659 spider species for Georgia, including A. bruennichi and
    A. lobata. Seropian et al. 2023 repeat that Araneidae count (47 species in
    Georgia) and do not list A. bruennichi among the specimens in that paper.
  - >-
    Georgian names: არგიოპა (Wikipedia / Wikidata) and ბრუნიქის არგიოპა (Ilia
    database). English: wasp spider. Russian: аргиопа Брюнниха. Turkish:
    eşekarısı örümceği.
  - >-
    The Ilia database gives IUCN NE and national LC*; assessors Otto, Kramer,
    Karalashvili, Seropian (2021). The species is not on the 2014 Red List.
faq:
  - question: Is the wasp spider found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Otto 2022 and the Ilia biodiversity database treat it as native in
      Georgia. That is country-level confirmation.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Named records include Tbilisi, Gamarjveba, David Gareja, Adjara, several
      Kakheti sites, Guria, Imereti, Samtskhe–Javakheti, Poti, the Abkhaz coast,
      and the Tskhinvali area. Look in grass and meadow in summer. Racha and
      Mtskheta–Mtianeti are not coloured on this page.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      The female has yellow-and-black cross-bands on the abdomen and often a
      white zigzag in the web hub. The male is much smaller and plainer. In
      Georgia the similar species is Argiope lobata, which has a lobed abdomen.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      At species level the female is about 10–22 mm, the male about 7–8 mm. Legs
      are not included. There is no Georgian series on this page.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      It has venom for insects. It is not a medical risk to people. Bites are
      rare and usually local. Do not handle it. Call 112 if serious symptoms
      appear.
  - question: When is it visible?
    answer: >-
      Adult females are most obvious in the second half of summer at species
      level. A named Georgian record is July at David Gareja. A full national
      calendar is not on this page.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      IUCN has not assessed it. It is not on the 2014 Red List. The Ilia
