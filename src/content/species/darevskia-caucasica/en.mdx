overview: >-
  The Caucasian rock lizard (Darevskia caucasica) is associated in Georgia with
  rocky habitats of the Greater Caucasus. The 2026 Georgian checklist treatment
  lists records and DNA barcodes from Zakagori Fortress, Mount Tbatana, and
  Lebarde; this atlas keeps the taxon as candidate because limits inside the
  caucasica complex and the genetic distinctness of Georgian populations remain
  active research topics. It has no venom and is not a medical danger to people.
  Field identification should use locality, rocky microhabitat, and scalation
  details, not green or brown colour alone.
habitat: >-
  Named Georgian points are Truso Valley and Zakagori Fortress (figure/barcode),
  Mount Tbatana, and Lebarde. The closest comparative NCBI sequence is also from
  Truso. This page therefore does not claim a countrywide range. The map layer is
  conservative and fills administrative regions only when the source gives an
  administrative unit or a reliably placeable locality. Look for sunny rock,
  stony slopes, road-cut rock, and rocky parts of mountain valleys where the
  lizard can escape into crevices.
diet: >-
  The Georgian records used here do not summarize stomach contents or a measured
  diet for this species. Small invertebrates are expected for a small rock
  lizard, but that is not presented here as measured Georgian evidence for
  D. caucasica.
behavior: >-
  This is a day-active rock lizard: it basks on stone or rock and retreats into
  crevices when disturbed. The Georgian sources used on this profile do not give
  a separate hourly calendar, exact emergence from winter shelters, or breeding
  months. For a useful observation, photograph the locality, elevation, back,
  side, and head scalation; catching the lizard is unnecessary.
conservation: >-
  Globally, the IUCN Red List assesses the species as Least Concern, but that
  older assessment covers the whole range, not Georgia alone. The 2026 checklist
  stresses that Georgian D. caucasica differ strongly from northern Caucasus
  populations, and the 2025 genomic study also supports several distant units
  inside the species. A separate Georgian population trend, abundance estimate,
  and verified national Red List status are not given on this profile.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. Do not pick it up: a small
  lizard may be stressed, bite, or drop its tail. Watch from where you are, take
  photographs, and leave it at the same crevice or stone.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The Caucasian rock lizard is a small, fast Darevskia of rocky places. Colour
    varies and does not separate close species; locality, rock habitat, and, when
    visible, head and precloacal scalation are more reliable.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is slim and adapted to rock; the tail is long and may look short
      or regenerated after injury. A damaged tail does not change the species ID.
    - >-
      Colour can be greenish, yellow-brown, grey, or brown, often with pale
      lateral stripes and dark marks. These are useful descriptive notes, but not
      diagnostic because several Darevskia species look similar.
    - >-
      The 2026 figures for the Zakagori/Truso material show head scales and
      precloacal plates. Those details matter when a photo has to be checked
      against similar rock lizards.
    - >-
      [Daghestanian rock lizard](darevskia-daghestanica) is the hardest
      comparison around the upper Tergi and Tbatana: colour does not separate
      them, and the literature mentions possible hybridization. In that zone, one
      photo is often not enough.
    - >-
      [Colchian rock lizard](darevskia-mixta) is linked to humid western Georgian
      valleys and has a narrow Greater Caucasus contact with caucasica.
      [Alpine rock lizard](darevskia-alpina) is also a candidate taxon in this
      atlas; colour is not a reliable separator.
