  The Spotted Whip Snake is a harmless, blotched snake with round pupils. It lives in dry, stony places. In Georgia, field records come mainly from the east: Kakheti, Tbilisi, Shida Kartli and Samtskhe-Javakheti.
overview: >-
  The Spotted Whip Snake (Hemorrhois ravergieri) is a blotched snake with no venom, harmless to people. In Georgia it lives in dry, stony or grassy places, mostly in the east. As of 2026, its presence in the country is confirmed, and by 2021 knowledge of this species had grown considerably. The precisely named locality is in Samtskhe-Javakheti, near Tmogvi in Aspindza, and its DNA sample comes from there too. Field records come from Kakheti, Tbilisi, Shida Kartli and Samtskhe-Javakheti. Do not look for it all over the country. Its wider range stretches from the Caucasus to Central Asia. Across the species, adults may reach about 1–1.3 m. Globally, it faces little risk of extinction. It belongs to the family Colubridae. Older books often called it Coluber ravergieri or Zamenis ravergieri; today its genus is Hemorrhois.
habitat: |-
  The Spotted Whip Snake depends on a dry climate. It lives in rocky or grassy places, in Georgia mostly in the east. The named locality in the country is in Samtskhe-Javakheti: a specimen was found in Aspindza, at Tmogvi, in a rocky canyon of the Mtkvari valley. Field records also come from other regions. Most are from Kakheti: the Dedoplistskaro area, Vashlovani and David Gareji. There are also records from Tbilisi, from near Uplistsikhe in Shida Kartli and, in Samtskhe-Javakheti, from Khertvisi and Akhaltsikhe. On the map these four regions are marked as its range. Imereti, Kvemo Kartli and Mtskheta-Mtianeti have only one or two records each, so they are not counted as part of its range. No locality has been named in Colchis. According to 2021 data, some eastern snakes of the same family have also been recorded in suitable places in the west, and knowledge of this species has grown.

  Across its range it lives on dry and semi-dry slopes, in canyons and in steppe. Outside Georgia it occurs in the Caucasus, Anatolia, Iran and Central Asia. The place where the species was first described is usually given as Baku, not Georgia.
diet: >-
  The Spotted Whip Snake eats lizards, small mammals, birds and other snakes too — this is how it feeds outside Georgia, in other parts of its range. It has no venom, so it catches its prey with speed.
behavior: |-
  In other parts of its range it is mainly active by day, often basks in the sun and moves fast. It lays eggs: across the species, a female often lays 4–10 eggs at a time. When threatened it usually flees. It has no venom, but a large snake can bite, so do not pick it up.

  In the same dry south you may come across Dahl's whip snake (Platyceps najadum), the Urartian ratsnake (Elaphe urartica) and, more rarely, the Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetinus). You can tell the Spotted Whip Snake apart from them by its pattern, head shape and pupil. Confusing it with a viper is dangerous: vipers have a vertical pupil.
conservation: >-
  Worldwide, the Spotted Whip Snake faces little risk of extinction: the 2017 assessment lists it as "Least Concern" because it lives across a large area. It is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List. The 2026 Georgian checklist confirms that it occurs in the country, though its distribution is known only with medium accuracy. The records are still uneven: most field records come from Kakheti, and by 2021 knowledge of the species had grown. Locally, it is threatened by changes to the places where it lives and by deaths on roads. Some people mistake it for a venomous snake and kill it. Even so, do not pick it up.

  summary: >-
    The Spotted Whip Snake is a slender, non-venomous snake with a round pupil and a blotched back. In Georgia it lives in dry, rocky places. The pupil is the key sign: a blotched snake with a vertical pupil is not a Spotted Whip Snake: it may be a viper, a Levantine viper or a European cat snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      A slender, fast snake with the build typical of whip snakes. The head is moderately distinct from the neck, and the pupil is round. Each scale on the back has a faint ridge running down its middle. At mid-body there are often 21 scale rows. Across the species, length may reach about 1.3 m. Do not pick the snake up to count its scales.
    - >-
      The ground colour is grey, brownish or sandy. Dark rhomboid or crossbar blotches run along the back, with small spots on the sides. Toward the rear of the body the blotches often merge and turn into lengthwise stripes on the tail. Two dark streaks mark the eye: one runs diagonally below it, the other goes from the eye toward the corner of the mouth. The belly is whitish, often with fine dark dots.
    - >-
      Several other snakes look like the spotted whip snake, but each has its own giveaway. Dahl's whip snake (Platyceps najadum) is slimmer and has eye-like spots on its neck rather than diamonds along the whole back. The Urartian ratsnake (Elaphe urartica) is stouter, with a dark head and larger, rounder blotches. The steppe ratsnake (Elaphe dione) has a fine pattern and often lengthwise stripes. Adult red-bellied racers (Dolichophis schmidti) are often plain reddish, with a red belly. The smooth snake is smaller and has smooth scales. To tell them apart, look first at body build, colour and the pattern on the back.
    - >-
      A blotched snake with a vertical pupil is not a Spotted Whip Snake: it may be a viper, a Levantine viper or a European cat snake. Vipers are venomous: the pupil is a narrow vertical slit, the head is more triangular, and each scale has a strong ridge down the middle. The Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetinus) is a bulkier snake with a broad head and a vertical pupil. Its venom is a serious danger to human health. The Spotted Whip Snake has no venom, but its bite can be painful. So do not pick it up.
    - >-
      The Spotted Whip Snake is a confirmed species in Georgia. It is considered a dry-climate snake that lives in rocky or grassy places and is linked mostly to eastern Georgia. The checklist specimen comes from Tmogvi in Aspindza, Samtskhe-Javakheti, and field records come mainly from Kakheti, Tbilisi and Shida Kartli. Do not look for it across the whole country.

faq:
  - question: Does the spotted whip snake occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. According to the 2026 checklist, the Spotted Whip Snake is confirmed in Georgia. The checklist specimen comes from Tmogvi in Aspindza, and field records come mainly from eastern Georgia. It does not, however, occur throughout the country.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Mainly in eastern Georgia, in dry, stony or grassy places. Most field records come from Kakheti: the Dedoplistskaro area, Vashlovani and David Gareji. There are also records from Tbilisi, from near Uplistsikhe in Shida Kartli and from Samtskhe-Javakheti, including Tmogvi. The atlas map marks these four regions. No specific site has been named in Colchis.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No, the Spotted Whip Snake has no venom. When threatened, it usually flees. A large snake can still bite, so do not pick it up. A vertical pupil, on the other hand, may belong to a viper, a Levantine viper or a European cat snake.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      The Spotted Whip Snake has a slender body and a round pupil. Rhomboid blotches run along its grey or brownish back and often merge towards the tail, turning into stripes. A dark slanting streak shows by the eye. Dahl's whip snake has a different pattern: eye-like spots on the neck, not rhombs along the whole back. Vipers and the Levantine viper, by contrast, have a vertical pupil. So a patterned snake with a vertical pupil is not a Spotted Whip Snake.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adult Spotted Whip Snakes are about 1–1.3 m long. This figure applies to the species as a whole.
  - question: What does it eat, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      It reproduces by laying eggs. In general, the species often lays 4–10 eggs. In other parts of its range it catches lizards, small mammals and birds, and even other snakes.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      Mainly by day. That is how it behaves in other parts of its range. In Georgia, expect to see it in the warm season, in dry, rocky places.
  - question: How does it differ from the Urartian ratsnake?
    answer: >-
      The easiest way to tell them apart is by build and blotches. The Spotted whip snake is more slender and faster. Its back blotches are often diamond-shaped and turn into stripes on the tail. The Urartian ratsnake is stouter, with a dark head and larger, rounder blotches. Both snakes are non-venomous, so mixing them up is not dangerous.
