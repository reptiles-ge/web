description: >-
  Eastern Georgia’s high-risk venomous snake — Levantine viper; do not confuse

overview: |-
  The gyurza (Macrovipera lebetinus) is a large [venomous viper](/venomous-snakes) found in Georgia. It is one of the country’s most dangerous snakes for human health. It is found mainly in dry and semi-dry areas of eastern Georgia; it is not known to occur naturally in western Georgia or high mountain areas. Adult gyurzas have a thick body, a broad triangular head and vertically elongated pupils, but color or size alone is not a reliable way to identify them. Its currently accepted scientific name is Macrovipera lebetinus; older sources may use Vipera lebetina or Macrovipera lebetina. A gyurza can sometimes be mistaken for a [lizard-eating snake](malpolon-insignitus) or another large nonvenomous snake. A gyurza [bite](/snakes/giurzas-nakbeni) needs urgent medical care. Do not approach the snake or try to catch or kill it.
  Gyurza venom can cause bleeding, problems with blood clotting and damage to tissue around the bite, as is typical of vipers. Its bite is therefore dangerous even if symptoms seem mild in the first few minutes. Do not treat the bite at home: do not cut the bite site, try to suck out the venom, tightly bind any part of the body, or waste time on folk remedies. Call 112 immediately and follow the advice in the [snakebite guide](/snakes/gvelis-nakbeni).
habitat: |-
  The gyurza is found in dry, rocky places. In Georgia, its typical habitat includes dry areas along the lower reaches of the Iori, Mtkvari and Alazani rivers, Shiraki, Gareji and the dry foothills of [Kvemo Kartli](/regions/kvemo-kartli). It is rare around [Tbilisi](/regions/tbilisi) and Rustavi. On this atlas’s map, the gyurza is marked in [Kakheti](/regions/kakheti), Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. Confirmed locations include Kvareli, the area around Nekresi Monastery, the Alazani Valley and the Colorful Desert.
  In Tbilisi, only a few isolated records appear on the map. Records associated with Rustavi mostly concern dry areas of Kvemo Kartli along the Mtkvari River. The atlas lists the Tbilisi Botanical Garden, Tabori, Nadzaladevi and Saburtalo for Tbilisi, and Rustavi, Gardabani and the Jandari Reservoir in the direction of Rustavi. Such a record confirms that a gyurza could have been encountered at a particular place in a particular year. It does not mean the snake is common or frequently seen in every part of the city.
  The gyurza shelters in rock crevices, spaces under stones and rodent burrows; it sometimes enters buildings. During intense summer heat, it may appear near water. According to Georgian sources, it is found up to an altitude of about 1,500 meters. Its natural range has not been confirmed in western Georgia. The high mountains of the Greater Caucasus and the Colchic forest are not its typical habitat.
diet: >-
  Adult gyurza snakes eat mainly rodents, but they also catch small birds, lizards and other small animals with backbones. Young gyurza snakes hunt small lizards more often. The gyurza usually hunts by ambush. It sometimes climbs a bush or a low tree, including to catch birds.
behavior: |-
  In the South Caucasus, the gyurza is usually active from April through the end of October, although some sources say it may appear as early as late March. In spring and autumn, it often moves around during the day. In the summer heat, it is more active at dusk and at night. It spends the winter in rock crevices or burrows. When you might see one also depends on the weather and daytime temperature: in cooler seasons it may appear during the day, while in very hot weather an encounter is more likely in the evening, at night, or near water.
  The gyurza lays eggs, which sets it apart from other vipers found in Georgia. Mating takes place mainly in April and May. According to Georgian sources, it lays about 15–20 eggs in summer. The young hatch from the eggs after about 35–45 days and are about 23–25 cm long.
  The gyurza does not seek out people and usually defends itself when it feels threatened. If you encounter one, keep a safe distance and do not try to catch it.
conservation: |-
  According to the IUCN’s 2021 assessment, the gyurza faces a low risk of extinction (Least Concern). It is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List; the vipers listed there include [Dinnik’s viper](vipera-dinniki) and the [Caucasian viper](vipera-kaznakovi). Older Georgian encyclopedic sources sometimes link the gyurza to the Red Book, but this does not match the official 2014 list.
  Major threats to the gyurza include changes to its habitat, the growth of settlements, overgrazing by livestock, and deliberate killing by people. By hunting rodents, the gyurza plays an important role in the food chain in dry areas.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The gyurza is a thick-bodied viper with a broad, triangular head and vertical pupils. Its scales have a raised ridge down the middle. In the dry lowlands of eastern Georgia, it is sometimes mistaken for large, nonvenomous racer snakes or the lizard-eating snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      The gyurza has a thick, heavy body. Its broad, triangular head is clearly distinct from its neck. Its snout is blunt, and it has no horn. In the Caucasus, adult gyurzas are often about 1–1.5 metres long. There are also reports of gyurzas reaching 2 metres, but these reports are disputed.
    - >-
      The pupil is shaped like a vertical slit. A prominent raised line runs down the middle of the scales on its back, making the back look rough. The scales on its head, including those above the eyes, are mostly small.
    - >-
      It may be gray, sand-colored or brownish. Its back often has dark spots or bands running across it. These sometimes form a zigzag pattern. Its belly is lighter and often covered with small dark speckles. Some have a more uniform color.
    - >-
      [The common lizard-eating snake](malpolon-insignitus) (Malpolon insignitus) has round pupils and a distinct “eyebrow” above each eye. It is slimmer than the gyurza and is active mainly during the day. [Large racers (Dolichophis)](dolichophis-schmidti) also have round pupils and more elongated bodies. They move quickly and do not have the gyurza’s thick, bulky build.
    - >-
      The [steppe viper](vipera-renardi) is also found in eastern Georgia, but it is much smaller than the gyurza, usually measuring about 40–60 cm long. The [nose-horned viper](vipera-transcaucasiana) is easy to recognize by the horn on its nose and is found in more mountainous areas. [Dinnik’s viper](vipera-dinniki) lives in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus and is not found in the places where the gyurza usually lives.

  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      The gyurza is a venomous snake. Among snakes found in Georgia, its bite is considered one of the most serious threats to human health. It is large and can inject a large amount of venom when it bites. Its venom can cause bleeding, disrupt blood clotting and damage tissue at the bite site. The gyurza does not usually attack people. It bites when it feels threatened — if someone gets too close, steps on it or tries to catch it. If bitten, call 112 immediately.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
      If someone is bitten by a gyurza, call 112 immediately. Have the person lie down, keep them calm, and try to limit their movement as much as possible. Do not cut the bite, suck out the venom, or tie anything tightly around the person. The person must be treated in a hospital. If antivenom is needed, only a doctor should choose and administer it.
  - question: When is the Levantine viper active?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia and the South Caucasus, the gyurza is usually active from April through the end of October. Some sources also report sightings in late March. It often moves about during the day in spring and autumn, while in the summer heat it is more likely to appear at dusk or at night. Its activity always depends on the weather, temperature and location.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      The gyurza is a large, thick-bodied snake. It has a broad, triangular head and vertically elongated pupils. Its scales have a raised ridge down the middle. It has no horn on its nose. Large racers and the lizard-eating snake have round pupils and slimmer, more elongated bodies.
  - question: Is the Montpellier snake the same as the Levantine viper?
    answer: >-
      No. The common lizard-eating snake (Malpolon insignitus) is not a gyurza. Its venom-delivering teeth are at the back of its mouth, and this atlas places it in the medium-risk group. A gyurza has a broad, triangular head, vertically elongated pupils and a thick body. The lizard-eating snake is slimmer, with round pupils and a prominent “eyebrow” above each eye. If a snake bites you and you suspect the bite may be dangerous, call 112 immediately.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      In the Caucasus, adult gyurzas are often about 1–1.5 metres long. The gyurza is the largest viper in Georgia. There are also reports of gyurzas up to 2 metres long, but these reports are disputed.
  - question: Does it lay eggs or give live birth?
    answer: >-
      The gyurza lays eggs. Vipers of the genus Vipera found in Georgia give birth to live young. The gyurza mates mainly in April and May. According to Georgian sources, it lays about 15–20 eggs in summer.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Watch the snake from a safe distance and leave it where it is. Do not try to catch or kill it. If you give it way and do not disturb it, the gyurza will usually move away from you on its own.
