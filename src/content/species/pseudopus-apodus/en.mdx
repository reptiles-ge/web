overview: |-
  The European glass lizard (Pseudopus apodus) is a large legless lizard in the Anguidae family. In Georgia, it is often mistaken for a snake. [You can tell it apart from a snake](/lizards/xvlikis-da-gvelxokeras-gansxvaveba) by its movable eyelids, ear openings and a noticeable fold along the sides of its body.
  A 2026 scientific list confirms its presence in Georgia but describes its range only in eastern Georgia. The map on this page also shows publicly shared observations from Abkhazia and Adjara. The map’s “confirmed” label is based on the number of records; it does not mean the 2026 list confirms its range in western Georgia.
  The European glass lizard is not venomous and poses no danger to people. It is usually 1–1.2 metres long, exceptionally reaching 1.4 metres. If intact, its tail makes up more than half its total length. Worldwide, its risk of extinction is assessed as low. It is not on Georgia’s Red List. A separate national assessment in 2021 also rated its risk of extinction in Georgia as low.
  If you encounter one, leave it where it is and do not pick it up. To help identify it, take a photo from a safe distance.
habitat: |-
  Gvelkhokera is found in warm, open places: plains, sparse woodlands, areas with shrubs, rocky slopes, river valleys, gardens and the edges of village yards.
  In eastern Georgia, it also lives in dry and semi-dry parts of the Mtkvari, Iori and Alazani valleys. The official management plan for Chachuna Managed Reserve records it there.
diet: >-
  Gvelkhokera often eats snails and slugs. It also catches insects and sometimes small lizards or other small animals. Its strong jaws are especially helpful when eating animals with shells. Because it eats snails and slugs, it may be useful in a garden.
behavior: |-
  The gvelkhokera lives on land and is mostly active during the day. In eastern Georgia, it is most active in spring and early summer.
  Females lay eggs; some have also been observed guarding their nests.
  When frightened, it may hiss or bite. This is how it defends itself; it has nothing to do with venom. It can also drop its tail, though this rarely happens in this species. Do not touch a wild animal with your hands—take a photo to identify it.
conservation: |-
  A global assessment considers Pseudopus apodus to face little threat. The species is not on Georgia’s Red List, though a 2021 national assessment also considers it to face little threat.
interaction: >-
  It is harmless to people and has no venom. If you see it in a yard or garden, leave it where it is. Don’t kill it just because you think it’s a snake. If you’re unsure what animal it is, keep a safe distance and take a photo without touching it. Call 112 only if there is a real danger.
