overview: >-
  Dahl’s rock lizard (Darevskia dahli) is a harmless rock lizard of Georgia’s
  mountain forest. It belongs to the lacertid family (Lacertidae). It has no
  venom and is not dangerous to people. It is parthenogenetic: females lay eggs
  without mating, so almost every animal you see is female. In Georgian samples,
  adult snout–vent length is usually about 5.4–5.8 cm. The 2026 checklist treats
  it as a biologically confirmed hybrid form whose parents are the Georgian
  lizard (D. mixta) and the Kura lizard (D. portschinskii).   The named modern voucher is from Kojori. On one rocky outcrop of about 1 ha
  near Tbilisi, dahli was about five times more abundant than the Kura lizard.
  A 2008–2009 survey recorded it at 82 sites in Georgia, mainly in Trialeti
  mountain forest. Do not expect it throughout the country.
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List classifies it as Vulnerable (VU); IUCN lists it as Near
  Threatened.
habitat: >-
  It occurs in Georgia. A 2010 field study named 82 occupied sites: rocky
  outcrops in mountain forest, meadow, and above the treeline. Elevation at those
  points was about 830–1960 m (mean about 1200 m). It shared 28 sites with the
  Kura lizard; overlap with D. mixta was almost nil.   The 2026 barcode and figures are from Kojori. A 2021 microhabitat study on a
  rock near Tbilisi found dahli more tightly tied to a permanent water source
  than the Kura lizard. A later distribution model places the northern edge of
  the main range along the southern Trialeti foothills from Tsalka to the Vere
  gorge near Tbilisi — that is a model, not a complete map. Do not assume it in
  Abkhazia, Adjara, or the Colchis lowlands. This atlas map shows regional
  records, not even density inside a region. The type locality is near Shagali
  in northern Armenia, not Georgia. The species also occurs in Armenia and, in
  the Reptile Database, Ukraine; this page follows Georgian records.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study for this species. Rock lizards usually
  take small invertebrates — insects, spiders, and other arthropods. The 2010
  study treats competition with the Kura lizard as food and cover, and notes no
  marked menu difference among these rock lizards. The Georgian diet has not been
  measured on this page.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day: it basks on rock and hides in crevices. In Georgia, the
  2010 counts followed mass emergence from about April below 1000 m through July
  above 1800 m. It lays eggs. Parthenogenesis means fertilisation is not needed,
  so populations are almost all female. Literature often cites 2–5 eggs per
  clutch; a Georgia-specific clutch size is not published here. Kura lizard
  males sometimes bite parthenogens during mating attempts, but that does not
  create a sexual population. Rare triploid hybrids are infertile. Do not pick
  it up.
conservation: >-
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Decree No. 190) lists it as Vulnerable (VU, criterion
  B1) for a small, fragmented range. The 2009 IUCN assessment is Near Threatened.
  The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically confirmed, with medium confidence
  in the mapped range. Populations are often isolated. Literature names habitat
  change and urbanisation as threats. It is not a candidate species in the
  checklist.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. A bite from a small lizard is
  not a medical emergency. Do not pick it up: the tail breaks easily, and field
  confusion with other Darevskia is common. Photograph the head, flank, and
  cloacal area from a short distance, then leave it.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small rock lizard. Colour is not enough. In Georgia, identify it by range,
    scalation, and an almost all-female population — not by dorsal colour.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is small and slender, the head flattened, the limbs well developed.
      In Georgian samples, adult snout–vent length is usually about 5.4–5.8 cm;
      literature maxima are about 63 mm. The tail is longer than the body and
      breaks easily. Do not handle a lizard to count scales.
    - >-
      The back is grey, brown, or olive, often with darker spots or a network. The
      belly may be yellowish. Those tones occur in other rock lizards — colour
      does not identify the species. The 2026 figures from Kojori show head
      scalation and the preanal plates.
    - >-
      A more reliable mark than colour is paired preanal plates. The Armenian rock
      lizard (D. armeniaca) has one very broad preanal plate, a larger body (max.
      about 74 mm), and a southern Georgian range (Ninotsminda / Saghamo,
      Akhalkalaki) — not Kojori. That contrast follows Darevsky 1967 and a 2017
      comparison.
    - >-
      The Kura lizard (D. portschinskii) often shares the same rocks. Size in
      Georgian samples is similar. The practical difference: portschinskii is
      sexual (males and females); dahli is almost only females. Reliable
      separation uses head scalation and the anal region; colour will not do it.
      D. mixta is a western Georgian endemic absent from Adjara — it almost never
      occurs with dahli.
facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1957 (Darevsky) as Lacerta saxicola dahli. Type locality: near
    Shagali, northern Armenia. The genus Darevskia follows Arribas 1997. Synonym:
    Lacerta dahli.
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist voucher and COI barcode are from Kojori (CaBOL-ID
    1027435). Mitochondrial DNA matches D. mixta and D. armeniaca because mixta
    is the maternal ancestor of both — a barcode is not a field colour mark.
  - >-
    The name dahli honours the zoologist Sergei Konstantinovich Dahl. The
    Georgian name დალის ხვლიკი uses the same eponym.
faq:
  - question: Is Dahl’s rock lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. The named
      modern locality is Kojori. A 2010 survey recorded it at 82 sites in mountain
      forest. It is not countrywide.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      On rocky outcrops in mountain forest, about 830–1960 m, mainly in the
      Trialeti area. Kojori is the named modern point. Do not search for it in
      Abkhazia, Adjara, or the Colchis lowlands. The atlas map shows a region,
      not every crag.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and is not a medical risk. Do not pick it up: the tail
      breaks easily.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A small rock lizard; almost only females. Colour is not enough. Preanal
      plates are paired — the Armenian rock lizard has one broad plate and occurs
      in southern Georgia. The Kura lizard has males and often shares the same
      rocks.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      In Georgian samples, adult snout–vent length is usually about 5.4–5.8 cm.
      Literature maxima are about 63 mm. Total length is greater because of the
      tail; the tail breaks easily and changes the measurement.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia. Rock lizards mainly take
      small invertebrates. It has no venom.
  - question: When is it active, and how does it reproduce?
    answer: >-
      It is active by day. In Georgia, mass emergence in surveys ran from April
      to July, depending on elevation. It reproduces without males and lays eggs.
      Literature often cites 2–5 eggs; a Georgian clutch size is not given here.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Watch from a short distance, photograph the head, flank, and cloacal area,
      and leave it. Do not pick it up. Confusion with other rock lizards is
      normal — colour is not enough.
