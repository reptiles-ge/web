description: >-

overview: >-
  The common cuckoo (Cuculus canorus) is a medium-sized bird in the cuckoo family (Cuculidae). It lays its eggs in other birds’ nests. Its breeding in Georgia is confirmed: it nests there but does not remain outside the breeding season. It is also seen during migration. Its presence in Georgia has been confirmed by a photograph, and the information about its seasonal movements is reliable. An adult is about 32–34 cm long. It is slate gray above and barred below. It poses no direct threat to people. The IUCN classifies it as Least Concern. Georgia’s 2024 bird checklist gives it the same status at the national level.
habitat: >-

The species is associated with open and partly open places: forest edges, farmland with trees, river valleys, reed beds, gardens and similar places where its host songbirds nest. diet: >-
  This page does not present a separate study of its diet in Georgia. In other parts of its range, adults eat various insects, especially hairy caterpillars that other birds often avoid. When visiting a nest, a female may also eat an egg or chick belonging to the bird whose nest it is. These are observations about the species in general, not findings from a study of its diet in Georgia.
behavior: >-
  It is active during the day, and its call is often heard before the bird is seen. The male usually sings a two-note “cu-coo” from a conspicuous perch. The female has a different, humming call. It does not build its own nest: the female lays eggs in other birds’ nests and often removes one of their eggs. It is known to use the nests of many bird species. In Europe, these often include meadow pipits, reed warblers, dunnocks and wagtails. Some of these birds also nest in Georgia, but that does not mean the cuckoo lays eggs in their nests there. This page does not list which birds’ nests it uses in Georgia. Its chick usually pushes any remaining eggs or other chicks out of the nest. In general, the eggs hatch after about 11–13 days. A female can lay many eggs in different nests over a season; guidebooks often give a figure of about 9–22. Adults leave their breeding areas before young birds do. A 2024 list classifies the species as breeding and migratory in Georgia, but not as wintering there.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Cuculus canorus as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List. The global range is huge; some European farmland populations have
  declined, including a well-documented decline in parts of the United Kingdom,
  but the global decline is not treated as fast enough for a higher threat
  category. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution
  No. 190). The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global and national Red
  List category LC. A Georgia-specific population trend is not quantified on
  this page. The EU Birds Directive does not apply in Georgia. Do not catch or
  keep wild birds.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a medium-sized grey bird with a long tail and a striped belly. Its call has two notes. Georgia’s 2024 bird list records it as a migratory bird that nests here, but does not list it as a winter bird. It is often mistaken for a sparrowhawk.
  traits:
    - >-
      This bird is about 32–34 cm long, with a wingspan of about 55–60 cm. It often weighs around 110–130 g; males are slightly heavier than females on average. It has a slim body, pointed wings and a long tail with feathers arranged in steps. An adult male is dark grey with a slate tint on its head, back and chest. Black and white bars begin sharply on its belly. Its eye, the ring around the eye, the base of its beak and its feet are yellow. A grey adult female looks similar, but often has a yellowish or brownish tint on its chest and the sides of its neck.
    - >-
      Some adult females have reddish coloring, which is more common in young birds: the back is reddish brown with dark stripes. Young birds have more noticeable scale-like markings or stripes on their feathers. They often have a white patch on the back of the head and pale edges to their feathers. In flight, the bird can look like a sparrowhawk. Its call is a reliable way to tell them apart: the male sings “coo-coo,” while a sparrowhawk does not make that sound.
    - >-
      Georgia’s 2024 list records the sparrowhawk (Accipiter nisus) both as a year-round resident and as a migratory bird. It has broader, rounded wings and a shorter, striped tail. Its beak is hooked, and its eyes are bright yellow or orange. It does not make a “cuckoo” call. The Levant sparrowhawk (Accipiter brevipes) also nests in Georgia and is migratory. It is a hawk, not a cuckoo. The great spotted cuckoo (Clamator glandarius) is listed as an occasional visitor in the 2024 list, with its presence confirmed by photographs. It has a crest and white spots, is sandy brown rather than grey, and does not resemble a sparrowhawk. The Oriental cuckoo (Cuculus optatus) is absent from that list. These species do not yet have separate pages in this atlas.
    - >-
      It poses no direct threat to people. Do not pick it up. In Georgia in late spring, the gray, striped bird calling “goo-goo” from a treetop is this species, not a sparrowhawk.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the
    IOC World Bird List and the OSME Region List for names: order
    Cuculiformes, family Cuculidae, genus Cuculus.
  - >-
    Widely recognised subspecies include C. c. canorus (Europe east through
    Turkey and much of northern Asia), C. c. bangsi (Iberia and north-west
    Africa), C. c. subtelephonus (Central Asia), and C. c. bakeri (Himalayan
    region and nearby). The nominate form is the one whose mapped range
    includes Turkey and therefore the expected form for the Caucasus. Field
    identification is to species. A measured Georgian specimen series assigned
    to subspecies is not given on this page.
  - >-
    Georgian is გუგული; ჩვეულებრივი გუგული is also used. SABUKO lists the
    family as გუგულისებრნი. English names in use are common cuckoo, European
    cuckoo, and Eurasian cuckoo. The 2024 checklist uses Common Cuckoo.
