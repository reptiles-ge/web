overview: >-
  The Caucasian scorpion (Olivierus caucasicus) is a buthid scorpion. The
  species was first described from Georgia: the type locality is listed in the
  sources as Tiflis, present-day Tbilisi.

  In older literature, the species often appears as Mesobuthus caucasicus and
  under other old combinations. Modern catalogues accept the name Olivierus
  caucasicus. The 2018 revision separated the Caucasian populations from the
  older, broad Central Asian concept.

  On this page, only Tbilisi is marked on the map of Georgia, because that exact
  locality is supported by sources.
habitat: >-
  Within Georgia, a detailed, locally confirmed habitat picture for the Caucasian
  scorpion is not yet available on this page. The type locality is Tbilisi, and
  the material discussed in the 2018 revision is linked to the Caucasus and its
  neighbouring relatively dry areas.

  Habitat can therefore only be described cautiously: it is probably associated
  with warm, dry, rocky or open environments where scorpions hide in shelters
  during the day.

  This does not mean the species occurs in every part of Tbilisi or in every dry
  place in Georgia.
diet: >-
  In Georgia, the diet of the Caucasian scorpion has not been studied separately
  in detail. Like other scorpions, it is predatory and mainly hunts small
  invertebrates and arthropods.

  It uses pincers to catch prey, and the sting and venom to subdue it.
behavior: >-
  Scorpions mostly have a secretive lifestyle. During the day they often hide
  under stones, in cracks, or in other protected places, while they become more
  active in the evening and at night.

  The Caucasian scorpion does not seek people and does not deliberately attack.
  Stings mostly happen when a scorpion is touched by hand, pressed, or its
  shelter is disturbed.

interaction: >-
  The Caucasian scorpion is venomous: it uses venom to subdue prey. This does
  not mean it seeks people or attacks them, but its sting can be painful and
  should be treated with caution.

  Do not handle the scorpion. Seek timely medical advice for severe pain or a child
  who has been stung. Call 112 in Georgia for breathing difficulty, loss of
  consciousness, or other serious symptoms beyond the sting site. See the [guide](/scorpions/morielis-nakbeni) for first steps.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium to fairly large yellowish-brown buthid scorpion with two pincers
    and a segmented tail ending in a sting.

    Exact species identification from coloration or a distant photo is not
    reliable.
  traits:
    - >-
      According to the 2018 revision, adult males grow to about 50-55 mm and
      females to about 58-75 mm. Accordingly, the Caucasian scorpion is larger
      than Georgia's smaller Euscorpius-type scorpions.
    - >-
      The body is yellowish-brown. The carapace and dorsal plates are more
      darkly pigmented, and the anterior part of the fifth tail segment is also
      darkened.

      This coloration alone cannot identify the species accurately.
    - >-
      Many scorpions in the family Buthidae have relatively slender pincers and a
      well-developed tail. These traits help with identification, though a more
      detailed photo and specialist assessment are often needed to settle the
      species.
    - >-
      When comparing it with the [mottled scorpion](mesobuthus-eupeus)
      (Mesobuthus eupeus), body size, coloration details, tail and pincer
      proportions, and the place where it was found are important.

      The fact that a scorpion is "yellow" or yellowish is not enough to
      identify the species.
    - >-
      The [Italian scorpion](euscorpius-italicus) and [Mingrelian
      scorpion](euscorpius-mingrelicus) belong to the family Euscorpiidae, while
      the Caucasian scorpion belongs to Buthidae.

      To tell them apart, pincer build, tail proportions, body size, coloration,
      and habitat should be assessed together. One character alone is not enough
      for accurate identification.
facts:
  - >-
    The accepted scientific name is Olivierus caucasicus (Nordmann, 1840). The
    protonym is Androctonus caucasicus Nordmann, 1840.
  - >-
    In the 2018 revision, Mesobuthus caucasicus sensu stricto is restricted to
    the Caucasus; many older Central Asian records are assigned to other taxa.
  - >-
    The type locality is Georgia, Tiflis, now Tbilisi. This page uses only that
    confirmed administrative unit on the regional map.
  - >-
    Source-supported global range framing includes Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia,
    Iran, the northern Caucasus in Russia, Turkey, and Ukraine with uncertainty.
  - >-
    The English common name used here is a descriptive "Caucasian scorpion."
    Scientific sources often rely on the Latin name rather than a fixed English
    common name.
