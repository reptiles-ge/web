commonName: "Derjugin's Lizard"
location: "Lagodekhi, Sioni, Mulakhi, Racha, Baniskhevi, Kintrishi"
description: >-
  Derjugin's Lizard (Darevskia derjugini) is a small, non-venomous forest lizard. It lives on the ground in western Georgia and in the forest belt of the east.
overview: |-
  Derjugin's Lizard (Darevskia derjugini) is a small forest lizard that lives on the ground. It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. In Georgia, it is found in the west and in the forested belt of the east. Its head and body together, without the tail, are often about 4–6.5 cm long, while the tail is often nearly twice as long as the body. [You cannot reliably identify it by color alone](/lizards/identifikacia).
habitat: |-
  Derjugin's Lizard lives mainly in forests and at forest edges. There it stays on the ground, in the grass and among fallen leaves and roots. It can also be seen beside forest roads, in clearings and along the banks of rivers and streams.
  A 2026 list names Lagodekhi, Sioni, Mulakhi, Racha, Baniskhevi and Kintrishi among its locations. These are separate places. Their listing does not mean the lizard lives everywhere between them.
diet: >-
  Derjugin’s lizard mainly eats small animals without backbones, including insects and spiders.
behavior: |-
  Derjugin's Lizard is active during the day and lays eggs. In warm weather, it basks in the sun at forest edges and in open areas. When threatened, it quickly runs away and hides.
conservation: |-
  A 2009 global assessment rates Derjugin's Lizard as close to being threatened with extinction. It is not, however, included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Across much of the area where it lives, forests are being lost and its habitat is being split into separate patches. As a result, its numbers are probably declining.
interaction: >-
  Derjugin’s lizard has no venom and poses no risk to human health. If you catch it, it may bite you or drop its tail to defend itself. Do not pick it up—take a photo and leave it where it is.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a small forest lizard that lives on the ground. Its head is less flat than that of rock-dwelling [Darevskia species](/lizards/darevskia). Color alone makes it hard to identify. So look at its body features too, and at where and in what kind of habitat you saw it.
  traits:
    -  |-
      Derjugin's Lizard has a less flat head than many Darevskia rock lizards. The scales on its back are rounded and mostly smooth, though some have a slightly raised line down the middle. Some distinguishing features, such as the small holes on its thighs, are visible only up close. Do not catch the lizard to count them.
    -  |-
      Back colour varies from lizard to lizard: dark brown, olive green or brownish. Pale stripes often run along the sides, and some lizards also have faint pale spots. In young lizards, the rear part of the tail is often turquoise or blue.
      Males often have a greenish belly and may have blue spots on their sides. Females more often have a yellowish or whitish belly. Because colour can vary this much, you cannot identify the species accurately by colour alone.
    -  |-
      Derjugin's Lizard may resemble the [Meadow Lizard](darevskia-praticola) (Darevskia praticola), the [Pontic Meadow Lizard](darevskia-pontica) (Darevskia pontica) and some Darevskia species that live on rocks in the same area. Rock-dwelling species, including [Brauner's Rock Lizard](darevskia-brauneri) (Darevskia brauneri) and the [Obscure Rock Lizard](darevskia-obscura) (Darevskia obscura), have flatter heads. Derjugin's Lizard moves mainly on the forest floor, but you may also find rock lizards in the same valley.
      Brauner's Rock Lizard has several scales between the central scale on its temple and the scale by its ear. This feature is visible only in a clear close-up photo. You cannot tell these species apart by color alone.
    -  |-
      The name “ართვინის ხვლიკი” refers to Artvin in northeastern Turkey. The lizard used to describe the species is believed to have been found there. In a 2026 Georgian list, however, the species appears as “კავკასიური ტყის ხვლიკი”. Older sources also call it Lacerta derjugini; today its scientific name is Darevskia derjugini.
    -  |-
      Derjugin's Lizard lives in western Georgia and in the forest belt of the east. It is less common on dry plains and in open steppe.

  - question: Is Derjugin’s lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In western Georgia and in the forest belt of eastern Georgia. Derjugin's lizard lives in both parts of the country, but in the east its home is the forest belt. So if you are looking for this lizard in eastern Georgia, look in the forest belt.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It lives in forests and at forest edges. It is often on the ground, among grass and fallen leaves. You may also see it near forest roads and along stream banks.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. Derjugin's Lizard has no venom and is not dangerous to human health. When threatened, it usually runs away. It may bite if you catch it, so do not pick it up.
  - question: How do I tell it from a rock lizard or a meadow lizard?
    answer: >-
      Compared with rock-dwelling Darevskia species, Derjugin's Lizard has a less flat head. It is found mainly in wooded areas, on the ground, while rock lizards more often move about on rocks. Colour alone will not reliably tell them apart, so also look at the head shape and at the place where you saw the lizard.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      The head and body together, excluding the tail, are often about 4–6.5 cm long. It is a small lizard, but its tail is long: often almost twice the length of the body. So with the tail included, the lizard is much longer than that figure suggests.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It mainly eats small animals without a backbone. Its food includes insects and spiders.
  - question: When is it active, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      It is a daytime lizard and lays eggs. It is especially active in warm weather, so you have a better chance of spotting it on a warm day.
  - question: What should I do if I find one?
    answer: |-
      Observe it from a distance, take a photo, and leave it where it is. You do not need to catch a lizard to identify it. If the lizard is moving on a vertical rock face and has a noticeably flat head, it may be another Darevskia species rather than Derjugin's Lizard.
