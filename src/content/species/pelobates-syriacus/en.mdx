description: >-
  A burrowing amphibian with a vertical pupil and a large hind-foot spade.
  Confirmed in dry eastern Georgia; harmless to people.
overview: >-
  The eastern spadefoot (Pelobates syriacus) is a burrowing amphibian of dry
  eastern Georgia and is harmless to people. It belongs to Pelobatidae — not a
  true toad and not a marsh frog. Adult body length is usually about 6–9 cm at
  species level. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat it as biologically confirmed
  in Georgia. Globally it is Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (2021). On
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List it is EN A2a (declining in Georgia). Do not expect it
  in humid western Colchis. Boettger described it in 1889; the type locality is
  Haifa, not Georgia. English names include eastern spadefoot and Syrian
  spadefoot. After the 2019 genus revision, Balkan populations are usually
  treated as Pelobates balcanicus; COI divergence between Georgia and the
  Balkans exceeds 10%. Georgian populations are tentatively assigned to P. s.
  boettgeri by region (Dufresnes et al. 2019; Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026). The
  closest relative, P. fuscus, does not occur in Georgia.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia you are most likely to meet it in dry or semi-arid steppe and
  shrubland of the east, and sometimes in human-altered land, where
  fishless ponds, pools, or canal widenings hold water in spring.
  Tarkhnishvili, Serbinova and Gavashelishvili (2009) mapped tadpoles in 88
  ponds in south-eastern Georgia in 2003–2008. The range roughly matches
  semi-arid landscapes; Gori is given as a western reference. It is usually
  absent from forest, though a few breeding sites sit at forest edges. In that
  study the species did not occur where annual rainfall exceeded 760 mm. The
  2026 checklist barcode is from Chachuna (Kakheti); the figure locality is
  Tbilisi. Tarkhnishvili (1996) listed Turtle Lake near Tbilisi (Delwig 1928),
  lakes Kumisi and Chili-Tba, Tsodoreti and Akhaldaba lakes, the Tbilisi–
  Rustavi stretch, and a fossil from the lower Khrami; the Manglisi record
  needs checking because the landscape is more humid than typical. Some 1996
  sites may no longer exist — that does not cancel the 2000s pond map. It is
  not expected in the Black Sea basin of the Caucasus or the forest belt of the
  Greater Caucasus. Caucasus literature sometimes cites about 0–1900 m; a
  separate elevational range for Georgia is not published here. This atlas map
  shows regional records and does not mean the species is evenly present in
  every highlighted region.
diet: >-
  No Georgian stomach-content study is cited on this page. At species level,
  adults take molluscs, myriapods, spiders, orthopterans and other small
  invertebrates on land (AmphibiaWeb). Tadpoles eat mainly algae and plant
  debris in water. Do not treat it as biological control of garden pests.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly nocturnal and spends the day underground. It digs with the large
  yellowish inner metatarsal tubercle (the “spade”) and also uses rodent
  burrows and spaces under stones. AmphibiaWeb notes possible aestivation in
  hot dry weather; drought in the Caucasus has been linked to mass mortality.
  Hibernation is in soil, among roots, or under rocks, usually singly, rarely
  in groups of 2–6. In Georgia breeding is early and short: late March to early
  April, about two weeks, according to the 2009 study. If a pond is dry then,
  the season is lost — marsh frogs and green toads can still breed later.
  Tadpoles are large; they are easy to see in early June (body length already
  over about 4–6 cm) and metamorphose in late June or early July. Eggs need
  about 2.5–3 months to metamorphosis, so ponds must hold water from March to
  July. In Georgia they avoid ponds with fish. AmphibiaWeb gives about
  5500–6500 eggs per clutch at species level; a Georgian clutch size is not
  published here. Males lack external vocal sacs. Do not catch wild amphibians.
conservation: >-
  IUCN Red List Least Concern (IUCN SSC Amphibian Specialist Group 2021) —
  this assessment follows the taxonomic split and does not treat Balkan P.
  balcanicus as the same unit as older combined concepts. A 2024 update exists
  for the same taxon; this page still treats the global status as LC. Georgia’s
  2014 Red List lists EN A2a (“declining in Georgia”) — the national status is
  stricter than the global one. In the 1990s Georgian populations were
  considered threatened and some localities may have been lost; the 2003–2008
  map showed more breeding ponds in the south-east than the eight 1996 points
  implied. Documented pressures are loss or drying of breeding ponds, stocking
  with fish, drought, and land-use change. Breeding sites are scarce: in the
  2009 dataset P. syriacus used under 5% of ponds used by marsh frogs and
  green toads. It is not listed on CITES. Do not take wild amphibians.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous in the snake sense, and a bite is not a medical
  emergency. Like other amphibians it has skin mucus; irritating secretions are
  better documented in P. fuscus within the same genus — do not handle it. If
  you touch one by accident, wash your hands and keep them away from your
  eyes. Photograph from a distance and leave it in place.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A stocky, short-legged amphibian with a vertical pupil, relatively smooth
    skin, and a large yellowish digging spade on the hind foot. Georgia’s only
    Pelobates.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is robust, the head large and flat, the hind legs short. The
      pupil is a vertical slit. The eardrum is not visible. The skin is
      relatively smooth with small warts — not a true toad’s rough skin. The
      hind toes have deeply indented webbing. The key mark is the inner
      metatarsal tubercle: large, spade-shaped, yellowish — used for digging.
      The back is yellowish or grey with large greenish blotches; the belly is
      whitish-grey without a pattern. Adult body length is often about 6–9 cm
      at species level (Dufresnes et al. 2019, P. s. boettgeri: females mean
      68 mm, range 40–92 mm; males mean 69 mm, 57–83 mm). A separately measured
      Georgian series is not on this page. The sexes are similar in size; in
      the breeding season the male’s shoulder gland is more prominent.
    - >-
      Tadpoles are among the largest of any amphibian; literature maxima reach
      about 165 mm. In Georgia they are already large and easy to see in
      fishless ponds in June. After metamorphosis, toadlets often bury
      themselves in pond-shore mud. In some water bodies tadpoles overwinter
      and finish the next year — that is a species-level possibility, not a
      rule for every Georgian pond. Do not pick it up: pupil, body shape, and
      the spade if visible are enough.
    - >-
      The Caucasian parsley frog (Pelodytes caucasicus) is smaller and slimmer,
      with a small rounded inner tubercle; Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 tie it to
      humid western Caucasian forest, not dry eastern steppe. Green toad
      (Bufotes viridis) and Caucasian toad (Bufo verrucosissimus) have
      horizontal pupils and large parotoid glands; the skin is warty. Marsh
      frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) and Caucasian brown frog (Rana macrocnemis)
      have horizontal pupils and dorsolateral folds; marsh frogs often share
      ponds but stay aquatic. Tree frogs (Hyla) are green with toe pads. The
      common spadefoot (P. fuscus) does not occur in Georgia.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. A stocky “toad-like” amphibian with a vertical pupil
      and a large hind-foot spade, seen at night in dry eastern country, fits
      this species better than a true toad. Very large tadpoles in a fishless
      pond in June also point to a spadefoot. Do not handle it.    - >-

faq:
  - question: Does the eastern spadefoot occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 list it as biologically confirmed and
      present in eastern Georgia.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In dry and semi-arid eastern landscapes, at fishless ponds. Named points
      include Chachuna, Tbilisi, and 88 ponds mapped in 2003–2008 in the
      south-east, with Gori as a western reference. Do not expect it in Colchis
      forest.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A stocky, short-legged amphibian with a vertical pupil, relatively smooth
      skin, and a large yellowish spade on the hind foot. It lacks toad
      parotoids and the horizontal pupil of marsh frogs.

      No. It is not venomous in the snake sense. The skin produces mucus; do
      not handle it.
