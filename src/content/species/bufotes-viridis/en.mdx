description: >-
  The Green Toad is not venomous to people and has green spots on its back. In Georgia it is common in open country but rare in dense forest.
overview: >-
  The green toad (Bufotes viridis) is a toad with green spots on its back. It is not dangerous to people: it has no venomous bite. In Georgia it lives mainly in open country, and 2026 data confirm that it occurs here. Its length is usually about 5–12 cm. This figure applies to the species as a whole, and females are generally larger than males. Globally it is at low risk of extinction, and it is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. It belongs to the true toad family (Bufonidae), and Georgia’s 2026 checklist still calls it Bufotes viridis.
habitat: |-
  The green toad is an animal of open country. It avoids dense, closed forest, though it may appear in forest clearings. Data from 1996 show it is widespread across the Caucasian Isthmus, from forest to desert, including settled land. Look for it in steppe, clearings, gardens, parks and settlements. It lays its eggs in shallow pools, ditches and ponds, usually less than 50 cm deep. This depth figure applies to the species as a whole.

  In Georgia it is common, except on the humid south-western Black Sea coast, where it is rare. Records from Batumi and Poti are old: they date from 1913 and were repeated in later works. By 1996 it was known from 28 sites in eastern Georgia and 14 in the west. As of 2026, knowledge of its distribution in Georgia is rated as medium confidence.

  The DNA data and illustration published in 2026 come from a toad from Pantishara Canyon (Dedoplistskaro Municipality, Kakheti). Field studies in 1993–1994 confirmed that the green toad breeds in Borjomi Canyon. The study site lies at about 900–1200 m. That is the elevation of one local site, not a limit the species never crosses in Georgia.

  Publicly shared photos show the green toad in 10 regions: Kakheti, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Tbilisi, Kvemo Kartli, Shida Kartli, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Imereti, Racha-Lechkhumi and Kvemo Svaneti, Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti, and Adjara. Abkhazia has a single record so far and Guria none. Few or no records from a region do not mean the toad is absent there. Record counts show how often people look for animals in an area, not how many toads live there.
diet: >-
  Adult green toads hunt mainly on land, and ants make up an important part of their diet. They also eat beetles, spiders and other small crawling animals. Young toads that have just changed from tadpoles catch smaller prey: springtails, small beetles, mites and flies. Tadpoles feed on algae and decaying plant matter. These diet data describe the species in general, not Georgian toads specifically.
behavior: |-
  The Green Toad is mainly active at dusk and at night and hides by day. In dry weather it may come to water at night to drink. During breeding it may also appear by day near shallow water.

  In Borjomi Canyon it breeds most actively from April to June. It lays its eggs in water in long strings. There, females of this open-country toad lay more than one clutch a season, while forest toad females usually lay one. At the same site, most females can already breed after three winters. The oldest females recorded there were 9–12 years old.

  It spends the winter on land: in burrows, holes or under stones, and sometimes in water. It is not dangerous to people. If you find one, do not pick it up and leave it where it is, and wash your hands after any contact.
conservation: >-
  In the 2023 global assessment, the Green Toad (Bufotes viridis) faces little risk of extinction. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. The amphibians listed there are the [Caucasian Salamander](mertensiella-caucasica) and the [Eastern Spadefoot](pelobates-syriacus). It is, however, listed in Appendix II of the Bern Convention. It often lives in gardens, parks and settlements too. Local threats include the loss of meadows and the drying out of marshes. Forest loss, on the other hand, may even help it, because it is an animal of open country.
interaction: |-
  The green toad (Bufotes viridis) is not venomous to people and usually poses no danger when you meet it. Large glands behind its eyes release a defensive fluid. This fluid can irritate the eyes and mouth. So do not pick the toad up or disturb it.

  In Georgia it is widespread. It is especially common in open, dry and semi-dry places, including gardens, the edges of settlements and farmland. In the evening or at night you may also come across it on a road, in a yard or near a lit area. If you find one in nature, it is best to leave it where it is. Do not try to catch it, take it home or move it somewhere else. Do not let pets touch the toad with their mouths.

  If you touch the toad, wash your hands well with soap and water. Until then, keep your hands away from your eyes and mouth. If the fluid gets into your eye, rinse it right away with plenty of clean water. If the irritation is strong or does not go away, see a doctor. The best way to protect the toad is to leave it where it lives.

  summary: >-
    You can recognise the Green Toad first of all by the irregular green or olive spots on its back. It has a short, warty body, a horizontal pupil and large glands behind the eyes. In Georgia it is most often confused with the [Caucasian Toad](bufo-verrucosissimus), so check the spots on its back first.
  traits:
    - >-
      The back is grey or olive, marked with irregular green or olive spots. The sides often show red or orange warts, while the belly is grey. In the dry south-east of Georgia, some toads have also been described with a narrow stripe down the middle of the back.
    - >-
      The pupil is a horizontal slit, and the eardrum is usually visible. Large glands sit behind the eyes. They release a defensive fluid that can irritate the eyes or mouth.
    - >-
      The skin is warty, and length is usually about 5–12 cm. Females are generally larger than males. In the breeding season, males develop rough pads on their fingers. The pads appear first on the first finger, and at the peak of breeding often on the second and third as well.
    - >-
      The [Caucasian Toad](bufo-verrucosissimus) is brown or reddish-brown, with no mottled pattern of green spots on its back. It lives more in forest, while the Green Toad is an animal of open country. Both occur in Borjomi Canyon, but in different spots. To tell them apart, look at the back first: of the two, only the Green Toad has green spots.
    - >-
      The [Eastern Spadefoot](pelobates-syriacus) has a vertical pupil, while the Green Toad's is horizontal. The spadefoot lacks large glands behind the eyes, but it has a hard digging “spade” on the hind foot. Treefrogs have smooth skin and sticky discs on their toes, whereas the Green Toad's skin is warty.
faq:
  - question: Does the green toad occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes, the Green Toad is found in Georgia, and 2026 data confirm this. According to 1996 records, it is a common species across the country. The exception is the humid Black Sea coast, where it is scarce.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It lives in open, dry or semi-arid country, especially in eastern Georgia. That means steppe, forest clearings, gardens and settlements. Known sites include, for example, Pantishara in Dedoplistskaro Municipality (Kakheti) and Borjomi Gorge. On the humid Black Sea coast, though, it is scarce. Publicly shared photos show it in 10 regions. Abkhazia has a single record so far and Guria none. Few records do not mean the toad is absent there.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venomous bite and is harmless to people. It has large glands behind its eyes, and their fluid can irritate the eyes or mouth. So do not pick it up, and wash your hands after any contact. If a dog takes the toad in its mouth, this can harm the dog.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      You can recognise the Green Toad by its green or olive spots. It has a short, warty body, a horizontal pupil and large glands behind the eyes. A plain brown forest toad is a different species. So look at the colour first: spots point to the Green Toad.
  - question: How does it differ from the Caucasian toad?
    answer: >-
      Look at the back first. The green toad has green spots on its back, while the [Caucasian toad](bufo-verrucosissimus) is brown and lacks that spotted pattern. They also live in different places: the green toad lives in open country, and the Caucasian toad is found more in forest. Both live in Borjomi, but in different spots. So if you see green spots on a toad's back, then of these two, it is the green toad.
  - question: Is Bufotes sitibundus a separate species from the green toad?
    answer: >-
      No. Georgia's 2026 checklist still uses the name Bufotes viridis, because no evidence of geographic or genetic isolation between Caucasian and European green toads has been found. Some studies still call the green toads of the Caucasus B. sitibundus. Whichever name is used, you identify the animal in the field the same way.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      The green toad is about 5–12 cm long. This figure applies to the species in general. Females are generally larger than males. Young toads are smaller: in Borjomi Canyon, they are about 3–5 cm long at the end of their second summer.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Watch it and leave it where it is. It poses no health risk to people, but still do not pick it up. If you find it in a pool, do not move it somewhere else. And wash your hands after any contact.
