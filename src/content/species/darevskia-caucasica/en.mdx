overview: >-
  The Caucasian rock lizard (Darevskia caucasica) is found in rocky areas of the Greater Caucasus in Georgia. A 2026 Georgian list includes records of the lizard and DNA data from Zakagori Fortress, Mount Tbatana and Lebarde. This atlas has not yet settled whether to treat it as a separate species: researchers are still studying which lizards belong to the caucasica group and how members of that group in Georgia differ genetically.
  According to the “Records by region” table on this page’s map, distribution is confirmed in Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Kakheti, and Racha — Lechkhumi — Kvemo Svaneti. In Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti, and Abkhazia, only isolated finds have been recorded so far. Those regions are not treated as part of the range. A place named in the checklist does not by itself make the whole region part of the range.
  The lizard is not venomous and poses no risk to human health. To identify it in the wild, the location, the rocks immediately around it and the features of its scales all matter. Green or brown colouring alone is not enough to identify the species.
habitat: >-
  This page treats a region as part of the range only where the map table status is “Distribution confirmed”: Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Kakheti, and Racha — Lechkhumi — Kvemo Svaneti. Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti, and Abkhazia are not treated as part of the range.
  The 2026 checklist names the Truso Valley, Zakagori Fortress, Mount Tbatana, and Lebarde. The closest genetic data used for comparison also comes from Truso. Those are named places, not a regional status. Look for the species on sunny rocks, stony slopes, roadside rock debris or rocky parts of a valley. It quickly hides in cracks.

behavior: >-
  This rock lizard is active during the day. It warms itself on a rock and slips into a crevice when threatened. Georgian sources do not specify which hours of the day it is active, exactly when it emerges after winter, or which months it breeds. For photo records, it helps to note the location and elevation above sea level and take clear photos of its back, side, and the scales on its head. There is no need to catch the lizard.
conservation: >-
  Globally, the species is in the “Least Concern” category, meaning it faces a low risk of extinction. However, this assessment is old and covers its entire range, not just Georgia. A 2026 list notes that D. caucasica in Georgia differs markedly in its genes from groups in the North Caucasus. A 2025 study of its genetic material also found several genetically distant groups within the species. This profile does not say how the species’ numbers are changing in Georgia, estimate how many there are in the country, or give a confirmed status for it on Georgia’s Red List.
interaction: >-

  summary: >-
    The Caucasus lizard is a small, fast rock lizard (Darevskia). Its color varies, so color alone cannot reliably distinguish it from closely related species. More reliable clues are where it was found, its rocky habitat and, when possible, the features of the scales on its head and just in front of the opening at the rear of its body.
  traits:
    - >-
      It has a slender body suited to life on rocks. Its tail is long, though after an injury it may look shorter or as if it has grown back. It can still be identified despite this.
    - >-
      It may be greenish, yellowish-brown, gray or brown, with pale stripes and dark spots along its sides. These features help describe its appearance, but they are not enough to identify the species: several Darevskia species look alike.
    - >-
      The 2026 images of the Zakagori/Truso material show the scales on the head and the plates in front of the opening at the rear of the body. These details are needed to identify a lizard in a photo among similar rock lizards.
    - >-
      The [Dagestani lizard](darevskia-daghestanica) is hardest to distinguish in the upper reaches of the Terek and near Tbatana: colour does not help, and sources also mention possible crossbreeding. In these places, one photo is often not enough.
    - >-
      The [Colchian rock lizard](darevskia-mixta) is associated with the damp valleys of western Georgia. In the Greater Caucasus, it comes into contact with caucasica only in a small area. The [Abkhazian rock lizard](darevskia-alpina) is also considered a candidate for this atlas; colour alone is not a reliable way to identify it.
