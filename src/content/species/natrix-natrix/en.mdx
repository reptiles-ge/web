commonName: Grass snake
location: Georgia — throughout, most numerous in the humid west
description: >-
  Non-venomous grass snake in Georgia — yellow collar; not a viper.
overview: >-
  The grass snake (Natrix natrix) is a non-venomous snake in Georgia — yellow
  collar, harmless to people. It is semi-aquatic; in the west it is often
  confused with the Caucasus viper, and near water with the dice snake
  (Natrix tessellata). The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as a biologically
  confirmed species.
  A 2021 survey of snakes lists it, with the dice snake and the smooth snake,
  as widespread through Georgia. It is most numerous in the humid west; in the
  dry east it is scarcer, and the dice snake (Natrix tessellata) is often the
  more obvious water snake. Georgian populations belong to the subspecies
  N. n. scutata, including the striped morph formerly called N. n. persa. The
  former “big-headed grass snake” (Natrix megalocephala) is now treated as a
  morph of this subspecies, not a separate species. It is Least Concern on the
  IUCN Red List and is absent from Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  The 2021 study treats the species as occurring throughout Georgia. This atlas
  maps it in all 12 regions. That does not mean it is equally common everywhere:
  in the humid west — the Colchis lowlands, Adjara, Guria, Samegrelo, Abkhazia,
  Imereti — it is locally ordinary; on the dry eastern plains it is more tied to
  remaining marshes, lakes, and river margins. The 2026 checklist includes a
  concrete eastern record from Tianeti Municipality (Tushurebi village).
  Distribution confidence in the checklist is medium: a wide range is supported,
  but occupancy is not counted municipality by municipality. It prefers damp
  ground: marshes, floodplains, lakes, streams, humid forest, and riverside
  meadows. Compared with the dice snake it chooses cooler, shadier, wetter
  sites; N. tessellata favours more open, rocky, semi-arid shores. In Colchic
  forest it has been recorded climbing trees to bask and to hunt treefrogs. It
  also occurs in gardens, parks, and farmland where amphibians are plentiful.
  It ranges from the lowlands into mountain valleys; a single published maximum
  elevation for Georgia is not established in the sources used here.
diet: >-
  It feeds mainly on amphibians — frogs, toads, and tadpoles. It also takes
  fish, but less than the dice snake. The Georgian snake review lists Natrix
  as hunting amphibians and fish; a separate quantitative diet study from
  Georgia is not used on this page. It swallows prey alive. It hunts in water
  and along the shore; sometimes it travels inland through woodland in search
  of toads.
behavior: >-
  Active in the warm season, mostly by day. Most Georgian snakes are diurnal;
  in hot weather it may also move at dusk or after dark. The active period is
  longer in the western lowlands than in the high mountains; a single national
  calendar is not published in the sources used here. It is egg-laying. Mating
  occurs in spring; in summer it typically lays about 8–40 eggs in a moist,
  warm spot — rotting vegetation, compost, or bank soil. Young hatch in late
  summer or early autumn. These figures are species-level; a separate Georgian
  fecundity study is not cited here. When threatened it releases a foul-smelling
  cloacal secretion, flattens its head like a viper, or feigns death — often
  with the tongue out and the mouth open. It does not seek people. Do not pick
  it up: it is harmless, but handling stresses the animal and leaves a risk of
  confusion with a viper.
conservation: >-
  Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List —
  among snakes that list includes, with others, the Caucasus viper and Dinnik’s
  viper. It is locally common in the humid west. It is listed on Appendix III
  of the Bern Convention. Local threats include wetland drainage, water
  pollution, road mortality, and killing after confusion with vipers. A grass
  snake in a garden or park usually means amphibians are present; it is not a
  danger.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A harmless, semi-aquatic snake. The first cues are a yellow collar behind
    the head and a round pupil. In the west it is easily confused with the
    Caucasus viper; near water, with the dice snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      Behind the head there is often a yellow, white, or cream collar — two
      half-moons with a dark blotch behind. This is the most reliable field
      mark, but in melanistic individuals it may be weak or absent.
    - >-
      The back is greenish, grey, or brown; the dorsal scales are keeled.
      Dark, almost black animals are frequent in Colchis. Some individuals have
      two pale dorsal stripes (the former “persa” morph); this pattern has been
      recorded near Lake Paliastomi. The pupil is round.
    - >-
      Georgian sources treat it as a medium-sized snake: typically about
      0.7–1.2 m. Females are larger than males. European maxima above 1.5 m
      occur but are not treated here as typical for Georgia. The head is only
      slightly distinct from the neck, not a short triangular viper head.
    - >-
      The dice snake (Natrix tessellata) lacks the collar; it has checkered
      blotches and is more tightly bound to open water. Grass snakes eat mainly
      amphibians; dice snakes eat mainly fish. In Georgia the dice snake is
      more common in semi-arid country.
    - >-
      The Caucasus viper has a vertical pupil, keeled scales, and a short, stout
      body; it has no collar. It is venomous. The smooth snake has smooth scales
      and no collar. The glass lizard has movable eyelids and an ear opening —
      it is a lizard, not a snake. Do not pick up an unidentified snake.

faq:
  - question: Is the grass snake venomous?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and is harmless to people. When threatened it may
      release a foul smell, flatten its head, or feign death. Do not pick it
      up — in the west it is easily confused with the Caucasus viper.
  - question: How does it differ from the dice snake?
    answer: >-
      The grass snake often has a yellow collar and prefers damp, shady
      habitats; it feeds mainly on amphibians. The dice snake has checkered
      blotches, is more tightly bound to water, and in Georgia is more common
      in semi-arid areas; most of its diet is fish.
  - question: How can I tell it from a viper?
    answer: >-
      Look for a yellow or white collar behind the head, a round pupil, and a
      relatively slender body. In Colchis it may be almost black. Vipers have
      a vertical pupil and a short, stout body; they lack a collar and are
      venomous.
  - question: Is the grass snake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. The 2021
      snake survey lists it as widespread through Georgia.
  - question: Where might I encounter it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      At marshes, lakes, streams, and in damp forest. It is most numerous in
      the humid west. In the east it is scarcer and more tied to remaining
      water. The map shows all 12 regions; that reflects a wide range, not even
      density.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly frogs, toads, and tadpoles. It also takes fish, but less than the
      dice snake. It swallows prey alive.
  - question: Does it lay eggs or give live birth?
    answer: >-
      It lays eggs. Mating is in spring; in summer it typically lays about
      8–40 eggs in a moist, warm spot. Young hatch in late summer or early
      autumn. These are species-level figures.
  - question: What should I do if one comes into the yard?
    answer: >-
      Keep your distance and do not pick it up. It is harmless and often follows
      amphibians. If you are not sure of the species, treat it as if it could be
      a viper and leave it alone.
