commonName: Cinereous vulture
location: Year-round resident and migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A very large, almost uniformly dark scavenging raptor. Confirmed in Georgia as
  a breeding resident and a migrant on the 2024 national bird checklist.
  Nesting is documented in the south-east dry zone.
overview: >-
  The cinereous vulture (Aegypius monachus) is a very large scavenger; in
  Georgia the 2024 checklist confirms it as a breeding resident and a migrant.
  It belongs to Accipitridae; Aegypius is a monotypic genus. In Georgian the
  official name is სვავი. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia
  codes it YR-R; M: a breeder recorded in the country through the year, and a
  passage migrant. Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence
  quality is high (H), with photographs. Adults are uniformly dark brown to
  black, with a paler bluish-grey head and a massive bill. It is not a threat to
  people on ordinary encounters; nests should not be approached. IUCN lists the
  species as Near Threatened. Georgia’s 2014 Red List treats it as Endangered
  (EN, D1). The 2024 checklist assigns national category NT; those two national
  assessments disagree.
habitat: >-
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level resident and migrant status but does not
  publish a regional gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly on the Colchis
  lowland or on high alpine ridges. Gavashelishvili, McGrady and Javakhishvili
  (2006), from surveys in 1994–2002, restrict breeding to about 1,993 km² in
  south-east Georgia at 100–900 m: steppe (Bothriochloa ischaemum), semi-desert
  (Artemisia fragrans) and arid woodland (Juniperus, Paliurus, Pistacia). All 31
  nests found were in mature juniper, mostly on steep north-facing slopes. Named
  protected areas in that paper are Vashlovani Nature Reserve (about half the
  pairs in 2002), and the Korugi, Iori and Chachuna sanctuaries. Abuladze (1983)
  reported breeding elsewhere in the country; by 2006 no breeding had been seen
  in those areas. In July 2024 SABUKO tagged a chick still in the nest. VCF
  states that the last estimate was about 50 breeding pairs, mostly along the
  Georgia–western Azerbaijan border pastures. That is a named breeding range, not
  a full national map. This page includes a photograph by Vazhiko Kochiashvili
  from Vashlovani Protected Areas (2020-08-05) and photographs by Mamuka
  Tsiskarauli (31 May) without a named locality here.
diet: >-
  It is a scavenger: it feeds on dead animals, not as a typical hunter of live
  large prey. In the south-east Georgian breeding range the 2006 paper links food
  to winter (October–April) sheep flocks and high mortality under extensive
  livestock farming; grazing there is almost absent in other seasons. VCF (2024)
  names the same pastures as among the highest year-round densities of extensive
  livestock in the Caucasus. No separate percentage diet study for Georgia is
  given on this page. At species level it takes large-mammal carcasses, including
  livestock. Do not bait wild birds or put out poisoned carcasses.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. In flight the wings are very long and broad, with
  fingered tips; the tail is short and wedge-shaped. When soaring it often holds
  the wings almost flat, not in a deep V, which helps separate it from griffon
  vulture. It nests in loose clusters or as scattered pairs. In Georgia in 2002
  the mean nearest-neighbour distance among identified pairs was about 1.1 km
  (range 440–2,469 m); the shortest distance between nests was 20 m. At species
  level the clutch is usually one egg; incubation and nestling periods are long.
  A separate Georgian egg-date series is not given on this page. On 30 July 2024
  SABUKO tagged a nestling (7.1 kg) still in the nest. The 2024 checklist also
  codes the species as a migrant. VCF states that Caucasian cinereous vultures,
  like those in Central Asia, leave the breeding grounds in winter and winter
  farther south, unlike more sedentary European birds; a full analysis of
  Georgian GPS tracks is not on this page. Voice is rarely heard in flight;
  hisses or grunts may occur at the nest. Do not handle wild birds or approach
  nests.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International lists Aegypius monachus as Near Threatened on the IUCN
  Red List. The 2024 Georgian checklist gives global and national category NT
  and notes that national status improved from EN to NT. Government of Georgia
  Decree No. 190 (2014) places the species on the Red List as სვავი, Aegypius
  monachus, EN, criterion D1 (very small population). That is the legal list; the
  2024 ornithological checklist codes it nationally as NT. The 2015 European Red
  List of Birds annex gives Georgia 9–30 breeding pairs (2005–2012, medium
  quality). The Flyway Action Plan table lists Georgia at 50 breeding pairs
  (2016, quality G, trend stable). VCF (2024) repeats about 50 pairs as the last
  estimate. A complete 2020s national census is not on this page.
  Gavashelishvili et al. (2006) linked the restricted nesting range to human
  disturbance (seasonal grazing) and climate (rainfall), not to an obvious lack
  of food or nest trees in that sample. At species level main threats are
  poisoning, habitat degradation, collision with power lines and wind turbines,
  illegal shooting, and nest disturbance. Do not catch or kill wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people on ordinary encounters. It is not venomous. It
  has a large bill and talons; do not handle it. Pairs can be aggressive at the
  nest. Do not approach nests. Capture or killing of a Red List species is
  prohibited. Poisoned baits are a direct risk to scavengers.
danger: Harmless

  summary: >-
    A very large, almost uniformly dark scavenger with a short wedge-shaped tail
    and a massive bill. Confirmed in Georgia as a breeding resident and a migrant
    on the 2024 checklist. Usual confusion is with griffon, bearded and Egyptian
    vultures and with large eagles.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level length is often about 100–110 cm, wingspan about
      250–295 cm, mass often about 7–12 kg; females are generally larger than
      males. No separate measured Georgian series is given on this page. Adult
      plumage is uniformly dark brown or black; the head is paler bluish-grey
      with sparse down and a dark cowl-like ruff. Juveniles are blacker, including
      the head. The legs are partly bare; the cere and bill base are pale, the
      bill massive. In flight the silhouette is rectangular broad wings and a
      short wedge-shaped tail.
    - >-
      Eurasian griffon (Gyps fulvus) is also YR-R; M on the 2024 checklist; this
      atlas has a page for that species. It has a pale sandy body, contrasting
      dark flight feathers, a longer neck and a pale ruff; it often soars with
      wings in a V. Cinereous vulture is uniformly dark and holds the wings
      flatter. Bearded vulture (Gypaetus barbatus) is YR-R: narrower wings, a
      long diamond-shaped tail, rusty body and a black “beard” in adults.
      Egyptian vulture (Neophron percnopterus) is BB; M and much smaller; adults
      are white with black flight feathers and a yellow face. Golden eagle
      (Aquila chrysaetos) has a page in this atlas: longer tail, golden nape,
      fully feathered legs; it hunts live prey and is not a carrion specialist.
      Common buzzard (Buteo buteo) is much smaller.
      Bearded and Egyptian vultures do not yet have separate pages here.
    - >-
      It is not a direct threat to people on an ordinary hike. Do not handle it.
      A very large uniformly dark bird with a short wedge-shaped tail over dry
      juniper slopes in the south-east is more likely this species than a griffon
      or an eagle.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1766 as Vultur monachus; the accepted name is
    Aegypius monachus. The genus Aegypius is monotypic. The 2024 Georgian
    checklist follows the OSME Region List: order Accipitriformes, family
    Accipitridae. IOC and ITIS accept the species. Georgian dictionaries and
    Wikipedia often misspell the genus as Aegipius; the correct spelling is
    Aegypius.
  - >-
    The official Georgian and Red List name is სვავი. English names are
    cinereous vulture (2024 checklist / IOC), also Eurasian black vulture and
    monk vulture. The American black vulture (Coragyps atratus) is a different
    family and does not occur in Georgia.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes are YR-R; M, data quality A, global and checklist
    national Red List NT, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). The
    legal Red List (2014) is EN D1. That is country-level confirmation plus named
    south-east nest records, not a full regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the cinereous vulture found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia accepts it as a
      year-round resident and a migrant (YR-R; M), with photographs.
  - question: Where can it be seen in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Breeding is documented in south-east dry steppe and juniper woodland at
      about 100–900 m, including Vashlovani, Korugi, Iori and Chachuna protected
      areas. This page does not map the whole country. Do not approach nests.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A very large, uniformly dark bird with a short wedge-shaped tail, a massive
      bill and, in adults, a paler head. Griffon has a pale body and often soars
      with a V. Eagles have a longer tail and a golden nape.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      At species level length is often about 100–110 cm, wingspan about
      250–295 cm, mass often about 7–12 kg. No separate Georgian measurement
      series is given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Carrion, especially large-mammal carcasses. In south-east Georgia food is
      linked to winter sheep flocks. It is not a typical hunter of live large
      prey.
  - question: When is it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The 2024 checklist codes it as both resident and migrant. It is in the
      south-east in the breeding season. VCF states that Caucasian birds leave
      the breeding grounds in winter; a precise winter map is not given here.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R means a breeding resident. Nests are documented in mature juniper
      in the south-east. A nestling was tagged in the nest in July 2024.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      Harmless on ordinary encounters; do not approach nests. Globally NT. The
      2014 Red List is EN D1; the 2024 checklist codes it nationally as NT. Do
      not catch wild birds.
