commonName: Caucasian badger
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A stocky, short-legged member of the weasel family with a black-and-white
  striped head and a grizzled grey back. It is a native species that lives in
  Georgia all year round.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian badger (Meles canescens) is a mammal of the weasel family. In
  Georgia people simply call it the badger, though some sources still list it as
  the European badger (Meles meles). Under the current scientific
  classification, the badger living in Georgia and the South Caucasus is a
  separate species, Meles canescens. The badger is not venomous. It is mainly a
  risk to people when someone tries to catch it or corners it: then it may bite
  you. The badger is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. IUCN has not yet
  assessed Meles canescens as a separate species.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia the badger lives in forests, woodland edges, scrub, farmland
  landscapes and foothills, especially where the soil is good for digging a
  burrow (sett). National map data show the badger almost throughout Georgia and
  in almost all habitat types, though it is rare in the high-mountain alpine
  belt. The same map gives an upper limit of about 2,700 m above sea level.
  Badger records are known from Vashlovani, Lagodekhi, Batsara, Kintrishi,
  Ajameti Managed Reserve and Sataplia. The badger is also mentioned in the
  management plan of the Iori floodplain hunting farm.

  The map on this page shows confirmed distribution in Kakheti, Imereti, Kvemo
  Kartli, Adjara, Mtskheta-Mtianeti and Samtskhe-Javakheti, the regions with at
  least one public photo observation of a badger. If there is no record from
  another region, that does not mean the badger is absent there. The number of
  records shows how often people watch and photograph animals in a place, not
  how many badgers live there.
diet: >-
  The badger is an omnivore and eats what it finds in its surroundings. Its food
  may include earthworms, insects, fruit, small vertebrates and crops. Badger
  diet varies considerably across Europe. In wetter regions earthworms are often
  important, while in dry, Mediterranean-type environments insects, fruit and
  other available food play a larger role. Studies in western Anatolia also
  describe fruit, insects and earthworms in the badger's diet. In Georgia too,
  the badger probably eats whatever is available and changes its diet with the
  local environment and the season.
behavior: >-
  The badger is mainly active at night and at dusk, but it sometimes appears by
  day too, especially in quiet places with little disturbance. Seeing a badger
  in daylight does not by itself mean it is sick.

  The badger lives in a burrow system called a sett. A sett may have several
  entrances, underground tunnels and chambers. Heaps of dug-out soil are often
  visible near the entrances. A well-built sett may be used for many years,
  sometimes by several generations.

  Badgers of the genus Meles have delayed implantation: after fertilisation, the
  embryo stops developing for a while. Studies across the species' wider range
  show that mating may take place in spring and summer, while cubs are usually
  born in late winter or early spring. A litter often has several cubs.

  Badger tracks are broad and easy to notice. Each foot has five toes. The long
  claws on the front feet, which the badger uses to dig its sett, often show
  clearly in the tracks. The track shown on this page was photographed in Vayots
  Dzor, Armenia, on 18 August 2026.
conservation: >-
  In its 2016 assessment, IUCN lists the European badger (Meles meles) as Least
  Concern (LC). That is a global assessment, and it included some populations
  that are now often treated as the separate species Meles canescens. Meles
  canescens itself does not yet have a separate IUCN assessment.

  The badger is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. On the national map its
  status is given as NE, and the population is described as “small”. That is the
  map's description, not the result of a new nationwide count.

  In Georgia the badger is listed as a hunting species. In the official list its
  Latin name is mistakenly written as Martes meles, but the animal meant is the
  badger, not a marten. Hunting seasons and quotas are set by the current
  documents of the responsible agencies and change from time to time. Locally,
  badgers may be threatened by hunting, road deaths and conflict with people,
  for example near crops and apiaries.

  Do not try to handle or catch a wild badger.
interaction: >-
  The badger has no venom. If you try to catch it or corner it, it may bite you.
  It has a strong jaw and long claws. In principle, any wild mammal can carry
  rabies. In Georgia the [red fox](vulpes-vulpes), [golden
  jackal](canis-aureus), [grey wolf](canis-lupus) and dog are more often named
  as the main sources of rabies. Do not feed or handle a badger. Keep your dogs
  vaccinated.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A badger is easiest to recognise by its head: two black stripes run along a
    white head. The body is stocky and low, the legs and tail are short, and the
    back is grizzled grey.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is low and broad, the legs are short and the head is
      wedge-shaped, narrowing towards the front. The ears are small, with a
      white edge along the top. The Caucasian badger (Meles canescens) is
      slightly smaller than the European badger (Meles meles). In nearby
      regions, including Anatolia, described badgers have a head-and-body length
      (without the tail) of about 73–84 cm and weigh about 5–9 kg. These figures
      come from nearby regions, not from Georgia. The European badger,
      especially in northern Europe, is often heavier in autumn, so its size and
      weight should not be applied directly to Caucasian badgers in Georgia.
    - >-
      In the field, a badger is easiest to recognise by its black-and-white
      facial “mask”. A white stripe runs from the muzzle towards the forehead,
      with a black stripe on each side passing through the eye and ear. The back
      and sides are grizzled grey; the legs and often the belly are darker.
      Badgers in the South Caucasus often have a darker, brownish-grey back than
      European badgers in northern Europe. Meles canescens and Meles meles are
      hard to tell apart reliably by appearance alone.
    - >-
      The [least weasel](mustela-nivalis) is much smaller than a badger, has a
      long, slender body and no black-and-white face mask. Beech martens and
      pine martens are slimmer than a badger, have long bushy tails and climb
      trees more often. The [red fox](vulpes-vulpes) is built like a dog, with a
      narrow muzzle, relatively long legs and a long bushy tail. The
      [raccoon](procyon-lotor) has a black mask across its face and usually 4–7
      dark rings on its tail. A badger instead has lengthwise black-and-white
      stripes on its head, and its tail is the same colour as its back, with no
      rings. The honey badger (Mellivora capensis) is not considered a
      characteristic species of Georgia’s fauna.
    - >-
      The badger is not venomous, but it is a wild animal, so do not try to
      handle it. If you see an animal with a black-and-white face, short legs
      and a grey body near a woodland edge or on a road, it is more likely to be
      a badger than a marten.
  coloration: >-
    The Caucasian badger's back and sides are a dirty grey with brownish tints.
    The head is white with two pure black stripes. Each stripe starts on the
    upper lip, runs through the eye, gradually widens towards the back and
    covers the whole base of the ear. Between the two black stripes, a broad
    white stripe runs from the tip of the nose over the forehead and crown. The
    ears are black with a broad white edge along the top. The lower lip and chin
    are white, and the throat is black.

    In general, a badger's chest and legs are black too, and the belly is black
    with a brownish tint. The tail is the same colour as the back. Colour varies
    noticeably between individuals: some are lighter, almost silvery, while
    others are darker or have a yellowish tinge on the back. In summer the coat
    on the back is darker and more yellowish, and the black of the belly is less
    deep and brownish. Males and females do not differ in colour. Newborn cubs
    have short white fur, young badgers are lighter than adults, and with age
    the white part of the hairs increases and the badger becomes lighter. This
    general description is based mainly on material from the European badger.

facts:
  - >-
    Blanford described Meles canescens in 1875 from Iran (Abadeh). Abramov and
    Puzachenko in 2013, using cranial morphometrics in agreement with molecular
    data, recognise four parapatric species: Meles meles (Europe), Meles
    canescens (Southwest Asia and the mountains of Middle Asia), Meles leucurus,
    and Meles anakuma. The name used on this page for the Georgian population is
    Meles canescens. Order Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Mustelidae,
    genus Meles.
  - >-
    Satunin described Meles meles minor in 1905, type locality Borjomi, Georgia.
    That name is now treated as a synonym of Meles canescens. Other historical
    names include Meles urartuorum and Meles meles ponticus. Official Georgian
    documents still write Meles meles. Do not assign a field individual to a
    subspecies.
  - >-
    The boundary between Meles meles sensu stricto and Meles canescens lies in
    the North Caucasus and is not sharply defined; hybridisation has also been
    mentioned. For Georgia this page follows canescens as the accepted
    Transcaucasian name and does not claim that northern Meles meles is
    confirmed in Georgia.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as native for Meles meles (lumped concept). It is in
    Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s 2002 annotated list and in Eliava et al.
    2007. That is country-level confirmation plus named localities, not a full
    regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the badger found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The badger lives in Georgia and is part of the country’s mammal
      fauna. Older sources and international assessments often list Georgia's
      badger as Meles meles, while the current scientific classification places
      it in Meles canescens.
  - question: Why do some sources say Meles meles and others Meles canescens?
    answer: >-
      Older sources often list Georgia’s badger as Meles meles, because badgers
      of the South Caucasus, Anatolia and the Near East used to be included in
      that species. Under the current scientific classification, these badgers
      form a separate species, Meles canescens. That is why newer sources more
      often use this name for the Georgian badger, while some official Georgian
      documents still use the older name, Meles meles. In the field you cannot
      easily see the difference: both have the typical black-and-white badger
      face.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Badgers occur almost throughout Georgia, though they are rare in the
      high-mountain alpine belt. You may find them in forests, woodland edges,
      scrub and farmland, especially where the soil is good for digging burrows.
      Badger records are known from Vashlovani, Lagodekhi, Batsara, Kintrishi,
      Ajameti, Sataplia and the Iori floodplain. The map on this page shows
      confirmed distribution in Kakheti, Imereti, Kvemo Kartli, Adjara,
      Mtskheta-Mtianeti and Samtskhe-Javakheti.
  - question: How can I identify a badger?
    answer: >-
      You can recognise a badger by the black-and-white stripes on the face, a
      stocky short-legged body, a grizzled grey back and a short tail. The
      [least weasel](mustela-nivalis) is much smaller and more elongated and has
      no such facial mask. A marten is slimmer and has a long bushy tail.
  - question: How large is a badger?
    answer: >-
      The Caucasian badger is slightly smaller than the European badger. Badgers
      described from Anatolia have a head-and-body length (without the tail) of
      about 73–84 cm. These figures come from a neighbouring region, so for
      Georgia’s badgers they are only a rough guide.
  - question: What does a badger eat?
    answer: >-
      The badger is an omnivore and eats what is available locally. Its food may
      include insects, earthworms, fruit, small vertebrates and sometimes crops.
      Its diet can change with the place and the season.
  - question: When is a badger active?
    answer: >-
      The badger is mainly active at night and at dusk, though it may sometimes
      appear by day. It stays in its home area all year and does not move
      elsewhere with the seasons.
  - question: Is a badger dangerous? Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      The badger is not venomous and normally does not attack people. However,
      it is a wild animal and may bite you if you try to catch or handle it, so
      keep a safe distance. IUCN assesses Meles meles globally as Least Concern.
      In Georgia the badger is not included in the 2014 Red List and is defined
      as a hunting species. Hunting seasons and quotas are set by current
      official rules.
