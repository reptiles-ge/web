commonName: Northern raven
location: Year-round resident in Georgia
description: >-
  A very large all-black crow with a heavy bill and a wedge-shaped tail.
  Confirmed in Georgia as a year-round breeding resident on the 2024 national
  bird checklist; the checklist lists the nominate subspecies Corvus corax
  corax.
overview: >-
  The northern raven (Corvus corax) — also called common raven — is the largest
  crow in Georgia and one of the heaviest passerines worldwide. Georgian name is
  ყორანი. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia codes it YR-R: a
  breeder present throughout the year. Seasonality is classed as high-quality
  (code A). Occurrence quality is high (H), with photographic evidence. The
  checklist lists subspecies C. c. corax. Adults are about 54–71 cm long at
  species level, with a wingspan of about 116–153 cm. It is not venomous and is
  not a threat to people on ordinary encounters. IUCN lists the species as Least
  Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns the same category nationally.
habitat: >-
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level year-round residence but does not publish a
  regional gazetteer. Do not assume it is equally common in every settlement or
  on every alpine ridge. At species level it uses cliffs, open mountains,
  woodland edge, river gorges, and mixed farmland; it often nests on cliffs or
  in tall trees and may use high structures. NPLG’s Georgian dictionary entry
  treats it as a resident found widely in the country, more numerous near the
  upper forest line, and notes vertical occurrence to about 2,500 m, with
  foraging higher when food allows — that is a secondary summary, not a new
  field census on this page. Named encounter contexts (tour lists, protected-area
  checklists, and photo localities; not a full national map): Greater Caucasus
  birding areas around Kazbegi; Svaneti (including a 2016 Mestia photograph
  gallery record); Tusheti National Park on regional Avibase checklists. Dense
  city centres are poorer habitat than cliffs, gorges, and mountain pastures.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range it is
  an opportunistic omnivore and scavenger: carrion, small mammals, birds and
  eggs, invertebrates, fruit, and human refuse. It may take weak or dying
  animals but is not a typical large-game hunter. Do not put out poisoned baits
  or carcasses. It is not food for people.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. Flight is strong and often soaring; the tail looks
  diamond- or wedge-shaped in flight, not squared like a typical crow. Pairs
  hold territories; young birds may gather in loose groups. The usual call is a
  deep, resonant croak, not the higher caw of hooded crow. Species-level nests
  are large stick platforms on cliffs or trees; clutch size is often about
  four to six eggs, with incubation of roughly three weeks — those figures are
  from the wider range, not a Georgian nest sample on this page. The 2024
  checklist treats the species as a year-round resident breeder in Georgia, not
  as a passage migrant. Local birds may make short seasonal movements for food;
  that is not the same as long-distance migration. Voice on this page: xeno-canto
  XC727695 (Johannes Worona); no Georgian recording is included here. Do not
  handle wild birds or approach nests closely.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Corvus corax as Least Concern on the IUCN Red
  List, with a very large range and an increasing global trend in the last
  published assessment used here. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns
  global and national Red List category LC. A current national population census
  is not given on this page. The species does not appear as ყორანი / Corvus
  corax on the 2014 Georgian Red List (Government Decree No. 190). At species
  level, historical persecution, poisoned baits, and habitat change can reduce
  local numbers; those pressures are not quantified for Georgia on this page.
  Do not catch or kill wild birds without a legal basis.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and is not a medical threat to people. A large bill and
  strong feet can injure if the bird is handled or cornered. Leave nests alone.
  Wild birds must not be kept as pets. Carrion-feeding means it may appear near
  roads and livestock carcasses; that is normal scavenging, not an attack on
  people.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Very large all-black crow with a heavy bill, shaggy throat feathers
    (hackles), and a wedge-shaped tail in flight. Deep croak. In Georgia the
    usual confusion is hooded crow by size and colour pattern, and alpine
    choughs when all-black birds soar over mountains.
  traits:
    - >-
      Size and shape: about 54–71 cm long at species level; wingspan about
      116–153 cm. Much larger and heavier-billed than hooded crow or rook. In
      flight the tail is graduated and looks wedge- or diamond-shaped, not
      straight-edged. Soaring is frequent. A separate measured series from
      Georgia is not given on this page.
    - >-
      Plumage: adults are glossy black with blue or purple iridescence in good
      light. Throat feathers are long and pointed (hackles). The bill is deep
      and slightly curved; the nasal bristles cover much of the base. Juveniles
      are duller, with a less glossy plumage and a pinkish gape when very young.
      Sexes look alike in the field.
    - >-
      Hooded crow (Corvus cornix) is a year-round resident in Georgia on the
      2024 checklist and is grey-bodied with a black head, wings, and tail — not
      all black. Rook (Corvus frugilegus) is also confirmed there (YR-R; M; WV);
      adults have a bare greyish face around the bill base and a more peaked
      crown; the tail is not strongly wedge-shaped. Red-billed and yellow-billed
      choughs (Pyrrhocorax pyrrhocorax, P. graculus) are smaller alpine corvids
      with slender, curved bills that are red or yellow, not black. Carrion crow
      (Corvus corone) is not on the 2024 Georgian checklist as a confirmed
      species; do not assume every all-black crow is a raven. This atlas does
      not yet have separate pages for those lookalikes.
    - >-
      It is harmless to people on ordinary encounters. A large black bird
      soaring over Caucasian cliffs with a deep croak and a wedge-shaped tail is
      typically this species.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 in Systema Naturae. Order Passeriformes,
    family Corvidae, genus Corvus. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the OSME
    Region List and uses the English name Northern Raven and the trinomial
    Corvus corax corax. IOC World Bird List uses Common Raven for the species.
    Field identification is to species; subspecies is not usually separable in
    Georgian field conditions beyond the checklist assignment.
  - >-
    Handbooks place Caucasus birds in the nominate subspecies C. c. corax
    (northern and central Europe east to Lake Baikal, south to the Caucasus and
    northern Iran). Other subspecies occur elsewhere in the Holarctic range and
    are not expected as Georgian breeders.
  - >-
    Georgian ყორანი is this species. რუხი ყვავი is hooded crow (Corvus cornix).
    English names in use are Northern Raven (2024 checklist / OSME) and Common
    Raven (IOC / many field guides). Russian is ворон.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R, data quality A, global and national Red List LC,
    occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    confirmation plus the named localities above, not a complete regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the northern raven found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident breeder (YR-R), with high-quality
      photographic evidence.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Often over cliffs, mountain pastures, gorges, and open country away from
      the densest city centres. Named encounter contexts include Kazbegi-area
      birding routes, Svaneti (including Mestia photo records), and Tusheti
      National Park checklists. This page does not map every municipality.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Very large all-black crow, heavy bill, shaggy throat hackles, wedge-shaped
      tail in flight, deep croak. Hooded crow is grey and black. Alpine choughs
      have red or yellow bills.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 54–71 cm long at species level, wingspan about 116–153 cm. A
      separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Carrion and a wide mix of animal and plant food. Diet has not been studied
      separately for Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year as a resident breeder, according to the 2024 checklist (YR-R). It
      is not coded as a passage migrant there.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means resident breeder present through the
      year.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless to people on ordinary encounters. Globally and nationally
      it is coded LC on the 2024 checklist. It is not on the 2014 Georgian Red
      List. Do not catch wild birds or use poisoned baits.
