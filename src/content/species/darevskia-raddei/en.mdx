overview: >-
  Radde’s rock lizard (Darevskia raddei) is nonvenomous. Its presence in Georgia has been confirmed along the upper reaches of the Mtkvari River in Samtskhe-Javakheti. The 2026 Georgian list records it as a confirmed species, rather than merely a possible one. Confirmed records from Georgia are linked to Aspindza Municipality: Pia, Tmogvi, and the Mtkvari valley near the Turkish border. This does not mean the species occurs throughout Georgia. Distinguishing D. raddei from D. nairensis is difficult. The 2026 list does not treat them as clearly separate species. It places the lizards in Georgia closer to the nairensis lineage, while retaining nairensis as a subspecies. Worldwide, the species is rated Least Concern, meaning its risk of extinction is low. This page does not establish whether its numbers in Georgia are rising or falling.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, the species has been confirmed along the upper Kura River in Samtskhe-Javakheti, near the Turkish border. It has been recorded in Aspindza (Fia) and Tmogvi; the images show one individual found in Fia, in the Kura Valley. Samtskhe-Javakheti is marked on this atlas’s map based on those records. This does not mean that every suitable rocky site there has been checked. The species is also known from Armenia, southern Azerbaijan, northwestern Iran and northeastern Turkey. Data from across its range place it at elevations of 600–2,500 metres above sea level, in wooded, shrubby, grassy and rocky places. This elevation range is not a measured limit for Georgia.

    - >-
      It belongs to the true lizard family (Lacertidae). It has a slender body, well-developed legs and a long tail. Unlike a legless lizard, it has legs. You would usually look for it near cracks in rocks or on rocky slopes, not in water.
    - >-
      The back is often brown or olive-brown, with dark and light spots. Color varies, so Georgia’s Darevskia species cannot be told apart by color alone.
    - >-
      In Georgia, where a lizard was seen is an important clue when identifying Rade’s lizard: confirmed records come from the upper reaches of the Mtkvari River, around Phia–Tmogvi. A similar lizard photographed in another region should not automatically be identified as Rade’s lizard.
    - >-
      A close-up photo or a specimen is needed to examine the scales on the head and the plates in front of the anal opening. The images in the 2026 list show these features on an individual Fia.
    - >-

    - >-
      This page does not present D. nairensis as a separate species in Georgia. The 2026 list also does not consider it clearly distinct from raddei and continues to treat it as a subspecies.
