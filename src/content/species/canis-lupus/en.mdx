commonName: Grey wolf
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A large dog-like predator with grey-brown fur, erect ears and long legs. It
  lives in Georgia all year round and is more common in the mountains than in
  the lowlands.
overview: >-
  The grey wolf (Canis lupus) is a mammal of the dog family (Canidae) and the
  largest wild member of that family in Georgia. Georgia is part of its native
  range, and the wolf appears on Georgia’s national mammal checklists.
  Across the species, total length is often about 1.3–1.6 m, while body mass
  varies widely. The wolf has no venom: the main risks to people are a bite and
  rabies, and there are uncommon reports of attacks. IUCN assesses it globally
  as Least Concern; it is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia the grey wolf is more common in the mountains than in the lowlands.
  A 2020 study confirms this pattern and shows that wolf numbers have grown in recent decades and that wolves are seen more often in
  the lowlands of western Georgia, including near the Black Sea coast. For a 2014
  genetic study, most Georgian samples came from three areas: Kazbegi, Svaneti
  and Colchis. A 2015 study interviewed local people in the Lanchkhuti area and
  Kazbegi District; it links wolf conflict there with growing livestock numbers and abandoned
  habitat. One field photograph on this page was taken in the David Gareja
  desert on 7 December 2019. The map on this page shades Kakheti,
  Samtskhe-Javakheti and Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti:
  regions where public iNaturalist photo observations reach the table threshold.
  Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Abkhazia, Adjara, Imereti and Shida Kartli have only
  individual records, which do not yet count as regional distribution. Few or no
  records do not mean the species is absent, and record counts reflect
  observation effort, not density, so do not assume that wolves are equally
  common across the whole country. Across its whole range the species lives in
  many kinds of
  habitat: forest, mountains, open country and sometimes the edge of
  settlements.
diet: >-
  The grey wolf is a carnivore. Across the species it hunts ungulates and
  medium-sized mammals and also eats carrion. What it eats in a given place
  depends on which prey is available there. In some parts of
  Georgia — for example the Colchis lowland and the Kazbegi region
  — the wolf's main prey is domestic ungulates: cattle, sheep, goats, donkeys
  and horses. Potential wild prey in Georgia
  includes red deer, European roe deer, wild boar and chamois, and the most
  common non-ungulate prey in the South Caucasus is the brown hare. In a 2010
  questionnaire survey on the Vashlovani winter pastures, most people
  surveyed named the wolf as the main problem predator. These are people's
  answers, not a full verified count of animals killed. This page has no
  nationwide diet study (stomach-content analysis) for wolves in Georgia.
behavior: >-
  The grey wolf is mainly active at dusk and at night, but where it is little
  disturbed it can also be seen by day. It lives in a family group, the pack,
  which is usually made up of the parents and their offspring. Wolves also
  travel alone or in pairs. Across the species, pregnancy lasts about 63 days,
  and pups are often born in spring, often 4–7 in a litter. These figures come
  from the species' wider range, not from dens studied in Georgia. Wolf tracks
  look like those of a large dog but are often more oval and larger. The
  forefoot has five toes, although one of them, the dew claw, often does not
  show in the print; the hind foot has four. Claw marks usually show, and a
  wolf's toe prints line up symmetrically. A Eurasian lynx print is round and
  asymmetrical and usually shows no claw marks, although a lynx does use its
  claws in heavy or hard-packed snow. In soft snow or a wet marsh a wolf spreads
  its toes, and its tracks can then be confused with a lynx's. Wolves rarely
  bark; howling is more typical. Do not feed or handle wild animals.
conservation: >-
  On the IUCN Red List, Canis lupus is assessed globally as Least Concern
  (2018 assessment, 2023 amended version). The global population trend is assessed as stable, although some
  regional populations are threatened. The wolf is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red
  List (Government Resolution No. 190). It is listed as a hunting species;
  hunting quotas and seasons are set in agency documents, and current figures
  are not given here. Under CITES, Georgia’s wolves are in Appendix II; Appendix
  I applies only to the populations of Bhutan, India, Nepal and Pakistan.
  A 2020 study found that wolf numbers in Georgia have increased
  in recent decades, but a national census is not confirmed on this page. Local
  threats can include hunting, conflict over livestock, interbreeding with
  free-ranging dogs and deaths on roads. Do not handle wild animals.
interaction: >-
  The grey wolf has no venom, but it can bite. It is a large predator, and its
  killing of livestock is a documented source of conflict in Georgia. A 2014
  study cites reports that wolves have attacked livestock more often since
  the 2000s, as well as several reports of attacks on people. These are reports
  only, not medically verified cases checked for this page. In a survey in
  the Lanchkhuti area and Kazbegi District, people who
  knew less about wolf habits and kept livestock in less suitable ways reported
  more wolf damage. The study recommends returning to simple traditional
  practices, such as guarding herds with shepherd dogs and adding bulls to the
  herds. In Georgia the wolf is named among wild carnivores that can carry
  rabies. Do not feed or handle a wolf, and keep your dogs vaccinated. If you
  meet a wolf, keep your distance and back away slowly; do not run away from it
  and do not move towards it.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The grey wolf is a large dog-like predator. Its fur is grey-brown and often
    mottled, its ears are erect and triangular, its muzzle is thick and its legs
    are long. It lives in Georgia all year round.
  traits:
    - >-
      Across the species, total length is often about 1.3–1.6 m, the tail about
      30–50 cm and shoulder height often about 70–90 cm. Weight varies widely:
      across the range females weigh about 18–55 kg and males 20–80 kg; males
      from the far north are near the upper end. Caucasian wolves are usually in
      the middle of that range. There are no separate measurements of Georgian
      wolves on this page. Males are usually slightly larger than females. The
      muzzle is thick, and the ears are relatively short and erect.
    - >-
      The coat is usually grey, mixed with brown, black and pale hairs. The
      belly is paler. Fully black or nearly white wolves occur in the species,
      but a typical wild wolf in Georgia is grey-brown. The tail is bushy and
      hangs low. A white tail tip, like the one a red fox has, is not a reliable
      sign for identifying a wolf.
    - >-
      The [golden jackal](canis-aureus) (Canis aureus) also occurs in Georgia.
      It is much smaller than a wolf, with shorter legs, a more uniform
      yellowish-grey coat and a finer muzzle. The [red fox](vulpes-vulpes)
      (Vulpes vulpes) is smaller still and rusty red, with black backs to its
      ears and often a white tail tip. A free-ranging dog can match a wolf in
      size and colour. A wolf typically has more erect ears, a thicker muzzle,
      longer legs and larger feet, but a wolf–dog hybrid cannot always be
      identified in the field.
    - >-
      The wolf is not venomous. Its bite or saliva can carry a risk of rabies.
      Do not handle a wolf, especially one that approaches without fear or
      behaves aggressively. A wolf seen by day is not necessarily rabid.
  coloration: >-
    The grey wolf's coat colour varies across the species' range. The fur can be
    a mix of white, grey, brown (including reddish-brown) and black hairs. In
    Arctic populations a wolf can be pure white, while some colour phases are
    almost uniformly black. This description covers the species as a whole, not
    Georgian wolves specifically.

facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758. The accepted name is Canis lupus. Order
    Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Canidae, genus Canis. The domestic
    dog (C. l. familiaris) is a domestic form of the same species; this page
    covers the wild wolf.
  - >-
    Caucasus literature historically names C. l. cubanensis (Ognev, 1923).
    Genetic data do not show Caucasian wolves as isolated:
    haplotypes are shared with European and West Asian populations. Do not
    assign a Georgian individual to a subspecies in the field with confidence.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual names are მგელი and რუხი მგელი; some texts use
    ნაცრისფერი მგელი. In English: grey wolf or gray wolf; older texts also say
    timber wolf.
  - >-
    Georgia is part of the species' native range. The wolf is on the 2002
    annotated list of Georgia's mammals. A 2007 review of Georgia's animal biodiversity lists it as LC. That is
    country-level confirmation, not a regional map.
  - >-
    A 2014 genetic study documents gene flow between wolves and livestock
    guarding dogs in Georgia: more than about 13% of sampled wolves had
    detectable dog ancestry. A hybrid may be less wary of people; that does not
    mean every wolf seen near a village is a hybrid.
faq:
  - question: Is the grey wolf found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. IUCN lists the grey wolf as native to Georgia, which means it occurs
      here naturally. It is on Georgia’s national mammal checklists and on the
      list of hunting species. It lives in Georgia all year round.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia the wolf is found more often in the mountains than in the
      lowlands. Areas named in research include Kazbegi, Svaneti, Colchis and
      the Lanchkhuti area. One field photograph on this page was taken in the
      David Gareja desert on 7 December 2019. In recent decades wolves have also
      been seen more often in the lowlands of western Georgia, including near
      the Black Sea coast. Regions with enough photo observations are Kakheti,
      Samtskhe-Javakheti and Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti; Mtskheta-Mtianeti,
      Abkhazia, Adjara, Imereti and Shida Kartli have individual records so far.
      See the map on this page.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      The grey wolf is a large, long-legged dog-like predator with grey-brown
      fur, a thick muzzle and erect ears. A [golden jackal](canis-aureus) is
      smaller and more yellowish. A [red fox](vulpes-vulpes) is rusty red and
      often has a white tail tip. Telling a wolf from a dog is not always easy,
      because a free-ranging dog can look like a wolf in size and colour.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      Across the species, total length is often about 1.3–1.6 m. Weight varies
      widely, and males are usually slightly larger than females. There are no
      separate measurements of Georgian wolves on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      The grey wolf is a carnivore. In some parts of Georgia, for example the
      Colchis lowland and the Kazbegi region, its main prey is domestic
      ungulates — cattle, sheep, goats, donkeys and horses. Potential wild prey
      in Georgia includes [red deer](cervus-elaphus), [European roe
      deer](capreolus-capreolus), [wild boar](sus-scrofa) and chamois. In the
      South Caucasus, its most common prey apart from ungulates is the brown
      hare. Its diet across the whole country has not been measured on this
      page.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      Mainly at dusk and at night. It can also appear by day, especially where
      it is little disturbed. The wolf lives in Georgia all year round and does
      not migrate.
  - question: Can you hear it?
    answer: >-
      Yes. A wolf's howl carries a long way. The recording on this page was
      made in Poland (xeno-canto XC961981); there is no recording made in
      Georgia here.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      The wolf is not venomous, but it can bite. It also kills livestock. In
      Georgia it is named among wild carnivores that can carry rabies. Reports
      of attacks on people exist, but how often they happen is not measured on
      this page. Do not handle a wolf. If a wolf bites you, wash the wound and
      seek medical care.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      The wolf is assessed globally as Least Concern and is not on Georgia’s
      2014 Red List. It is a hunting species: the hunting season and quota are
      set by official rules. International trade in wolves is regulated under
      CITES Appendix II.
