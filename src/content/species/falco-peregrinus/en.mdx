commonName: Peregrine falcon
location: Year-round resident and migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A powerful pointed-winged falcon with a dark hood and moustache. Confirmed in
  Georgia as a year-round resident and passage migrant on the 2024 national bird
  checklist.
overview: >-
  The peregrine falcon (Falco peregrinus) is a medium-sized diurnal falcon in the
  family Falconidae. The usual Georgian name is შავარდენი; SABUKO’s checklist
  uses ჩვეულებრივი შავარდენი. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of
  Georgia codes it YR-R; M: a breeder present throughout the year, and also a
  bird of passage in spring and autumn. Seasonality is classed as high-quality
  (code A). Occurrence quality is high (H), with photographic and ringing
  evidence. Adults are about 34–58 cm long at species level, with a wingspan of
  about 74–120 cm; females are clearly larger than males. It is not a threat to
  people on ordinary encounters. IUCN lists the species as Least Concern. The
  2024 checklist assigns the same category nationally. It is not on Georgia’s
  2014 Red List (Decree No. 190).
habitat: >-
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level resident and migrant status but does not
  publish a regional gazetteer. Do not assume it nests evenly across Georgia,
  including treeless plateaus without cliffs. Abuladze (2017) treats the
  Mediterranean subspecies F. p. brookei as the year-round resident in Georgia,
  with local seasonal movements, and nominate F. p. peregrinus as a passage and
  winter visitor. Breeding pairs use little-visited sites and nest on
  inaccessible cliffs. He places the upper limit of breeding distribution at
  about 2,000 m, with most nests usually up to about 1,500 m. Outside the nest
  season the species may hunt over open country, coasts, wetlands, and towns
  where prey birds are available. A separate national elevation band for every
  Georgian region is not published here. The eastern Black Sea raptor bottleneck
  near Batumi is a documented corridor for migrating falcons generally; this
  page does not give a separate published peregrine count series for that site.
diet: >-
  There is no separate weighed Georgian diet study on this page. At species
  level it takes almost exclusively birds in flight — pigeons, doves, shorebirds,
  ducks, and many passerines — killed with the beak after a high-speed stoop
  from above. Small mammals and insects appear rarely in species-level accounts;
  that is not a measured Georgian menu. It is not food for people.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. The silhouette is a classic falcon: long pointed wings
  and a relatively short, tapered tail. Level flight is strong and direct; the
  hunting stoop is a steep dive from height. Species-level literature records
  stoop speeds well over 300 km/h — those figures are from the wider range, not
  a Georgian telemetry sample on this page. The usual call is a harsh, rapid
  “kak-kak-kak” or similar series, more often near the nest. The clip is
  xeno-canto XC976515 (Stuart Wilson, Yaxley, United Kingdom) — an alarm call,
  not a Georgian recording. Do not handle it or approach nests. Nests are scrapes
  or ledges on cliffs; elsewhere in the range buildings and bridges are also
  used — Abuladze’s Georgia summary emphasises inaccessible cliffs. Species-level
  clutches are often about three to four eggs; those figures are from the wider
  range, not a Georgian nest sample on this page. The 2024 checklist treats the
  species as a breeding resident in Georgia and as a passage migrant.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Falco peregrinus as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global and national Red
  List category LC. Government Decree No. 190 of 2014 does not list this
  species; related falcons on that legal list include saker (Falco cherrug, CR),
  red-footed falcon (Falco vespertinus, EN), and lanner (Falco biarmicus, VU).
  Older Georgian secondary sources that place “შავარდენი” in a national Red Book
  as nearly extinct pre-date the 2014 decree and the 2024 checklist coding —
  treat them as historical wording, not current legal status. Abuladze (2017)
  estimated about 60–80 breeding pairs in Georgia, then considered stable with a
  slight increase after a mid-20th-century collapse linked to shooting,
  disturbance, and pesticides. The same abstract estimated autumn migrants of
  the order of about 300–500 birds in then-recent years (versus roughly 100 in
  the 1980s) and a wintering population of about 50–70 individuals in some
  winters. Those figures are period estimates, not a 2020s national census on
  this page. He named ongoing threats as occasional illegal shooting, trapping
  for falconry during passage, taking of nestlings, habitat damage, and
  disturbance at some territories. Do not catch wild birds without a permit.
  International trade is restricted under CITES Appendix I for the species.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people on ordinary encounters. It has strong talons and
  a powerful beak; pairs defend nests. Do not handle wild birds or approach
  nests. It is not venomous.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A powerful falcon with pointed wings, a dark hood, and a bold dark
    moustache stripe on a pale face. Adults are blue-grey above and barred
    below; juveniles are browner and streaked. In Georgia the usual confusion is
    with other large falcons and with smaller pointed-winged falcons on
    migration.
  traits:
    - >-
      Silhouette: long, pointed wings and a relatively short tail — not the
      short broad wings and long square tail of a sparrowhawk. Length about
      34–58 cm and wingspan about 74–120 cm at species level; females are up to
      about 30% larger than males. Adults show a dark slate crown and nape
      (“hood”), a pale cheek with a thick dark moustachial stripe, blue-grey
      upperparts, and finely barred underparts. The cere and legs are yellow;
      the eye is dark. A separate measured series from Georgia is not given on
      this page.
    - >-
      Juveniles are browner above, with underparts streaked rather than barred,
      and a less neat hood. Sex in the field is mainly a size judgement.
      Mediterranean F. p. brookei (the resident form in Abuladze’s Georgia
      account) is often described as slightly smaller and darker than nominate
      northern birds; field separation of subspecies on a brief view in Georgia
      is specialist and not required for species identification.
    - >-
      Eurasian hobby (Falco subbuteo) is a breeding migrant on the 2024
      checklist (BB; M): smaller, with a more slender silhouette and rusty
      “trousers” in adults. Common kestrel (Falco tinnunculus) is smaller, with
      more rounded wings in hover and rufous upperparts in males. Lanner (Falco
      biarmicus; Georgian წითელთავა შავარდენი) and saker (Falco cherrug; გავაზი)
      are large falcons that can be confused with peregrine; both are scarcer
      and coded differently on the 2024 checklist (lanner OB; WV; saker OB; M;
      WV, nationally EN). Saker and lanner usually show a less complete dark
      hood and different underwing patterns; confident separation needs good
      views. Sparrowhawk and buzzard are Accipitridae, not falcons. This atlas
      has pages for Eurasian sparrowhawk and common buzzard; separate pages for
      hobby, kestrel, lanner, and saker are not yet published.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people on an ordinary hike. Leave it alone. A
      pointed-winged falcon with a black moustache diving on birds over cliffs
      or open ground is more likely this species than a buzzard or sparrowhawk.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Tunstall in 1771. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the OSME
    Region List for names: order Falconiformes, family Falconidae, genus Falco.
    IOC World Bird List accepts Falco peregrinus. Abuladze (2017) assigns
    Georgian residents to F. p. brookei and passage/winter birds in part to
    F. p. peregrinus; field identification on this page is to species.
  - >-
    The usual Georgian name is შავარდენი. SABUKO’s bird checklist uses
    ჩვეულებრივი შავარდენი. English names in use are peregrine falcon and
    peregrine. Russian is сапсан. წითელთავა შავარდენი is lanner (Falco
    biarmicus); გავაზი is saker (Falco cherrug) — different species.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R; M, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph) and RD (ringing data).
    That is country-level confirmation plus the named Abuladze summary, not a
    complete regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the peregrine falcon found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident and passage migrant (YR-R; M).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Breeding birds use inaccessible cliffs, mostly below about 1,500–2,000 m
      according to Abuladze (2017). Outside nesting they may hunt over open
      country, coasts, wetlands, and towns with prey birds. This page does not
      map every municipality.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Pointed falcon wings, dark hood, thick dark moustache, blue-grey upperparts
      and barred underparts in adults; juveniles browner and streaked. Hobby and
      kestrel are smaller; saker and lanner need careful separation.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 34–58 cm long at species level, with a wingspan of about 74–120 cm.
      Females are clearly larger than males. A separate measured series from
      Georgia is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly birds caught in flight after a stoop. Diet has not been studied
      separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year as a resident, plus spring and autumn passage, according to the
      2024 checklist. Nominate migrants and winter visitors add to local
      brookei birds in Abuladze’s account.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means resident breeder. Abuladze (2017)
      estimated about 60–80 pairs nesting on cliffs.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless on ordinary encounters; do not approach nests. It is
      globally Least Concern and coded LC nationally on the 2024 checklist. It
      is not on the 2014 Georgian Red List. Do not catch wild birds; CITES
      Appendix I restricts international trade.
