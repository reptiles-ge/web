description: >-
  Caspian turtle (Mauremys caspica) in Georgia — identification, freshwater
  habitats, range, and how to tell it from red-eared sliders and land tortoises.
overview: >-
  The Caspian turtle (Mauremys caspica) is a native freshwater turtle of
  Georgia. It is neither a land tortoise nor an introduced red-eared slider. In
  the modern Georgian checklist, the species is biologically confirmed and linked
  to southeastern Georgia. [When identifying a turtle](/turtles/identifikacia), pay attention to the aquatic
  setting, shell shape, neck stripes, and the absence of a broad red stripe
  behind the eye. The individual pictured at Lisi Lake is a specific record and
  does not show the species’ full range. The map below shows field records in Tbilisi,
  Kvemo Kartli, and Kakheti; highlighting a region does not imply occurrence
  throughout it.
habitat: >-
  The Caspian turtle is tied to water and occurs in ponds, lakes, and slow
  freshwater bodies. The sources used on this profile link it to southeastern
  Georgia. If you see a turtle on dry land with a high, domed shell, it is more
  likely another species.
diet: >-
  The Caspian turtle eats both plant and animal food; its diet can include
  aquatic plants, insects, and young fish. Juveniles generally eat more animal
  food than adults.
behavior: >-
  The Caspian turtle is a freshwater species and may often be seen in water or
  near the bank. In warmer parts of Georgia, it is sometimes active even in
  February; this does not mean it remains active throughout winter across the
  country. Picking it up does not improve identification and causes the animal
  unnecessary stress.
conservation: >-
  The Caspian turtle is native to Georgia. Across its range, changes to water
  bodies and pollution are described as threats; this is not an assessment of the
  population in Georgia. No national threat category is given on this page.
interaction: >-
  The Caspian turtle is not venomous. When encountered, it usually tries to flee
  or hide, but it may bite defensively if picked up or disturbed. A bite can
  become infected. If a turtle bites you, wash the injured area with soap
  and clean water. Seek medical help if there is heavy bleeding, a deep wound, or
  signs of infection. Do not take a wild turtle home or move it to another water
  body. If you meet one in nature, it is best to leave it calmly in place.

faq:
  - question: Does the Caspian turtle occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The Caspian turtle is native to Georgia and biologically confirmed
      there. The modern Georgian checklist links it to southeastern Georgia.
  - question: Where does it occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The modern checklist links the Caspian turtle to southeastern Georgia and
      shows a specific individual from Lisi Lake. Map records cluster in Tbilisi,
      Kvemo Kartli, and Kakheti; they do not establish occurrence across every
      part of those regions.
  - question: How do I identify a Caspian turtle?
    answer: >-
      Look for water-turtle traits: a relatively flat shell, webbed feet,
      lengthwise pale stripes on the neck, and no broad red stripe behind the
      eye.
  - question: Is this a land tortoise?
    answer: >-
      No. The Caspian turtle (Mauremys caspica) is a freshwater turtle.
      Georgia’s land tortoise, Testudo graeca, has a higher, more domed shell.
  - question: How do I tell it from a red-eared slider?
    answer: >-
      The red-eared slider (Trachemys scripta) is introduced in Georgia. Its
      well-known form, T. s. elegans, has a broad red stripe behind the eye. The
      Caspian turtle is native and lacks that red stripe.
  - question: How do I tell it from a European pond turtle?
    answer: >-
      The European pond turtle (Emys orbicularis) occurs in western and eastern
      Georgia and often has a dark shell with yellow speckling. The Caspian
      turtle is linked by the sources on this profile to southeastern Georgia
      and often has lengthwise pale neck stripes.
  - question: Is it venomous?
    answer: >-
      No. The Caspian turtle is not venomous, but it may bite if caught. A bite
      can become infected, so do not pick it up.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Plants and animals; its diet can include aquatic plants, insects, and young fish.
      Juveniles eat more animal food than adults; this is a general trait of the
      species, not the result of a separate study in Georgia.
