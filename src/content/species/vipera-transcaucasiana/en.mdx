commonName: Nose-horned viper
location: Lesser Caucasus
description: >-
  Venomous nose-horned viper of the Lesser Caucasus — high risk in this atlas;
  not a Black Sea coast species.
overview: |-
  The nose-horned viper (Vipera ammodytes) is found in Georgia. It is venomous and is considered a high-risk snake.
  Snakes living in Georgia and the South Caucasus belong to the subspecies V. a. transcaucasiana. Older scientific sources and some databases refer to this subspecies as a separate species, Vipera transcaucasiana.
  It is not found throughout Georgia. It occurs mainly around the Lesser Caucasus, including dry, rocky areas of Meskheti, Trialeti and the upper reaches of the Mtkvari River. Humid Colchic forests and high mountain areas of the Greater Caucasus are not its usual habitat.
  Adult snakes of this species in the Caucasus are usually less than about 75 cm long. A bite requires medical attention.
  IUCN has assessed Vipera transcaucasiana, treated as a separate species, as Near Threatened (NT). It is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: |-
  The nose-horned viper is found mainly on dry, rocky slopes where plants grow sparsely. It may also be found in stony areas, among bushes, at forest edges, near stone walls and in vineyards. In Georgia, it also occurs in relatively dry, rocky and grassy places, especially in the east of the country.
  The map marks only regions where its presence is confirmed. There are records from Adjara, but the region is not marked on the map as an area of confirmed presence. The Tbilisi record is not shown on the map. Confirmed locations include Borjomi, Gomaro in Adigeni Municipality, Tashiskari and Gori. It was also photographed in Dzegvi in August 2026.
  According to older regional sources, in the Lesser Caucasus it ranges from the Tbilisi area toward the mountains of Adjara, in the direction of Shuakhevi. Those accounts also describe its range extending along the upper Mtkvari River toward Akhalkalaki. This broad description does not change which regions are marked as confirmed on the map. The exact boundary of its range in western Javakheti has not been fully confirmed.
  Unlike the Caucasian viper, it is not typical of the Black Sea coast or the damp forests of Colchis. In the mountains, it reaches an altitude of about 1,700 metres. The lowest altitude at which it occurs in Georgia has not been precisely established.
  Its range continues from Georgia into northern Anatolia. Some records from Armenia, Azerbaijan and Iran are disputed and are not included in descriptions of its range in Georgia.
diet: |-
  Adult nose-horned vipers mainly eat lizards and small mammals. They rarely catch small birds. Young vipers hunt small lizards more often.
  They usually lie in wait for prey and use venom to subdue it when they bite. Their diet specifically in Georgia has not been fully studied, so this information is based on what is known about the species in general.
behavior: >-
  The nose-horned viper is active in the warm season. In cool spring and autumn
  weather it often moves by day, while in summer heat activity shifts more to
  evening and early night. It spends winter in rock crevices and under stones.

  It is live-bearing: eggs develop inside the female and the young are born
  alive. Mating takes place in spring, and young are usually born in late summer
  or early autumn. Litter size has not been separately studied for Georgia; data
  from other regions suggest about 5–17 young at a time, with an average around
  nine.

  If you meet one, keep your distance and do not pick it up. It does not seek
  people and bites mainly in defense when it feels threatened.

  In the middle Mtkvari valley there is a narrow contact zone with the Caucasus
  viper, where rare individuals with intermediate traits occur. Older literature
  used the name Vipera pontica for them. Such snakes may have a slightly
  upturned snout, but not a true nose horn.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assesses Vipera transcaucasiana as Near Threatened (NT). That assessment
  follows the taxonomic treatment where the Caucasian form is considered a
  separate species. If it is treated as part of Vipera ammodytes, the whole
  species has a much wider range in Europe.

  Georgia’s 2014 Red List does not include this taxon, even though it appeared
  in older USSR and Georgian Red Books. The Bern Convention lists Vipera
  ammodytes among species needing special protection.

  Main threats are conversion of dry rocky habitats, agricultural expansion and
  killing of snakes by people. Populations in Georgia are described as relatively
  rare and fragmented.

  The 2026 Georgian checklist keeps local animals as the subspecies Vipera
  ammodytes transcaucasiana. Some genetic studies place them closer to southern
  Balkan lineages, but under this approach they are not separated as a full
  species.

identification:
  summary: >-
    This is a stout-bodied viper. In Georgia, the main clues to identifying it are the clearly visible horn on its nose and the fact that it is found in dry, rocky areas of the Lesser Caucasus. Its pupils are vertical, and its scales have a raised line down the middle.
  traits:
    - >-
      Its main distinguishing feature is a clearly visible horn on its nose. It is made up of 9–17 small scales. The scales are arranged in 2–4 rows, usually 3. Other vipers in Georgia do not have a true horn like this on the nose. Its head is broad and clearly set apart from its neck. Adult snakes of the Caucasian form are usually no longer than 75 cm. Females are generally larger than males.
    -  |-
      The pupil looks like a vertical slit, and the scales on the back have a prominent raised ridge down the middle. Its color is often light gray, ash gray, or grayish white. The back usually has narrow crosswise bands or separate dark spots rather than one continuous, distinct zigzag.
      There are usually no prominent dark spots on the horn or the top of the head. A faint V-shaped mark sometimes appears on the neck. The colored part of the eye is often golden or copper-colored.
    -  |-
      The cat snake (Telescopus fallax) has no horn on its nose. Its pupil is also vertical, but its body is slimmer, its eyes are large, and the scales on its back are smooth.
      The Caucasian viper also has no horn on its nose. It is found mainly in damp parts of Colchis and often has a brick-red or orange tint. Dinnik’s viper lives in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus and has no horn either. Darevsky’s viper is smaller and is found in the high mountains of Javakheti–Erusheti.
      The gyurza is much larger: adults are often about 1–1.5 metres long. It has no horn on its nose and has a blunt snout. In Georgia, it is found mainly in the dry lowlands and foothills of the southeast.
    -  |-
      In parts of the Mtkvari River’s middle course where the Caucasian viper also occurs, rare snakes born from crosses with it may have a slightly upturned nose, but they have no true horn. Such a snake is not a separate, third species.
      To identify a viper, do not pick it up or try to count its scales — observe it from a safe distance.
    - >-
      It is venomous and considered a very dangerous snake. Its bite is serious and requires urgent medical attention. The gyurza is a larger snake and can usually inject more venom when it bites, but a bite from the nose-horned viper must also be taken seriously. Do not approach or pick up the snake.
faq:
  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to humans?
    answer: |-
      This snake is venomous and poses a serious danger to people. Its bite can cause serious symptoms both at the bite site and throughout the body. A bite requires urgent medical care. It does not deliberately attack people and usually bites when it feels threatened. Do not approach or handle it.
      The gyurza is larger than the nose-horned viper and can usually inject more venom, but a bite from the nose-horned viper should also be taken seriously.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
      Call 112 immediately. Help the person lie down, reassure them, and try to keep them from moving much. Do not cut the bite, suck out the venom, or tie a band tightly around a limb. Treatment must take place in a hospital.
  - question: Is the nose-horned viper found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 checklist of Georgian reptiles treats its presence in the
      country as biologically confirmed. Animals found in Georgia belong to the
      subspecies Vipera ammodytes transcaucasiana, whose type locality is
      Borjomi. The species does not occur throughout Georgia and its range is
      tied to particular regions and habitats.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, the nose-horned viper is mostly found in dry, rocky places of
      the Lesser Caucasus: Meskheti, Trialeti and the upper Mtkvari valleys.
      Older sources also mention highland Adjara towards Shuakhevi.

      Only regions marked confirmed in the map's records table count as
      distribution. Adjara is recorded-only and Tbilisi has no mapped record.
      The humid Colchic coastal belt is not typical range for it.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Among Georgian vipers, only the nose-horned viper has a true horn on the
      snout. The pupil is vertical and the dorsal scales are keeled. Colour is
      often grey, with narrow cross-bars or dark blotches on the back.

      The European cat snake (Telescopus fallax) has no horn on the snout; it
      has a vertical pupil, but the body is slimmer, the eyes are large, and the
      dorsal scales are smooth.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adult snakes of the Caucasian form are usually no longer than about 75 cm. Females are generally larger than males. The nose-horned viper is much shorter and smaller than the gyurza.
  - question: Is this the same as Vipera transcaucasiana?
    answer: |-
      Yes, these are the same snakes living in the Caucasus. The difference is whether they are treated as a separate species or a subspecies. The 2026 Georgian list uses the name Vipera ammodytes and treats the Caucasian group as its subspecies, V. a. transcaucasiana.
      IUCN, however, treats Vipera transcaucasiana as a separate species and still assesses it as Near Threatened (NT). The Vipera ammodytes found in the Balkans is not counted as a separate species for Georgia.
  - question: Why is it not on Georgia’s Red List if IUCN says Near Threatened?
    answer: |-
      The nose-horned viper is not included in Georgia’s 2014 state Red List. Dinnik’s viper and the Caucasian viper are listed there separately. The IUCN’s Near Threatened (NT) classification applies to the Caucasian viper, Vipera transcaucasiana, which is treated as a separate species.
      The nose-horned viper was included in older Red Books, but those entries do not mean it is included in Georgia’s current 2014 state Red List.
