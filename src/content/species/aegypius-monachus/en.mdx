commonName: Cinereous vulture
location: Breeds in Georgia; recorded year-round and on passage
description: >-
  The cinereous vulture is a very large, almost uniformly dark scavenger. It
  breeds in south-east Georgia's dry zone and is also recorded on passage.
overview: >-
  The cinereous vulture (Aegypius monachus) is a large scavenging bird. It
  breeds in Georgia, is recorded throughout the year, and also occurs on
  passage. Breeding is documented in the dry south-east; seeing a bird elsewhere
  does not establish that it nests there. Adults are almost uniformly dark, with
  a paler head, broad wings and a short wedge-shaped tail. It poses no threat
  to people in an ordinary encounter, but do not touch the bird or approach a
  nest.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia it breeds in the dry south-eastern landscape, a mosaic of steppe
  and juniper woodland. The breeding area studied in 1994–2002 lay at
  100–900 m; all 31 nests found were in mature juniper trees. The paper names Vashlovani Nature
  Reserve and the Korugi, Iori and Chachuna sanctuaries. These are findings from
  a historical survey, not a complete picture of today's distribution or
  breeding across Georgia. Do not approach nests.
diet: >-
  It feeds on carrion, particularly carcasses of medium-sized and large mammals,
  including livestock. Sheep flocks used the studied south-east Georgian area
  in winter; dead livestock provides food for the vultures. It does not usually
  hunt live large animals.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. When soaring on rising warm air, it often holds its broad
  wings almost flat; its short wedge-shaped tail is also visible in flight. It
  usually lays one egg, and raising the chick takes a long time. In July 2024
  a chick was tagged while still in a nest in Georgia. The species is also
  recorded on passage; many Caucasian birds appear to leave their breeding
  grounds in winter.
conservation: >-
  Globally, the cinereous vulture is Near Threatened (IUCN: NT). Georgia's 2014
  government Red List gives it Endangered status (EN, D1), while the 2024 bird
  checklist gives it a national assessment of NT. The scientific assessment
  does not automatically change the legal list. The 2018 European action plan
  gives an estimate of 50 breeding pairs in Georgia for 2016; this is not a
  current census. A study in south-east Georgia linked the restricted breeding
  range to human disturbance, including seasonal grazing. The species also
  faces poisoning, habitat damage and collisions with energy
  infrastructure.
interaction: >-
  It poses no threat to people in an ordinary encounter. Do not touch it: it
  has a large bill and talons. Do not approach nests or capture the bird.
  Poisoned baits pose a direct threat to scavengers.
danger: Harmless

    broad wings and a massive bill. Adults have a paler head; at a distance it

  traits:
    - >-
      At species level length is often about 100–110 cm, wingspan about
      250–295 cm, mass often about 7–12 kg; females are generally larger than
      males. Adult
      plumage is uniformly dark brown or black; the head is paler bluish-grey
      with sparse down and a dark cowl-like ruff. Juveniles are blacker, including
      the head. The legs are partly bare; the cere and bill base are pale, the
      bill massive. In flight the silhouette is rectangular broad wings and a
      short wedge-shaped tail.
    - >-
      Eurasian griffon (Gyps fulvus) is also YR-R; M on the 2024 checklist; this
      atlas has a page for that species. Adults have a pale sandy body, contrasting
      dark flight feathers, a longer neck and a pale ruff; juveniles are darker
      with a brown ruff. It often soars with wings in a V. Cinereous vulture is
      uniformly dark and holds the wings
      flatter. Bearded vulture (Gypaetus barbatus) is YR-R: narrower wings, a
      long diamond-shaped tail, rusty body and a black “beard” in adults.
      Egyptian vulture (Neophron percnopterus) is BB; M and much smaller; adults
      are white with black flight feathers and a yellow face. Golden eagle
      (Aquila chrysaetos) has a page in this atlas: longer tail, golden nape,
      fully feathered legs; immatures may show white at the tail base and on the wings.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1766 as Vultur monachus; the accepted name is
    Aegypius monachus. The genus Aegypius is monotypic. The 2024 Georgian
    checklist follows the OSME Region List: order Accipitriformes, family
    Accipitridae. IOC and ITIS accept the species.
  - >-
    The official Georgian and Red List name is სვავი. English names are
    cinereous vulture (2024 checklist / IOC), also Eurasian black vulture and
    monk vulture. The American black vulture (Coragyps atratus) is a different
    family and does not occur in Georgia.
faq:
  - question: Is the cinereous vulture found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia accepts it as a
      year-round resident and a migrant (YR-R; M), with photographs.
  - question: Where can it be seen in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It breeds in south-east Georgia's dry landscape, where steppe and juniper
      woodland form a mosaic.
      Seeing a bird elsewhere does not show that it nests there. Do not
      approach nests.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A very large, uniformly dark bird with a short wedge-shaped tail, a massive
      bill and, in adults, a paler head. Griffon has a pale body and often soars
      with a V. Golden eagle has a longer tail and a golden nape.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      At species level length is often about 100–110 cm, wingspan about
      250–295 cm, mass often about 7–12 kg.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Carrion, especially large-mammal carcasses. In south-east Georgia food is
      linked to winter sheep flocks. It is not a typical hunter of live large
      prey.
  - question: When is it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It is recorded in Georgia throughout the year and on passage. This does
      not mean the same birds stay at their nesting sites all year: many
      Caucasian vultures appear to leave those sites in winter.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R means a breeding resident. Nests are documented in mature juniper
      in the south-east. A nestling was tagged in the nest in July 2024.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      Harmless on ordinary encounters; do not approach nests. Globally NT. The
      2014 Red List is EN D1; the 2024 checklist codes it nationally as NT. Do
      not catch wild birds.
