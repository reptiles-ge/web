overview: >-
  The marsh frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) is a widespread water frog in Georgia and is not venomous to people. It lives in ponds, lakes, canals, puddles, slow streams and other water bodies, including in settled areas. Across the species as a whole, adults usually measure about 6–12 cm, and females are often larger than males. It belongs to the true frog family (Ranidae) and is considered of least concern worldwide. Older sources may also call it Rana ridibunda, while the current checklist of Georgia's species uses Pelophylax ridibundus.
habitat: |-
  The marsh frog is closely tied to water. It lives in canals, streams, puddles, ponds and lakes, and is especially common on open, well-warmed banks where plants grow. It also occurs in village and city water bodies.
  In Georgia it lives both in the lowlands and in mountain areas. In the high subalpine and alpine zones of the Greater Caucasus, however, it is rare. According to some data, across the species' whole range it also occurs above 2,500 m above sea level. This does not mean it is common everywhere at that height.
diet: >-
  The main food of adult marsh frogs is invertebrates that live in the water and along the shore, especially insects. A large frog may also eat another amphibian, a small fish or another small vertebrate. Tadpoles, in turn, live mainly on algae, decaying debris and tiny invertebrates. This describes the species as a whole; its diet has not been fully studied separately in Georgia.
behavior: |-
  It is most active in the warm season. By day it often sits at the water's edge or right on the water surface. If it senses danger, it quickly jumps into the water.
  The breeding season depends on place and elevation. In the Tbilisi lowlands, males may already be calling in late March, and spawn has been seen there from spring into autumn. In Borjomi Gorge and the southern uplands, the season starts later and is shorter. These are data from individual places, not one calendar for all of Georgia.
  Males have two vocal sacs at the corners of the mouth, and their croaking carries a long way. If you come across a frog, leave it where it is. Do not move it from one pond or stream to another.
conservation: >-
  Globally, the marsh frog is considered a species of least concern. It is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red List. It also lives in built-up areas and copes with some environmental change. Locally, though, it can suffer when a water body dries up, banks are destroyed or spawning sites disappear. Scientists hold different views on Pelophylax bedriagae, while the name Pelophylax ridibundus is used for the frogs in Georgia.
interaction: |-
  The marsh frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) is not venomous and usually poses no danger to people. If you come close, it often jumps into the water and avoids contact. There is no need to catch a wild frog or move it elsewhere.
  If you do touch one, wash your hands well with soap and water. Until then, don't touch your eyes or mouth. Don't move a frog from one body of water to another: this may carry diseases from the amphibians of one water body to those of another.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The Marsh Frog is a medium to large water frog with smooth skin and long hind legs. Its pupil is horizontal, and it has no sticky pads on its toe tips. In Georgia it is most often confused with the Caucasian Brown Frog, so don't rely on a single feature: check several together.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is stocky, the hind legs are long, and the toes are webbed. Adult marsh frogs are usually about 6–12 cm from the tip of the snout to the end of the body, not counting the legs. Females are often larger than males. The toe tips have no sticky discs, and this feature will help you tell it apart from treefrogs.
    - >-
      The back may be green, olive, grey or brownish, often with dark spots. Many frogs also have a pale stripe down the middle of the back. The belly is pale and may have dark spots too. Colour varies quite a lot, so do not rely on green colour alone when identifying it.
    - >-
      The pupil is horizontal, and the eardrum is clearly visible. Two distinct skin folds run along the sides of the back. Males have a pair of grey vocal sacs at the corners of the mouth. These features will help you identify it, but it is best to also consider body shape, size and the place where you saw the frog.
    - >-
      The Caucasian Brown Frog (Rana macrocnemis) lives more in forests and on land, and it often has a dark patch behind the eye. The Marsh Frog is far more tied to water and, when disturbed, often jumps straight into it. Telling them apart by leg proportions alone is not reliable. So don't rely on a single feature: check several together.
    - >-
      The eastern treefrog and the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog are smaller than the Marsh Frog and have sticky discs on their toe tips that help them climb plants. Toads have wartier skin and large glands behind the eyes. The Caucasian parsley frog and the eastern spadefoot have vertical pupils, while the Marsh Frog's pupils are horizontal.

      Yes. The marsh frog is a confirmed and widespread species in Georgia. It has been recorded in different parts of the country.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The marsh frog lives by water: at ponds, lakes, canals, puddles and slow streams. It occurs in lowlands and in foothills alike. High in the mountains, in the subalpine and alpine zones, it is rare. You may also see it in village and city water bodies.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. The marsh frog has no venom and poses no danger to people. There is no need to catch it. If you do touch one, wash your hands with soap and water.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      You can recognise the Marsh Frog by its smooth skin, horizontal pupil, long hind legs and webbed toes. Its toe tips have no sticky discs. It is often green or olive, with dark spots on the back. Its colour varies, though, so do not rely on colour alone: check several features together.
  - question: How does it differ from the long-legged wood frog?
    answer: >-
      Look at where you found the frog, what colour it is and whether it has a dark patch behind the eye. The marsh frog sticks more closely to water and is often green or olive. The Caucasian brown frog (Rana macrocnemis) is more often found in forest and on land, and it often has a dark patch behind the eye. These signs do not always hold, so do not rely on just one: check several together.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adult marsh frogs usually measure about 6–12 cm in body length. This figure applies to the species as a whole, not only to frogs in Georgia. Females are generally larger than males.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is most active in the warm season and is found mainly near water. Breeding time depends on place and elevation. In the lowlands the season starts earlier, while at higher sites it starts later and is shorter.
  - question: Is bedriagae a separate species in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      No. Georgia's current species checklist does not accept Pelophylax bedriagae as a separate species and uses the name Pelophylax ridibundus. So when you identify a frog in the wild, use the name Pelophylax ridibundus.
