overview: >-
  The mottled scorpion (Mesobuthus eupeus) is a buthid scorpion. This profile
  uses the modern narrow treatment: in the 2022 revision by Kovařík et al.,
  Mesobuthus eupeus sensu stricto is tied to the Caucasus region and no
  subspecies are recognized.

  The species is confirmed for Georgia by material linked to Tbilisi. MCZbase
  lists a specimen as Georgia, Caucasus, Tiflis (= Tbilisi), and older checklist
  literature also names Tbilisi. That evidence does not make every Georgian
  region confirmed on this page.

  The scorpion uses venom to subdue prey. In this atlas the risk to people is
  marked Moderate: a sting can be painful and may sometimes cause systemic
  symptoms, so handling is not safe practice.
habitat: >-
  This page does not have a separate Georgian habitat series. In the Caucasus
  and neighbouring parts of its range, Mesobuthus eupeus is associated mostly
  with warm, dry, rocky or open grassy places, hiding by day in shelter.

  The Tbilisi-linked evidence confirms the species for the country, but this
  profile does not draw a full regional map until sources name administrative
  regions or precise localities.
diet: >-
  The diet of the mottled scorpion in Georgia has not been fully studied
  separately. As a scorpion, it hunts small arthropods; likely prey includes
  insects and other small invertebrates.

  It uses pincers and the venomous sting to subdue prey.
behavior: >-
  Scorpions are mostly secretive animals: by day they hide under stones, in
  cracks, or in other shelters, and activity often increases in the evening and
  at night.

  Do not try to catch or move one by hand. If one must be moved from a house or
  yard, use a container and a stiff card, or ask a specialist.

interaction: >-
  The mottled scorpion is venomous, but that does not mean it seeks out people
  or attacks them. Stings are usually accidental, when a scorpion is touched,
  pressed, or disturbed in a hiding place.

  Do not handle it. If a sting happens and pain is strong, the person is a child,
  allergic or general symptoms appear, or the situation is unclear, call 112 and
  seek medical care.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small to medium yellowish-brown scorpion with pincers and a segmented tail
    ending in a sting. Species-level confirmation from photos needs specialist
    review.
  traits:
    - >-
      A scorpion has two pincers and an upcurved segmented tail. The tail ends in
      a sting; that is the key difference from pseudoscorpions, which have
      pincers but no tail or sting.
    - >-
      In the Mesobuthus eupeus group the body is often yellow to yellowish brown,
      with darker marks or stripes on the back. Colour alone is not reliable
      identification.
    - >-
      Many Buthidae scorpions have relatively slender pincers and a clearly
      developed tail. Without close photographs, reducing a scorpion to exact
      species is risky.
    - >-
      Older literature may use Buthus eupeus or a broad Mesobuthus eupeus
      concept. After the 2022 revision, those old usages should not be treated
      automatically as the modern narrow species.
facts:
  - >-
    The accepted name is Mesobuthus eupeus (C. L. Koch, 1839). The protonym is
    Androctonus eupeus C. L. Koch, 1839; older sources may also use Buthus
    eupeus.
  - >-
    Kovařík et al. 2022 restrict Mesobuthus eupeus sensu stricto to the Caucasus
    region and recognize no subspecies.
  - >-
    Georgian evidence is linked to Tbilisi/Tiflis. This page does not add other
    regions from a general country-level “Georgia” record.
  - >-
    The Georgian name used here is ჭრელი მორიელი. English sources often use
    mottled scorpion; older broad usage also used lesser Asian scorpion.
