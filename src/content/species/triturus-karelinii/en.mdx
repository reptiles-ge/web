description: >-
  The Eastern Crested Newt is Georgia's largest newt, with coarse skin and a spotted belly. It is not venomous and lives in western Georgia and the forest belt of the east.
overview: >-
  The eastern crested newt (Triturus karelinii) is Georgia's largest newt, and its presence in Georgia is confirmed. Adults usually reach about 15–18 cm. It has rough skin and dark spots on its belly. These two features tell it apart from smooth-skinned newts and newts with a stripe along the side. It is not dangerous to people: its bite is not venomous. Globally it is rated “Least Concern”, and it is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List. It belongs to the salamander family, and the 2026 Georgian species list places it, together with T. cristatus, in a group of closely related species. In English it is called the eastern crested newt or southern crested newt; in Georgian it is also simply called the crested newt.
habitat: |-
  In Georgia this is a newt of the forest belt, living in ponds, lakes and slow water. When breeding, it is found in ponds and small lakes with no fish or only a few, in pools of slow streams and sometimes in ditches. On land it hides under stones, logs and fallen leaves. It copes with dry conditions better than other Caucasian newts and in 1996 data it is described as a newt of relatively dry places. In the humid forests of the west, however, it is rare, and usually only a few live in one place. It is not known from the far south-west of Georgia, and a 1956 report from Batumi is considered an error.
  The 2026 checklist places it throughout western Georgia and in the forest belt of the east, but rates the reliability of these data as moderate. DNA-checked specimens come from Imereti, from Gogni and Tskaltubo. It is also known from Tkibuli.
  By 1996 it was known from 33 sites in Georgia. They include Poti, Senaki, Sukhumi, Gagra, Pskhu, Ritsa, Kutaisi, Ajameti and Kobuleti. The list has Baniskhevi, Borjomi, Bakuriani, Didi Mitarbi, Surami, the Tana valley, the Liakhvi reserve, Ertatsminda and Lake Pitsesi (Kaspi). It also includes Betania, Lake Akhaldaba near Tbilisi, Tsodoreti, Saguramo, Satovle, Chili-Tba, Chiantba (Telavi), Batsara and Lagodekhi. Lake Duruji and the Bursa valley (Kvareli), the Sakochavi lakes, the village of Tba, Kodiani and Adjara are named too, though the Adjara report is doubtful. From these records, the atlas map marks Abkhazia, Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Shida Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Kakheti and Tbilisi. These reports do not mean it occurs equally in all these regions today.
  In the South Caucasus it reaches about 2,100 m above sea level. As the species is understood today, it lives in Crimea, the Caucasus and on the southern Caspian coast. It was first described from Iran, at the Gulf of Gorgan, not from Georgia.
diet: >-
  The Eastern Crested Newt eats small invertebrates both in water and on land. In water, adults catch snails, crustaceans and insect larvae, and sometimes small land invertebrates that have fallen in. On land they feed on worms, slugs, spiders and insects. Larvae start with tiny crustaceans and later move on to larger prey. This describes the species as a whole; its diet in Georgia has not been studied separately.
behavior: |-
  It lives in water while breeding and is often on land the rest of the time. It is active mainly at night. Its winter rest usually lasts from September–October to March. It spends the winter on land, though some individuals stay in deep, still water. In the lowlands it mates in March–May, in the mountains in May–July. In a warm winter, mating may begin as early as February. The female lays about 100–250 eggs and wraps each one separately in a water plant. These dates and egg numbers describe the species as a whole, not Georgia specifically. In Georgia, most larvae hatch from late May through June. In August–October the larvae take on the adult form. Some larvae spend the winter in the water.
  If you find one, just watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up or move it from one body of water to another: that is how fungal disease spreads easily. Its skin releases a defensive substance, and the orange, spotted belly is a warning sign. It poses no medical risk to people, but wash your hands after touching it.
conservation: >-
  Worldwide, the Eastern Crested Newt is not under threat: a 2023 assessment rates it as "Least Concern". That assessment covers T. karelinii as understood today, found in Crimea, the Caucasus and the Caspian region, and not the Balkan T. ivanbureschi. The 2026 checklist confirms the newt's presence in Georgia, but it is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List. The amphibians on that list are the Caucasian Salamander and the Eastern Spadefoot. On the Black Sea coast, however, its numbers are falling, and at some sites in the western Caucasus its situation is serious. This is a regional picture, not Georgia's official status. The main threats are ponds being destroyed or filled in, fish being released into them, forest cutting and pollution. If you find one, do not pick it up.
interaction: |-
  The Eastern Crested Newt (Triturus karelinii) is not venomous and poses no direct danger to people. If you approach, it usually tries to hide or escape. Still, do not pick it up: amphibian skin is sensitive, and too much handling stresses the animal.
  In Georgia it lives in the west and in the forest belt of the east. Its home is small pools, ponds and other small bodies of water, especially in wooded areas. If you see one by the water, there is no need to catch it or take it anywhere else. Above all, do not move it to another pond or pool: moving amphibians like this can spread disease and disturb the natural state of local amphibians. Do not take it home or keep it as a pet either.
  If you touch it, wash your hands well with soap and water, and do not touch your eyes or mouth before you do. Accidental contact is usually no medical risk to people. Simply watch it and leave it where it is: that is the best way to protect a wild newt.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Georgia's largest newt, with coarse skin. Its belly is yellow or orange with large dark spots. In the breeding season, males grow a high, jagged crest. Don't mistake it for a smooth-skinned or striped newt.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is stout and stocky, not slender, and the skin is coarse and granular. In water the tail is flattened from side to side. Adults usually reach about 15–18 cm including the tail, and females are often slightly larger than males.
    - >-
      The belly and throat are yellow or orange with black spots. The spots may merge, making part of the belly look black. The back is brownish or dull grey with darker patches. Females often have a narrow yellowish line along the middle of the back. There is no silvery stripe along the side.
    - >-
      In the breeding season the male grows a high, jagged crest that runs from the back onto the tail. At the base of the tail the crest often has only a small notch or no break at all. This sets it apart from the northern crested newt (T. cristatus), which does not occur in Georgia. Females lack this crest.
    - >-
      The Caucasian Smooth Newt (Lissotriton lantzi) is smaller, usually about 7–11 cm. Its skin is smooth or finely granular, and the male's crest is low. The Northern Banded Newt (Ommatotriton ophryticus) has a pale stripe along its flank and an unspotted belly. The Caucasian Salamander (Mertensiella caucasica) has yellow spots and a round tail. The salamander lives by streams, not in ponds, so note where you saw the animal.
    - >-
      The Balkan and western Anatolian crested newts, T. ivanbureschi and T. anatolicus, look almost identical to this species in the wild. Older books called the Eastern Crested Newt Triturus cristatus karelinii. Today T. karelinii is a separate species found in Crimea, the Caucasus and the southern Caspian region. Neither lookalike occurs in Georgia, so here geography alone is enough to tell them apart.
