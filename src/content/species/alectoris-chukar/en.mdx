overview: >-
  The Chukar Partridge (Alectoris chukar) is a ground-dwelling bird in the pheasant family, Phasianidae. Georgia's 2024 annotated bird checklist confirms it as a breeding, year-round resident, supported by photographs, sound recordings and a hunter-collected bird. The National Wildlife Agency names the Iori Plateau, the upper Mtkvari valley and a small population on the Kvernaki Ridge. Adults are about 34–38 cm long. A red bill, pale throat enclosed by a black band and strongly barred flanks are the most useful marks together. It poses no direct danger to people. Its global IUCN category is Least Concern; Georgian sources disagree about the national category, as explained below.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, look for Chukar on dry, rocky slopes with scattered shrubs. The National Wildlife Agency reports it from the Iori Plateau, the upper Mtkvari valley and the Kvernaki Ridge, and describes it in the Greater and Lesser Caucasus. The Georgian Encyclopaedia also names the southern slopes of the Greater Caucasus and the Tsiv-Gombori, Guria-Adjara, Trialeti and Erusheti ranges. These are published range descriptions, not a current count for each municipality. Sources describe slopes from the foothills to high mountains, but this page has no verified Georgia-specific elevational limit. Birds usually move on the ground among rocks and shrubs. Give any nest space.
diet: >-
  The Georgian Encyclopaedia lists seeds, shoots, buds and insects as food. This page does not have a Georgian study quantifying the proportion of each food type.
behavior: >-
  Chukars usually walk or run over rocky slopes rather than fly; when startled, they take a short, powerful flight. Their repeated, loud chuck-like calls can help with identification. The 2024 checklist confirms breeding and year-round presence in Georgia, without giving a local activity calendar. According to the National Wildlife Agency, laying begins in April on the Iori Plateau and may start later at higher sites. Nests are on the ground. The agency's average of 7–12 eggs and incubation of 22–25 days are general species figures, not measurements from all Georgian nests.
conservation: >-
  The 2016 IUCN global assessment classifies Chukar as Least Concern (LC). Georgia's 2024 bird checklist and the National Wildlife Agency also give LC as the national category; the agency says the species is not on the Georgian Red List. Yet a table in the 2023 Chachuna Managed Reserve plan gives the national category as Endangered (EN). The reason for this conflict has not been established here, so neither category should be treated as a settled legal designation. The agency lists poaching and habitat degradation and fragmentation as local threats. These sources do not establish a national population trend.
interaction: >-
  Chukars pose no direct danger to people. Watch from a distance and do not catch wild birds or chicks. Historical references to hunting are not a current permit: Chukar is absent from the Environmental Supervision Department's published list of birds permitted for hunting. Check current rules with the responsible authority before any take.

  summary: >-
    A stocky, medium-sized ground bird with a red bill and legs. A black line runs through the eye and wraps around the pale throat. Bold black bars mark its pale flanks. Look for this combination on rocky slopes.
  traits:
    - >-
      Adults measure about 34–38 cm; this is a general species range, not a sample measured in Georgia. The back is grey-brown, the throat pale and the flanks strongly barred. Males average slightly larger, but size alone does not reliably determine sex. Young birds may lack the full contrast of adults.
    - >-
      [Common Quail](coturnix-coturnix) (Coturnix coturnix) is much smaller, streaked above and usually hides in grass; it lacks the Chukar's red bill and black-bordered throat. [Common Pheasant](phasianus-colchicus) (Phasianus colchicus) is larger and has a long tail as an adult. Grey Partridge (Perdix perdix), also present in Georgia, lacks the Chukar's black throat border and bold vertical flank bars; it does not yet have its own atlas page.
