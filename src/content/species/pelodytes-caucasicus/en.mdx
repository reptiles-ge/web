commonName: "Caucasian Parsley Frog"
location: "humid Caucasian forest"
description: >-
  The Caucasian Parsley Frog lives only in the forests of the western Caucasus and is confirmed in Georgia. It is a small frog with a vertical pupil and a cross on its back.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian Parsley Frog (Pelodytes caucasicus) is a small tailless amphibian that lives nowhere in the world except the western Caucasus. That region extends beyond Georgia's borders, and the 2026 checklist confirms the species in Georgia. It was described in 1896 from a frog found on Mount Lomisi near Bakuriani, at about 2,130 m. It has also been seen at Gombori and Kistauri. It is not venomous to people. A global assessment rates it as “near threatened”, and the Georgian Red List gives it the same status. Its family's scientific name is Pelodytidae. In Georgian it is called jvriana or jvarula.
habitat: |-
  The Caucasian Parsley Frog lives in damp, shady mountain forests beside cold, clear streams, ponds or springs. The forest is broad-leaved or mixed. More rarely, it also occurs in coniferous forest and in the high subalpine belt. It is sometimes found 200–300 m away from water. It has also been seen in caves (Marshania II, Kotsha), but it does not live there permanently.

  The 2026 checklist confirms it in Georgia and names Gombori and Kistauri. The species was described from a frog found on Mount Lomis near Bakuriani, and it was photographed in Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park on 21 May 2015. By 2018, 56 places were known in Georgia. They stretch from the Colchis coast (Poti, Kulevi, Batumi) through the forests of Adjara, Guria, Imereti and Racha to the Borjomi Gorge. On the Greater Caucasus they follow the southern slope from Barisakho to Lagodekhi. It ranges from sea level up to about 2,300 m (Tskhra-Tskharo Pass).

  It is not evenly spread across the country. On the Lesser Caucasus its eastern limit is not settled. It is often placed at the Borjomi Gorge, but by 2018 it had also been recorded at Tabatskuri. Do not expect it on the dry eastern plains.
diet: >-
  It eats insects, their larvae and other small invertebrates. Adult frogs hunt invertebrates that live on land, while tadpoles feed in the water. Do not think of it as a way to control garden pests.
behavior: |-
  It is mainly active at night and chooses cool, shaded places. By day it hides among roots and stones, in fallen leaves or in holes in the bank. In the breeding season it returns to the water at night. It spends the winter from September–November to March. These are general dates for the species, not an exact calendar for every place in Georgia.

  In the Borjomi Canyon it breeds from the second half of May to the end of September, often peaking in June–July. The frogs do not all breed together in a short burst: in one body of water the season may last a long time. Males arrive before females and call in the evening or in the first half of the night. Their call is weaker than that of tree frogs or Marsh Frogs. Each female spawns once a season, laying about 80–750 eggs in jelly sacs.

  Some tadpoles turn into frogs within 2–3 months. Many, however, spend the winter in the water and leave it the following April–July; some need a second winter. Caucasian Parsley Frogs usually start breeding at 2–3 years old.
conservation: >-
  Under the 2009 global assessment, the Caucasian Parsley Frog is Near Threatened. It has the same status on Georgia's Red List. It does not live only in Georgia. The main known threats are forest cutting and the destruction and pollution of the waters where it spawns. Clearing away fallen leaves and dead trees is a threat too, and so are deaths on roads. In other parts of the Caucasus the introduced raccoon is also named as a threat, but its impact has not been measured everywhere in Georgia. It is not on the CITES lists, which cover international trade in wild species. Do not catch wild amphibians.
interaction: |-
  The Caucasian Parsley Frog (Pelodytes caucasicus) is not venomous and poses no danger to people. It is a small, shy amphibian that, as a rule, tries to hide when a person appears. In Georgia it lives mainly in the moist forests of the west and centre of the country, near water. Do not pick it up: amphibian skin is sensitive.

  If you do touch one, wash your hands well with soap and water. Do not touch your eyes or mouth with unwashed hands. Do not take the frog home or move it somewhere else: its presence in Georgia is confirmed, and its protection matters. If you come across one in the wild, it is best to simply watch it and leave it where it is.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small, slender frog with a vertical pupil, an oblique cross on its back and weakly webbed toes. It is the only member of the genus Pelodytes in Georgia. It is most often confused with the Eastern Spadefoot and with brown frogs or Marsh Frogs, so look at the cross on the back and at the pupil.
  traits:
    - >-
      A small, slender frog that does not look like a toad. Body length is about 4–5.5 cm, up to 55 mm in Georgia. Adults measured in Turkey in 2009 were about 40–53 mm. Outside the breeding season, the back shows an oblique cross with two pale spots at its rear corners. This cross gives the species its Georgian name. From above it is olive or grey-brown with dark spots, sometimes with red specks too. The skin on the back carries evenly spaced small bumps, and the belly is grey or whitish. The pupil is vertical, and the eardrum is visible. Webbing between the hind toes is weak, and the inner bump on the foot is small and rounded, not a large "spade".
    - >-
      In the breeding season the male darkens and loses the cross pattern on its back. At this time small black bumps appear on its chest, upper arms and front toes. Its vocal sac sits inside the throat. The female is usually paler, reddish-brown above, and the rear of her belly may be reddish. A Caucasian Parsley Frog photographed in Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park in 2015 had a creamy-white belly with fine dark speckles and a pinkish-orange tinge at the groin. Tadpoles often overwinter and turn into frogs only the following year. You do not need to pick the frog up to identify it, so please don't.
    - >-
      The Eastern Spadefoot (Pelobates syriacus) is bulkier and stockier, with a large “spade” on the inner side of the hind foot. In Georgia it is known from the east, not from Colchic forests. The Caucasian Brown Frog (Rana macrocnemis) and the Marsh Frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) have horizontal pupils and skin folds along the sides of the back. The Marsh Frog stays closer to the water's edge. The Caucasian Toad (Bufo verrucosissimus) and the Green Toad (Bufotes viridis) are large, warty toads, and they too have horizontal pupils. The Eastern Treefrog (Hyla orientalis) is green, has discs on its toes and climbs trees. The western European Pelodytes punctatus does not occur in Georgia, so you need not worry about it.
    - >-
      If you spot a small frog in forest shade, by a cold stream or a forest-road puddle, look at its pupil and back. A vertical pupil and a cross on the back point to the Caucasian Parsley Frog rather than a Marsh Frog. It is not venomous, but do not pick it up.
    - >-
      It is the only Pelodytes species found in Georgia. Georgia's 2026 checklist lists it as a confirmed species. The same checklist treats it as endemic to the western Caucasus: it lives only in this region, which extends beyond Georgia's borders. In that checklist, the DNA sample comes from Gombori and the illustrated frog from Kistauri. The species was described from a frog found on Mount Lomis near Bakuriani.
