description: >-
  A confirmed small lacertid in Georgia with fused eyelids; harmless, local, and

overview: >-
  The snake-eyed lizard (Ophisops elegans) is a confirmed, sporadically recorded
  small lacertid in Georgia. Its simplest field mark is the fused transparent
  eyelid: the eye looks snake-like, but the animal is a fully limbed lizard.
  Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat it as biologically confirmed and link Georgian
  barcoded individuals to Taribana, Dedoplistskaro. The Georgian Biodiversity
  Database also lists records near Kumisi and Rustavi in Kvemo Kartli. These data
  point to dry open landscapes of eastern Georgia, but they do not prove a
  continuous range across the whole east. It is harmless to people and should be
  left undisturbed.
habitat: >-
  Confirmed Georgian material is tied mainly to dry eastern landscapes. The 2026
  checklist gives barcoded individuals from Taribana in Dedoplistskaro, Kakheti.
  The Georgian Biodiversity Database lists the Kumisi and Rustavi area in Kvemo
  Kartli and describes semi-desert fragments with sparse grass cover. Realistic
  encounter sites are dry open ground, stony or grassy patches, and steppe-shrub
  edges; forest and wet sites are not treated here as confirmed Georgian habitat.
diet: >-
  No detailed diet study from Georgia was found. A Bursa population study found
  insects to be most of the recorded food, especially larvae. For Georgia, the
  safest wording is small invertebrates until local stomach-content data are
  published.
behavior: >-
  This is a day-active, fast ground lizard. When disturbed, it runs into low
  vegetation, stones, or uneven ground. No detailed seasonal calendar has been
  published for Georgia; field encounters are most likely in the warm season,
  when small lizards are active. It is oviparous, but clutch size and breeding
  timing in Georgia still need verification.
conservation: >-
  Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat the species as biologically confirmed for
  Georgia and list the subspecies as O. e. elegans. The Georgian Biodiversity
  Database shows a national status of Vulnerable (VU) and a global IUCN status
  of Least Concern (LC), with a fragmented Georgian range. Loss or alteration of
  dry semi-desert and steppe fragments is the practical conservation issue. Use
  the name Ophisops elegans for Georgia now, but with taxonomic caution: genetic
  divergence within O. elegans sensu lato is high and the wider complex may be
  revised.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small, quick lacertid with a transparent fused eyelid. The snake-like eye
    is the key field mark, but the body, limbs, and scales are those of a true
    lizard.
  traits:
    - >-
      The eyelids are fused into a transparent scale over the eye, so the animal
      does not blink like most familiar lizards. This is the feature behind the
      name “snake-eyed”.
    - >-
      The body is slender and built for quick movement on the ground. The tail is
      long, often about twice the body length; a broken or regenerated tail can
      make identification harder but does not change the species.
    - >-
      The upper side is usually olive, bronze, or brownish with dark spots or
      lengthwise rows. Pale longitudinal stripes are often visible along the
      sides, and the belly is light.
    - >-
      It is small: classical descriptions give a body length of about 5 cm and a
      tail of about 10 cm. A separate Georgian measurement series is not cited on
      this page.
    - >-
      The [Asian snake-eyed skink](ablepharus-pannonicus) is much smaller, smooth
      scaled, and often moves under cover. Ophisops is a lacertid, with longer
      limbs and faster movement on open ground.
    - >-
      [Steppe-runner](eremias-arguta) and [fast racerunner](eremias-velox) also
      use dry open habitats, but they do not have the fused snake-like eyelid of
      Ophisops. Green lizards such as [Caspian green lizard](lacerta-strigata)
      are much larger.

  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-

  - question: Is it venomous?
    answer: >-
      No. The snake-eyed lizard is harmless to people. Wild lizards still should
      not be handled.
  - question: How does it differ from the snake-eyed skink?

      larger lacertid with longer limbs and faster movement on open ground.
