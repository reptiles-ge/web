overview: >-
  The javelin sand boa (Eryx jaculus) is a non-venomous burrowing boa found in
  Georgia mainly in the semi-arid south-east; there is also an isolated photo
  observation from Mtskheta–Mtianeti. The 2026 checklist treats it as
  biologically confirmed and lists the subspecies E. j. turcicus. The 2021
  Georgian snake survey places it in the same landscape as the Levantine viper
  and Urartian ratsnake — not across the whole country. A named barcode and a
  checklist figure are from Tbilisi. Photos on this atlas are from Lake
  Kumisi, June 2020 (Giorgi Iankoshvili) and Lisi Lake, 2 July 2023 (Ioane
  Rostiashvili). It is usually 30–50 cm long, though some grow larger. It
  subdues prey by wrapping its body around it.
habitat: >-
  Look in dry open country with loose soil — sandy or loamy flats, semi-desert,
  sparse shrub, and stony slopes. It spends most of the day underground, in a
  burrow, or under a stone; it usually appears at dusk or at night. The 2021
  survey places it in the south-eastern semi-arid landscape. The map highlights
  confirmed distribution in Tbilisi and Kvemo Kartli; Kakheti and
  Mtskheta–Mtianeti have individual records only, not established range across
  those regions. Named points are Tbilisi (CaBOL 1027415; also the checklist
  figure), photos from Lake Kumisi (June 2020) and Lisi Lake (2 July 2023),
  public iNaturalist photos from Dedoplistskaro Municipality, and one 2012
  observation from Mtskheta Municipality. Obscured public coordinates are
  approximate and cannot establish a region on their own; record counts are not
  population density. There is no confirmed range in western
  Georgia, Colchic forest, or the high mountains. A separate elevation band
  for Georgia is not published here.
diet: >-
  It subdues prey by wrapping its body around it, not with venom. Small
  mammals and lizards are known foods. Their proportions in its Georgian
  diet are unknown.
behavior: >-
  The 2021 Georgian review lists it as nocturnal.
  It is rarely seen by day; encounters are more likely on a road, on freshly
  turned soil, or when a stone is lifted. It is live-bearing: eggs develop
  inside the female and young are born alive. Litter size and the exact season
  in Georgia are not published here. If you meet one, watch and leave it —
  do not pick it up. It is on the national Red List; removing it from the wild
  is restricted by law.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists it globally as Least Concern — the range is wide. Georgia’s 2014
  Red List (Resolution No. 190) treats it as Vulnerable (VU), criterion B2a —
  a small fragmented range. The list cites Duméril & Bibron 1844 as authors;
  the accepted name is Eryx jaculus (Linnaeus, 1758). The 2026 checklist places
  it in Boidae. Local threats are conversion of dry habitat, roadkill, and
  killing after confusion with vipers. Do not collect it or kill it.
interaction: >-
  Non-venomous; its bite contains no venom. Do not handle it: it is easily
  injured, it is Red Listed, and it can be mistaken for a viper. This
  non-venomous snake also has vertical pupils, so a pupil alone cannot identify
  an unknown snake safely. Keep your distance.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A short, stout harmless boa with small eyes and a blunt tail. The first
    photo is from Lake Kumisi (June 2020), then Lisi Lake (2 July 2023). Later frames show
    the species from other parts of the range.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is short and stout, almost cylindrical. The head is weakly set
      off from the neck and covered in small scales — not the large plates of
      most colubrids. The tail is short with a blunt tip; it can look like a
      second head. It is usually 30–50 cm long, though some grow larger.
    - >-
      The eyes are small but visible. The pupil is a vertical slit. A large
      rostral scale helps it push into soil. Dorsal scales are smooth in front
      and weakly keeled toward the tail. The belly scales are narrow.
    - >-
      The ground colour is grey, sand, brownish, or reddish. The back has
      darker irregular blotches or bars. A dark streak often runs from the eye
      to the mouth corner. The belly is pale. Pattern varies — do not rely on
      colour alone.
faq:
  - question: Does the javelin sand boa occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is biologically confirmed on the 2026 checklist. Its confirmed
      range is in the semi-arid south-east; there is an isolated photo record
      in Mtskheta–Mtianeti, but it is not established throughout the country.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In dry open country with loose soil. The map confirms distribution in
      Tbilisi and Kvemo Kartli; Kakheti and Mtskheta–Mtianeti have photo
      records only. Named points include Tbilisi (CaBOL), Lake Kumisi (photo,
      June 2020), Lisi Lake (photo, 2 July 2023), and iNaturalist records from
      Dedoplistskaro and Mtskheta municipalities. These records do not imply
      occurrence throughout each region. It is rarely seen by day.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      It is a non-venomous boa that subdues prey by constriction. Do not pick
      it up: it is Red Listed and can be mistaken for a viper. Vertical pupils
      alone do not identify a venomous snake.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A short stout snake with small eyes, a blunt tail, and a head covered in
      small scales.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      It is usually 30–50 cm long, though some grow larger. This is a general
      size for the species, not a measured Georgian average.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Small mammals and lizards are known foods. Their proportions in its
      Georgian diet are unknown.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      Mainly at night and at dusk. It spends most of the day underground. An
      exact Georgian seasonal calendar is not published here.
  - question: Is it on the Georgian Red List?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2014 Red List lists it as Vulnerable (VU, B2a) — a small
      fragmented range. Globally IUCN lists it as Least Concern.
