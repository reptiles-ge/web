overview: >-
  Clark's rock lizard (Darevskia clarkorum) occurs in Georgia as a harmless
  rock lizard of southwestern Adjara. It belongs to the true lizard family
  (Lacertidae). It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. Other English
  names include Clarks' lizard; the Georgian form is also called the Charnali
  lizard. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed, not
  as a candidate species. The type locality is in Turkey — Cankurtaran Pass
  between Hopa and Borçka (Artvin), not in Georgia. The Georgian population is
  kept here as a geographic subspecies: D. c. dryada. Do not assume it
  throughout the country: named modern material is from Charnali Gorge
  (Khelvachauri area). This atlas map marks Adjara from those records; it does
  not mean the species is limited to one gorge or present along the entire
  Adjara coast. IUCN assessed the species as Endangered in 2009. It is on
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List as EN.
habitat: >-
  It occurs in Georgia. Do not assume it in the eastern lowlands, the Javakheti
  highlands, or the Greater Caucasus. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 figure a
  specimen and barcodes from the Charnali river valley. Distribution confidence
  is medium. The type locality of dryada is the same gorge, Khelvachauri
  district (Darevsky & Tuniyev 1997). The original species description already
  included Adjara specimens together with the Turkish type locality (Darevsky
  & Vedmederja 1977). The 2009 IUCN account names a small Georgian isolate on
  the western slopes of Mtirala near Batumi; that point is not a named barcode
  in the 2026 checklist and is not treated as a confirmed locality on this
  page. Kintrishi Gorge appears in some sources as a possible northern limit —
  that is a personal communication, not a named record in this atlas. Elevation
  figures disagree: IUCN gives 1,500–2,200 m for clarkorum, but 50–700 m for
  dryada (the Charnali form). Turkish field samples have been measured from
  about 450–2,250 m (Ilgaz 2007; Bülbül et al. 2016). The exact elevation of
  the Charnali barcodes is not given on this page. Habitat is a rocky gorge
  under humid forest: large stones, outcrops, often near a stream. Darevsky &
  Tuniyev 1997 describe it as a typical rock-dweller (petrobiont) — stone
  heaps, rocky outcrops, stony talus. In Turkey it has also been taken in
  bushy ground by streams and, at some sites, on building walls (Ilgaz 2007).
  That is a Turkish biotope, not a full description of Charnali. Look for it on
  stones used for basking and shelter. Its closest relatives, D. mixta and D.
  caucasica, are geographically isolated: mixta is absent from Adjara.
diet: >-
  This page has no Georgian stomach-content study. Caucasian rock lizards
  usually eat small invertebrates. Franzen 1991 also recorded plant feeding in
  Turkish clarkorum — a Turkish observation, not a Georgian menu.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. It is a rock lizard: it basks on stones and hides in
  crevices. Darevsky & Tuniyev 1997 note that it comes out in cloudy or foggy
  weather and even in rain — that is a species description, not a Charnali
  hourly table. Turkish samples were taken about 08:30–18:30, often in cloudy
  or rainy weather (Ilgaz 2007). A full Georgian seasonal calendar is not on
  this page. It lays eggs. Clutch size in Georgia is not measured here. In
  Turkish populations sexual maturity is about 1–2 years at low elevation and
  2–3 years higher up; longevity there is described up to 10–12 years (Bülbül
  et al. 2016). That is a Turkish age table, not a Charnali calendar. If
  grabbed, the tail may break and the lizard may bite. Watch it and leave it
  where it is. Do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assessed the species as Endangered in 2009 (B1ab(i,iii); assessment
  2008): extent of occurrence under 5,000 km², fewer than five locations, and
  continuing habitat decline from overgrazing. The assessment is old, and its
  elevation range does not match later field data. The same authors assessed
  the Charnali form separately as D. dryada — Critically Endangered
  (CR B2ab(iii,v)), because area of occupancy is probably under 10 km², there
  is a single subpopulation, and forest habitat is shrinking. The 2026
  checklist does not keep dryada as a species, but that IUCN account still
  shows how narrow and sensitive the Charnali population is. Georgia’s 2014
  Red List (Resolution No. 190) lists Clark's rock lizard as EN, citing IUCN;
  the same table’s note calls it Vulnerable worldwide — an internal
  inconsistency in that document; this page keeps the category as EN.
  Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat it as biologically confirmed. A Georgian
  population size and trend are not measured here. It is not listed on CITES.
  Do not take wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and poses no medical risk. If handled it may bite; the
  tail may break. Leave it where it is. Do not keep wild animals.

  traits:
    - >-
      It is a rock Darevskia with well-developed limbs — not a ground-dwelling
      forest lizard (D. derjugini) and not a large ground Lacerta. In Turkish
      material the back is ivy green, light green, brown or greyish brown with
      small black dots; the temporal stripe is dark with 3–4 pale-centred
      rings, often bluish at the forelimb; the belly is bright yellow, the
      outer ventral plates blue (Ilgaz 2007). That is typical appearance and
      does not separate species by colour.
    - >-
      Snout–vent length in Turkish series is often about 4.4–6.7 cm; the tail
      is longer than the body (Ilgaz 2007). Arribas et al. 2021 describe
      Charnali dryada as larger than those series — a longer head and higher
      dorsal scale counts. A full Georgian measurement series is not on this
      page. The masseteric plate and frontonasal index no longer separate the
      taxa reliably (Arribas et al. 2021).
    - >-
      The Adjara rock lizard (D. adjarica) occurs in the same southwestern
      Adjara, with Shuakhevi material; it is a candidate in the parvula
      complex and is not clarkorum. The Colchic rock lizard (D. mixta) is the
      closest relative but is absent from Adjara. The Caucasian rock lizard
      (D. caucasica) is a Greater Caucasus species. Colour will not tell them
      apart.
    - >-
      Derjugin's lizard (D. derjugini) also occurs in Adjara, but mainly on
      the ground and in forest litter, not in rock crevices. Checklist figures
      show head scalation and preanal plates (Charnali).
faq:
  - question: Is Clark's rock lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is biologically confirmed in the 2026 checklist. Figure and
      barcodes: Charnali Gorge, Khelvachauri area.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In southwestern Adjara, with a named record in Charnali Gorge. Look on
      rock and large stones under humid forest, often near a gorge stream. Do
      not assume it throughout Georgia.
  - question: Is it a separate species, D. dryada?
    answer: >-
      Not in this atlas. The 2026 checklist ranks the Charnali form as a
      subspecies (D. c. dryada): mitochondrial differences from Turkish
      clarkorum fall in a high-similarity range. Some authors still treat
      dryada as a species on phenotype; IUCN assessed it separately in 2009.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A medium rock lizard with a green or brown back and a yellow belly.
      Colour is not enough. In Adjara compare it with adjarica and
      ground-dwelling derjugini. Place (a Charnali-type gorge) and head
      scalation are more reliable than back colour.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. If grabbed it may bite; the tail may break. Leave
      it where it is.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Snout–vent length in Turkish material is often about 4.5–7 cm. The
      Charnali form is described in the literature as larger than those
      series. A full Georgian measurement series is not on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      A Georgian diet list has not been published. Small invertebrates are
      expected; plant feeding has been observed in Turkey. That is not a full
      ration.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      By day. A full Georgian seasonal calendar is not on this page. It is
      described as coming out in cloudy and rainy weather; Turkish samples
      were taken from morning to evening.
