overview: >-
  The rapid racerunner (Eremias velox) is a fast, harmless lacertid lizard confirmed in Georgia's dry south-east, especially Kakheti. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 list it as biologically confirmed and describe it as fairly common in south-eastern Georgia. The same paper illustrates material from Vashlovani National Park and reports DNA-barcoded specimens from Pantishara Canyon. On the evidence used here, the confirmed Georgian range should be treated as Kakheti rather than the whole country. Georgian material is assigned to E. v. caucasia. COI barcodes suggest that the Caucasian form may need taxonomic revision, but this page follows the current Georgian checklist and keeps it within Eremias velox. Globally, the IUCN assesses the species as Least Concern; this is not a Georgian national status.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, the species is reliably tied to the dry south-east of Kakheti. Confirmed material includes Vashlovani National Park and Pantishara Canyon. Realistic encounter habitats are open, warm and dry landscapes: steppe, semi-desert, low shrubland, stony or sandy ground, and bare patches between low vegetation. The exact elevational range in Georgia is not summarized in the sources used for this page.
diet: >-
  It feeds on small invertebrates. The sources used here do not give a Georgian diet series, so prey should be understood broadly as small insects and other small invertebrates typically taken by fast ground-foraging lacertids.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day and escapes quickly into low vegetation or nearby shelter when disturbed. In the field, it is most plausible in sunny, dry, open places where bare ground alternates with low cover. A complete Georgian activity calendar is not available in the sources used here, so exact months still need verification.
conservation: >-
  The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. The global IUCN category is Least Concern, but a separate Georgian national status has not been verified here. Threats in Georgia do not appear to be quantified in the sources used for this page; disturbance of dry steppe and semi-desert habitat should be treated as a potential concern, not as a documented decline.
interaction: >-
  It is harmless to people: it has no venom and is not a medically significant species. Do not handle it; a captured lizard may bite, shed its tail or be injured. The best response is to photograph it from a distance and leave it where it is.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A slender, long-tailed Eremias of dry open habitats in Kakheti. The most useful field clues are body proportions, pale stripes or side ocelli, and comparison with the steppe-runner.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is slim, the legs are relatively long, and the tail looks much longer than the body. These proportions suit a fast lizard of open ground, but speed alone is not enough for identification.
    - >-
      Colour and pattern vary, but many individuals show a sandy, greyish or brownish back with pale lengthwise stripes and pale spots or ocelli along the sides. Juveniles, as in many racerunners, may look more sharply striped.
    - >-
      Close characters can help in good photos: the subocular scale, a large scale below the eye, borders the mouth, and the fourth toe lacks a distinct sand fringe along the sides. Check these on photographs or found dead specimens; do not catch a live lizard for scale characters.
    - >-
      The closest practical comparison is the [steppe-runner](eremias-arguta). That species occurs in the same south-eastern region but is listed as sporadic; rapid racerunner usually gives a slimmer, longer-tailed and faster Eremias impression.
    - >-
      [Snake-eyed lizard](ophisops-elegans) can share dry habitats, but its eye looks different because the eyelids are fused into a clear window. [Caspian green lizard](lacerta-strigata) and other Lacerta are often larger, greener or differently built.
    - >-
      Location matters in Georgia. Reliable records used here point to dry south-eastern Kakheti; a photograph from western Georgia, forested country or high mountains should not be identified as this species without supporting evidence.
    - >-
      It is harmless to people. For identification photos, take clear views from the side, from above, and with the habitat visible instead of handling the animal.
