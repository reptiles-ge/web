commonName: Eurasian sparrowhawk
location: Year-round resident and migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A small woodland hawk with short wings and a long tail. Confirmed in Georgia as
  a year-round resident and passage migrant on the 2024 national bird checklist.
overview: >-
  The Eurasian sparrowhawk (Accipiter nisus) is a small woodland hawk confirmed
  in Georgia as a year-round resident and a passage migrant. The 2024 annotated
  checklist of the birds of Georgia codes it YR-R; M: a breeder present
  throughout the year, and also a bird of passage in spring and autumn.
  Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality is high
  (H), with photographic evidence. The usual Georgian name is მიმინო; ქორი is a
  different species (northern goshawk). Adults are about 29–41 cm long at
  species level; females are clearly larger than males. It is not a threat to
  people. IUCN lists the species as Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns
  the same category nationally.
habitat: >-
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level resident and migrant status but does not
  publish a regional gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly across Georgia,
  including treeless high plateaus. Abuladze (2011, 2013) treats it in Georgia
  as a common forest breeder, a widespread passage migrant, and a winter
  visitor, with wintering birds often in towns and villages. Named locality:
  the Agency of Protected Areas lists the species among raptors of
  Batsara-Babaneuri Protected Areas (Akhmeta Municipality). In autumn the
  eastern Black Sea bottleneck near Batumi (Sakhalvasho, Shuamta) is a main
  watchpoint; birds pass low and solitarily there, so complete counts are
  difficult. Species-level habitat is woodland — deciduous, coniferous, or mixed
  — with forest edge, parks, orchards, and urban greenery from which it hunts by
  surprise. A separate national elevation band for Georgia is not published
  here.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range it
  takes almost exclusively birds: males smaller prey (tits, finches, sparrows),
  females larger (thrushes and thrush-sized birds; occasionally a pigeon). Small
  mammals appear rarely in species-level literature; that is not a measured
  Georgian menu. It hunts by surprise, a short dash from cover, often at garden
  feeders or woodland edge. It is not food for people.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. Flight is a flap-flap-glide; the silhouette is T-shaped,
  with a long, almost square-ended tail and blunt wing-tips. It is highly
  manoeuvrable in trees. The call is a sharp, rapid kek-kek-kek or similar
  series, more often around the nest; prey alarm calls are often heard first.
  Do not handle it. The nest is a stick platform in a tree. Species-level
  clutches are usually four to five eggs, with incubation of about a month;
  those figures are from the wider range, not a Georgian nest sample on this
  page. The 2024 checklist treats the species as a breeding resident in Georgia.
  Northern populations are migratory; southern birds are more sedentary. Georgia
  has both: local breeders and a passage flow, especially in autumn at the
  Batumi bottleneck.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Accipiter nisus as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List, with a stable trend. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global
  and national Red List category LC. A current national census is not given on
  this page. Abuladze’s 2011 summary spoke of several hundred breeding pairs;
  that is a period estimate, not a new national count. The 2014 Georgian Red
  List (Decree No. 190) includes Levant sparrowhawk (Accipiter brevipes, VU),
  not this species. Accipiter nisus still appears on state wildlife-accounting
  lists. Documented local pressures are illegal shooting and traditional
  trapping for falconry in Ajara at the Batumi bottleneck. Do not catch wild
  birds without a permit. Globally, organochlorine pesticides once thinned
  eggshells; after bans, many populations recovered. That is not a Georgian
  monitoring series.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people. It may take small birds in gardens. Wild birds
  must not be captured; the talons are strong.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small hawk with short broad wings, a long tail, and yellow eyes. The male
    is blue-grey above with rufous barring below; the female is larger and
    browner. In Georgia the usual confusion is Levant sparrowhawk on migration
    and northern goshawk by size.
  traits:
    - >-
      Silhouette: short, rounded wings and a long, almost square-ended tail.
      Flight is flap-flap-glide, not the pointed wings of a falcon. The adult
      eye is yellow to orange; the legs are long and yellow. Length about 29–41
      cm at species level. Males about 29–34 cm and 105–196 g, females 35–41 cm
      and 185–350 g — among the largest size gaps between the sexes in raptors.
      A separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this page.
    - >-
      The male is slate blue-grey above, with rufous-barred cheeks and breast;
      the underparts can look plain orange at a distance. The female is dark
      brown or grey-brown above, brown-barred below, often with a clearer
      supercilium. Juveniles resemble the female but the barring is coarser or
      blotchy, and the eye is paler yellow. Juveniles usually lack a dark
      mid-throat stripe — a Levant sparrowhawk mark.
    - >-
      Levant sparrowhawk (Accipiter brevipes; IOC now Tachyspiza brevipes) is a
      breeding migrant in Georgia on the 2024 checklist (BB; M) and is VU on
      the national Red List. It has more pointed wings, dark wing-tips, a less
      barred underwing, and a dark adult eye, not yellow. It often migrates in
      flocks; Eurasian sparrowhawk usually passes singly. Northern goshawk
      (Accipiter gentilis; IOC Astur gentilis) is much bulkier, with broader
      wings, a white supercilium, and a more rounded tail. Shikra (Accipiter
      badius) is casual (Cas) on the checklist; field identification is
      specialist. Kestrel and hobby are pointed-winged falcons, not hawks. This
      atlas does not yet have separate pages for those lookalikes.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Leave it alone. A small hawk appearing
      suddenly in a Georgian garden or park is more likely this species than a
      goshawk.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 in Systema Naturae. The 2024 Georgian
    checklist follows the OSME Region List for names: order Accipitriformes,
    family Accipitridae, genus Accipiter. IOC World Bird List keeps Accipiter
    nisus in Accipiter; northern goshawk is now Astur gentilis and Levant
    sparrowhawk Tachyspiza brevipes. Field identification is to species; the
    checklist still uses the older genus names.
  - >-
    Handbooks assign Caucasus birds to the nominate subspecies, A. n. nisus
    (Europe and Asia Minor east to western Siberia and Iran). Eastern A. n.
    nisosimilis is a more Siberian form and is not a field split in Georgia.
    Macaronesian A. n. granti does not occur here.
  - >-
    The usual Georgian name is მიმინო; this page title, ქორისებრი მიმინო, marks
    the hawk family (Accipitridae). English names in use are Eurasian
    Sparrowhawk (2024 checklist), northern sparrowhawk, and sparrowhawk.
    Russian is ястреб-перепелятник. ქორი (goshawk) and ქორცქვიტა (Levant
    sparrowhawk) are other species.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R; M, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    confirmation plus the named literature and localities above, not a complete
    regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the Eurasian sparrowhawk found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident and passage migrant (YR-R; M).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In woodland, forest edge, parks, and towns, where it hunts small birds. It
      is named for Batsara-Babaneuri Protected Areas. In autumn the Batumi
      migration bottleneck is a main watchpoint, though birds pass singly. This
      page does not map every region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Short broad wings, long tail, yellow eye, flap-flap-glide. Male blue-grey
      above with rufous-barred breast; female larger and browner. Levant
      sparrowhawk has a dark eye and more pointed wings; goshawk is much bulkier.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 29–41 cm at species level. Females are clearly larger than males
      (about 185–350 g versus 105–196 g). A separate measured series from Georgia
      is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly small birds. Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia on
      this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year as a resident, plus spring and autumn passage, according to the
      2024 checklist.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means resident breeder.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. It is globally Least Concern and coded LC nationally on
      the 2024 checklist. It is not on the 2014 Georgian Red List (Levant
      sparrowhawk is VU there). Do not catch wild birds.
