commonName: Common pheasant
location: Year-round resident in Georgia
description: >-
  A large ground-living gamebird of floodplain scrub, riparian forest and
  semi-open foothills. Confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident on the
  2024 national bird checklist. The species name refers to Colchis in western
  Georgia.
overview: >-
  The pheasant (Phasianus colchicus) is a large chicken-like bird in the pheasant family (Phasianidae). Its usual Georgian name is khokhobi. The name “Colchian pheasant” draws attention to its local Colchian/Caucasian origins. Georgia’s 2024 bird list gives it the code YR-R: it breeds in Georgia and lives there year-round. The information on when it is found during the year is of high quality (code A). Its presence in Georgia is also well documented (code H), with a photo, a sound recording and a bird obtained through hunting. It poses no direct threat to people. The IUCN rates the species as facing little threat. The 2024 list also assigns it the LC category for Georgia. Across the species as a whole, males are about 65–89 cm long and females about 53–70 cm long. They have long, pointed tails. Its scientific name means “pheasant of Colchis.” A site on the Rioni River (ancient Phasis) in western Georgia was later named as the place where the bird used to describe the species had been obtained.
habitat: >-
  Look for it in dense lowland cover: riparian forest, thick undergrowth along
  river valleys, scrub, dry grassland with bushes, and open dry foothill forest.
  It runs more than it flies; short strong flights end in cover. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level resident status but does not publish a
  municipality map. Do not assume it occurs evenly across Georgia, including
  high alpine zones. Named modern monitoring: Javakhishvili and Melikishvili
  (2022) surveyed Vashlovani Protected Areas (Kakheti) in April 2021 with
  crowing counts on Alazani riparian forest (about 150–250 m) and on Shavi Mta
  semi-arid light forest and scrub (about 300–800 m), treating the species as
  present up to about 1,000 m in that study area. Georgian encyclopedic sources
  historically place it on the Colchis lowland and in the Mtkvari, Iori and
  Alazani floodplains, often to about 850 m. Subspecies literature on the
  nominate black-necked form describes remnant western populations around the
  Lake Paliastomi and Rioni basins; extinction is reported for the Chorokh
  valley of southern Georgia and the Kodori valley in Abkhazia — those
  statements are from secondary range summaries, not a new Georgian atlas
  census. Captive breeding and releases (for example Khobi / Nojikhevi in
  Samegrelo, and releases at Krtsanisi Forest Park near Tbilisi) are
  conservation and restocking actions; they are not a full wild distribution
  map. The map uses public iNaturalist observations not marked captive. It
  highlights distribution only where the records-by-region table has confirmed
  status. The Vashlovani study and western literature describe localities, but
  do not automatically change the table status; reports of breeding and releases
  do not determine distribution status on their own.
diet: >-
  There is no separate weighed Georgian diet study on this page. At species
  level it takes plant material (seeds, berries, green shoots) and invertebrates,
  especially insects important for chicks. Georgian encyclopedic sources
  likewise emphasise weed seeds, fruit, vegetative parts and insects. It is a
  traditional gamebird in Georgia; hunting is regulated separately from Red List
  categories. Lynx diet accounts for the Vashlovani semi-arid zone name pheasant
  among main prey with hare and chukar.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. Males are territorial in the breeding season and gather
  several females; the male’s loud two-note crow, often with wing-whirring, is
  used for monitoring in Georgia. Nests are on the ground in thick cover.
  Species-level clutches are often about 8–15 eggs, incubation about 23–28 days,
  and broods stay with the female for about 70–80 days — those figures are from
  the Vashlovani monitoring report’s species summary and wider handbooks, not a
  nationwide Georgian nest sample on this page. The 2024 checklist treats the
  species as a resident breeder. It is not a long-distance migrant like many
  passerines. Do not chase or handle wild birds.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Phasianus colchicus as Least Concern on the
  IUCN Red List, with a very large global range that includes both native and
  widely introduced populations. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global
  and national category LC. Evidence codes include hunter samples (CH), so
  regulated hunting is part of the documented Georgian picture. The National
  Wildlife Agency presents the Colchic pheasant in Red List materials with
  national status LC and runs captive breeding and release programmes for native
  stock; MEPA and agency press notes describe restocking into protected areas
  and sites such as Krtsanisi. Whether the species appears as a listed entry on
  Government Resolution No. 190 (2014) should be checked against the official
  list text; this page follows the 2024 checklist’s national LC coding and
  agency LC wording rather than assuming a higher threat category. Local
  pressures named in the Vashlovani 2021 work include sheep-herding disturbance
  and dogs, illegal hunting near protected-area edges, and agricultural burning.
  Hybridisation with introduced farm or ring-necked stock is a documented
  concern for genetically distinctive black-necked (nominate) populations across
  the western native range; purity of every Georgian subpopulation is not proven
  on this page. A separate national population trend series is not given here.
interaction: >-
  It is not dangerous to people. Do not chase, catch or keep wild birds without permission. Adult males have spurs on their legs. They may injure you if you handle them improperly. Where hunting is legal, follow the rules in force. The relevant authorities publish information on protected areas and periods when hunting is prohibited. Leave nests and remains undisturbed.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large, long-tailed game bird. The male is colorful, with a green head, red skin on its face and a long, striped tail. The female is brown, which helps her blend in. The Caucasian form usually has no white ring around its neck. Some European photos on this page show birds introduced from elsewhere that have a white ring around their necks.
  traits:
    - >-
      Males of this species are about 65–89 cm long. Their long, pointed tails are often more than 35 cm long. Females are about 53–70 cm long, with shorter tails measuring about 20–40 cm. According to Georgian encyclopedias, males weigh about 1.6 kg and females about 0.8 kg. This page does not provide separate measurements for birds measured in Georgia. Males have glossy dark green or bluish-black heads, bright red skin on their faces, and tufts of feathers near their ears. The copper-colored feathers on their breasts and sides look like scales. Their long brown tails have stripes. Females are mottled brown, have shorter tails, and lack red skin on their faces. Both have strong legs and short, rounded wings. They run well and take off noisily.
    - >-
      Male P. c. colchicus (the black-throated, or Colchian, group) have no white ring around the throat. Many “white-throated” groups introduced to new areas or formed through crossbreeding have a white collar. The gallery on this page shows European birds, some of which may have a visible collar. A white ring is not evidence that a bird belongs to the Georgian P. c. colchicus group. The absence of a ring alone is also not enough to identify its subspecies: where the bird was seen and its other features must be considered. Young birds are browner and have shorter tails than adult males.
    - >-
      Chukar (Alectoris chukar; გავაზი) is a stockier rock partridge with bold
      flank bars and a different face pattern; YR-R on the 2024 checklist. Grey
      partridge (Perdix perdix) is much smaller and shorter-tailed; YR-R. Black
      francolin (Francolinus francolinus) is darker, shorter-tailed, and coded
      YR-R with national NT on the checklist. Caucasian grouse (Lyrurus
      mlokosiewiczi) is a highland forest grouse, not a lowland pheasant. This
      atlas does not yet have separate pages for those lookalikes.
    - >-
      It is harmless to humans. In Georgia, a large, long-tailed bird that people hunt, flying up from floodplain shrubs or vegetation in the foothills, is usually this species. Look at its size and tail length. Males have green heads and red faces.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 in Systema Naturae. The 2024 Georgian
    checklist follows the OSME Region List for names: order Galliformes, family
    Phasianidae, genus Phasianus. Field identification is to species; subspecies
    in Georgia are discussed mainly as the nominate Colchic / black-necked group
    versus introduced or hybrid farm stock.
  - >-
    The epithet colchicus refers to Colchis (western Georgia). The type locality
    was later fixed to the Rioni River (ancient Phasis). Handbooks place native
    Caucasus birds in the black-necked group (P. c. colchicus and allies).
    Ring-necked eastern subspecies and game-farm mixtures dominate many
    introduced European populations.
  - >-
    Georgian is ხოხობი; კოლხური ხოხობი is widely used for the native form.
    English names in use are Common Pheasant (2024 checklist) and, for the
    nominate group, black-necked or Colchic pheasant. Russian is обыкновенный
    фазан. გავაზი (chukar) is a different species.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R, data quality A, global and national Red List LC,
    occurrence quality H, evidence PH; SR; CH (photograph; sound recording;
    collected by hunter). That is country-level status. The regional map on this
    page shows iNaturalist observations not marked captive; confirmed status
    in the records-by-region table determines distribution.
faq:
  - question: Is the common pheasant found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident (YR-R).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In dense lowland and foothill cover — riparian forest, scrub and semi-open
      dry forest. Named modern survey work covers Vashlovani Protected Areas in
      Kakheti. Western Colchic literature names the Paliastomi and Rioni basins.
      Only regions confirmed in the map table are marked as distribution;
      other observations appear separately.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Large long-tailed gamebird. Male: green head, red face, coppery scaled
      body, long barred tail. Female: mottled brown, shorter tail. Caucasus
      nominate males typically lack a white neck-ring; many European photos show
      a white collar.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      Males about 65–89 cm and females about 53–70 cm at species level, including
      the long tail. A separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this
      page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Seeds, other plant material, and insects. Diet has not been studied
      separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year as a resident breeder, according to the 2024 checklist. Male
      crowing counts in Vashlovani were run in mid-April.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means resident breeder. Nests are on the
      ground in thick cover.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless to people. Globally and on the 2024 national checklist it is
      Least Concern (LC). It is a traditional game species; follow current
      hunting and protected-area rules. Do not disturb nests.
