commonName: "Valentin's Lizard"
location: "Samtskhe — Javakheti — Javakheti highlands"
description: >-
  Valentin's Lizard is a non-venomous rock lizard of the Javakheti highlands and one of the parent species of the Armenian Rock Lizard. You cannot identify it by colour alone.
overview: >-
  Valentin’s lizard (Darevskia valentini) is a confirmed species in Georgia; published biological records from Georgia name Ninotsminda Municipality. It belongs to the true lizard family (Lacertidae). It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. In English, it is called Valentin's lizard or Caucasian rock lizard. A 2026 Georgian list includes it as a confirmed species, rather than merely a possible one. Its subspecies with the same name is D. v. valentini. The specimen used for its scientific description was found in Armenia, at Basarchay / Bazarkent, not in Georgia. Published biological records name two places in Ninotsminda Municipality: Lake Saghamo and the Kirkhbulaki River. The records-by-region table lists observations from Samtskhe-Javakheti, but the region has confirmed status in the records-by-region table and is marked on the map; this reflects the record threshold, not uniform presence throughout the region. This does not mean the species is found only at those two places. The IUCN classifies it as Least Concern. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  Its named Georgian records are from Ninotsminda Municipality; this does not make all of Samtskhe-Javakheti confirmed range. Its presence should not be assumed in the wet Colchic forests of western Georgia or in the eastern lowlands. A lizard pictured in a 2026 study is from Lake Saghamo in Ninotsminda Municipality; DNA analysis data come from Lake Saghamo and the Kirkhbulak River. Confidence in the distribution data is moderate. The Armenian rock lizard (Darevskia armeniaca), which reproduces without males, has also been recorded at Lake Saghamo. It is a different species, and valentini is one of its parent species. At Lake Saghamo, these lizards have been recorded at about 2,015 metres. In the Lesser Caucasus, the species has often been recorded at 1,900–3,110 metres; IUCN gives a range of 1,300–3,000 metres, including records from Turkey. These do not establish its full elevation limits in Georgia. This page does not give the exact elevation of the location of the DNA data from the Kirkhbulak River. The species lives in high mountain meadows and steppes with rocky hills and cliffs. It also occurs by cliffs beside roads or rivers and along lake shores, but not in barren desert or wet Colchic forest. It may be seen on rocks, where it warms itself and takes shelter. One prediction of its range includes several separate areas of high mountain meadow and steppe, including in southern Georgia. This is a prediction, not another named location where it has been found in Georgia. The records-by-region table marks Samtskhe-Javakheti as confirmed; this reflects records in the region and does not mean every suitable mountain has been surveyed.
diet: >-
  In the highlands of Armenia, Valentin's Lizard eats mainly beetles and flies. Other insects, spiders and plant parts turn up in its diet only rarely. These findings come from the Armenian mountains, so they do not mean it feeds exactly the same way everywhere in Georgia.
behavior: |-
  It is active during the day and lives on rocks. It warms itself on stone and hides in crevices when threatened. In Armenia, it emerges after winter between late April and mid-May. It mates soon afterward and lays eggs in the second half of June. It has also been seen there through the end of September. These dates apply to Armenia and may differ in Georgia.

  In Georgia, a lizard specimen was collected near Lake Saghamo in May–June. There, it laid 2–6 eggs at a time; in Armenia, the average was about six, with a maximum of 12. In both places, it usually lays eggs only once per season. If you catch it, it may lose its tail or bite you. Watch it, but do not pick it up — leave it where it is.
conservation: >-
  A 2009 global assessment rated Valentin's Lizard as of "least concern", because it is widespread and its numbers are probably large. That assessment is now old. It is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190), nor on the CITES list. Georgia's 2026 checklist includes it as a species confirmed for the country. Where it meets the Obscure Rock Lizard and Portschinsky's Lizard in a narrow strip, gene exchange between them has been described. This is not a threat category, but it makes it harder to tell where one species ends and the next begins. Do not catch wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is not dangerous to your health. If you pick it up, it may bite, and its tail may come off. Leave it where you found it. It is a wild animal, so do not keep it at home.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Valentin's Lizard is a relatively large rock lizard found in the Javakheti highlands. Its back is often greenish-brown or bright green, but colour alone won't tell it apart from other rock lizards of the genus Darevskia. To identify it, also look at its scales, its size and the place where you saw it.
  traits:
    - >-
      This is a relatively large lizard of the genus Darevskia, with well-developed legs. It lives on cliffs and rocks and is not a large ground-dwelling Lacerta. Its back is often greenish-brown or bright green, and its belly is yellow or white. A stripe runs near the temple, marked with dark rings that have pale centers. This look is typical for it, but you cannot tell these species apart by color alone.
    - >-
      The head and body, not counting the tail, often measure about 6–7 cm. Among lizards measured in Armenia, one female reached 7.9 cm. Unusually for the genus Darevskia, females are often larger than males.
    - >-
      At Lake Saghamo, the Armenian Rock Lizard (D. armeniaca) occurs alongside Valentin's Lizard. Its females reproduce without males. One of its parent species is Valentin's Lizard itself; the other is D. mixta. The Armenian Rock Lizard often has a duller green back than Valentin's Lizard, with pale spots on its sides. Between the plate by the ear and the scale in the middle of the temple, it usually has one row of scales, while Valentin's Lizard has several. You cannot tell them apart by color alone.
    - >-
      Portschinsky's Lizard (D. portschinskii) is much smaller than Valentin's Lizard and lives only in the central Lesser Caucasus, for example in Kojori. The Obscure Rock Lizard (D. obscura) is named in the checklist as a possible candidate from the rudis group. In one study, photos of a lizard from Saghamo Lake show the head scales and the plates just in front of the anal opening. Valentin's Lizard, Portschinsky's Lizard and the Obscure Rock Lizard have been reported to meet in one small area, where their genes mix widely. So in that area, they cannot be told apart by color alone.
    - >-
      The 2026 list includes Valentin's Lizard as a confirmed species, not a possible one. In Ninotsminda Municipality, it has been seen at Lake Saghamo and the Kirkhbulak River. This does not mean it occurs throughout Georgia. The Armenian Rock Lizard (D. armeniaca), which reproduces without males, also occurs right there at Lake Saghamo. You cannot tell these two lizards apart by colour alone.
faq:
  - question: Is Valentin's lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 list confirms this with biological data. The picture of the lizard shown there is linked to Saghamo Lake, near Ninotsminda. The DNA data come from two places: Saghamo Lake and the Kirkhbulaki River.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Published records from Georgia name the areas around Saghamo Lake and Kirkhbulaki in Ninotsminda Municipality. The lizard has been recorded in rocky meadows, on cliffs and along lake shores. The map table marks Samtskhe-Javakheti as confirmed, but this does not mean the lizard occurs throughout the region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Not by color alone. Valentin's Lizard is a relatively large rock lizard. Compare it with the Armenian Rock Lizard (armeniaca), which reproduces without males and lives alongside Valentin's Lizard at Lake Saghamo. Also compare it with the smaller Portschinsky's Lizard (portschinskii) and Obscure Rock Lizard (obscura). The rows of scales near the ear and the place where you saw the lizard are more reliable clues than the color of its back.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom. If you catch it, it may bite you, and its tail may break off. So leave it where you found it.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-

  - question: Is it a candidate species?
    answer: >-
      No. The list includes Valentin's Lizard as a species confirmed by biological data. The Obscure Rock Lizard (D. obscura) is named as a possible member of this group, but Valentin's Lizard is not.
  - question: How does it differ from the Armenian rock lizard?
    answer: >-
      The Armenian Rock Lizard (armeniaca) reproduces without males, and Valentin's Lizard is its parent species. Both are found at Lake Saghamo. The difference shows near the ear: between the ear plate and the temple scale, the Armenian Rock Lizard usually has one row of scales, while Valentin's Lizard has several. Colour alone will not reliably tell them apart.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is active during the day. In Armenia, it has been seen from spring to autumn. In Georgia, a specimen was collected at Lake Saghamo in May–June. If you hope to see one, look for it in daylight.
