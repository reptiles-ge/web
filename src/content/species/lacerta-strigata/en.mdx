  A harmless lacertid of semiarid ground in Georgia. Juveniles show five pale

overview: >-
  The striped lizard (Lacerta strigata) is a confirmed, harmless lizard in
  Georgia that is mainly associated with semiarid habitats. It belongs to the
  true lizard family (Lacertidae). Adult snout–vent length is usually about
  7–11 cm, while total length at species level can reach about 30 cm. IUCN lists
  it as Least Concern, and it is not included on Georgia's 2014 Red List.

  Older sources sometimes grouped it with other green lizards, but Lacerta
  strigata is now recognized as a separate species.

  On this page's records-by-region table, distribution is confirmed in Kakheti,
  Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Tbilisi, Kvemo Kartli, and Shida Kartli. Imereti has only
  isolated records; that region is not treated as part of the range.
habitat: >-
  The striped lizard is confirmed in Georgia and is mainly linked to semiarid
  habitats. The map table confirms distribution in Kakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti,
  Tbilisi, Kvemo Kartli, and Shida Kartli. The Chankaani record in Dedoplistskaro
  Municipality sits inside Kakheti and does not add another region. Imereti has
  only isolated records, so it is not treated as part of the range. Older
  Caucasian literature mainly associates the species with eastern Georgia.
  Abkhazia is listed in an international database, but it has no records in this
  table and is not counted as distribution.

  Look for the striped lizard in steppe, semi-desert, open riverbank habitats,
  meadow and field edges, shelterbelts and bushy places. It often hides near
  shrubs, in dense grass, in rodent burrows or among rock piles. Across the
  species' broader range it has been described up to about 2,800–3,000 m above
  sea level, but that is not an established upper limit for Georgia.
diet: >-
  The striped lizard feeds mainly on small invertebrates. Beetles, spiders,
  snails, ants, flies, grasshoppers and other small invertebrates have been
  described in its diet.

  This information is based on species-level and wider regional data. The diet
  of Georgian populations has not been studied in detail on this page, so the
  prey list should not be generalized to every Georgian population.
behavior: >-
  The striped lizard is active by day. Caucasian data suggest that adults often
  become active after winter in March, while activity may begin earlier in mild
  weather. Adults usually enter winter dormancy in early autumn, and young may
  sometimes remain active later. These periods are not identical for every
  Georgian locality.

  Mating starts in spring, and egg-laying is described from late spring to early
  summer. Wider regional data suggest that a female may lay two clutches in a
  season, each with about 6–11 eggs. These figures do not represent a dedicated
  study of Georgian populations.

  If handled, the lizard may bite or shed its tail, so do not pick it up in the
  wild.
conservation: >-
  IUCN lists the striped lizard as Least Concern. It is not included on
  Georgia's 2014 Red List and is not listed in CITES appendices.

  The species is confirmed in Georgia, but the status of its local populations
  and specific threats have not been fully assessed. Loss or alteration of
  semiarid habitats is a potentially important pressure. Do not collect wild
  lizards; leave them where they are.
interaction: >-
  The striped lizard is not venomous and does not pose a danger to people. If
  picked up, it may bite defensively or shed its tail. In nature, it is best to
  leave it where it is and avoid disturbing it. There is no need to take a wild
  lizard home.

  summary: >-
    The striped lizard is a medium-sized lizard with well-developed limbs.
    Young individuals are usually brownish and have five pale stripes along the
    body. Adult males often become bright green, especially on the front of the
    body, while the rear can remain olive-brown. Do not rely on colour alone to
    separate it from other green lizards.
  traits:
    - >-
      The striped lizard is a robust lacertid with well-developed legs. It is
      neither a rock lizard (Darevskia) nor a skink. It has a distinct scale
      collar on the neck, and the belly has larger plates arranged in several
      longitudinal rows.

      These small characters are easier to see on a captured animal, so in the
      field overall size, colour, body pattern and habitat are usually more
      useful for identification.
    - >-
      Young striped lizards are olive-brown with five narrow pale stripes along
      the back and sides. They may also have small dark spots between the
      stripes. The stripes gradually fade with age, though females often retain
      traces for longer.

      Adult males are often bright green on the front of the body, while the
      rear and tail can remain olive-brown. During the breeding period, the
      head, throat and sides of the neck may become bluish. Females are usually
      paler and often keep spots or traces of stripes.
    - >-
      The sand lizard (Lacerta agilis) can resemble the striped lizard,
      especially when young, because both can have stripes. The sand lizard is
      widespread in western Georgia, while eastern records mainly point to sites
      above about 600–800 m.

      The striped lizard is more strongly linked to semiarid habitats. Colour
      alone is not a reliable way to separate the two species.
    - >-
      The Caucasian green lizard (Lacerta media) is another large green lizard
      that can resemble the striped lizard. In Georgia it is mainly associated
      with eastern semiarid areas, with separate records also known from the
      northwest.

      Where both species occur, pay attention to size, the pattern of young
      individuals and other distinguishing characters. Green colour alone is not
      enough.
    - >-
      The snake-eyed lizard (Ophisops elegans) is smaller than the striped
      lizard and mainly occurs in open, dry places in the southeast. Its eyelids
      are fused, so the eye can look permanently open.

      Rock lizards (Darevskia) are usually smaller and are often found on rocks
      and walls. They do not have the five-stripe pattern typical of young
      striped lizards. None of these lizards is venomous.
