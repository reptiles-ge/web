commonName: Eastern steppe viper
location: Kakheti and Shida Kartli
description: >-
  The Eastern steppe viper is a small venomous steppe snake, and it is rare in Georgia. It lives in open steppe and foothills; here it has been seen in Kakheti and Shida Kartli.
overview: >-
  The eastern steppe viper (Vipera renardi) is a small venomous snake that is rare in Georgia. It ranges from south-eastern Europe to Central Asia, and in the South Caucasus it lives in Armenia, Azerbaijan and Georgia. The atlas map marks Kakheti and Shida Kartli: one record is enough to mark a region, and the Shida Kartli points are approximate because public coordinates are hidden. In Kakheti it is known from the Shiraki Plateau and the area around Dedoplistskaro, and the snakes there are assigned to the Shemakha group, kakhetiensis. There are no records of the eastern steppe viper from Samtskhe-Javakheti, while vipers of the Javakheti and Erusheti plateaus are often treated separately, under the name V. r. eriwanensis. It is about 40–60 cm long, has a dark zigzag running along its back and prefers open steppe. The European regional assessment lists it as “Vulnerable” because its habitat is being lost. It is also known as Renard's viper.
habitat: >-
  It prefers open steppe, meadows, scrub, and rocky foothills — often
  well-drained slopes and semi-arid zones. In Georgia the regions with records are
  Kakheti and Shida Kartli. Kakheti records include Dedoplistskaro Municipality.
  The Shiraki Plateau, Dedoplistskaro, the area near Khornabuji, and the Artsivis
  Kheoba foothills are published localities in Kakheti. Vertically it occurs
  from roughly 0–2,500 m; eastern Georgian populations are mostly at low to mid
  elevations.
diet: >-
  The Eastern steppe viper often feeds on grasshoppers and related insects. It also eats lizards and small rodents. Young snakes hunt smaller invertebrates and lizards. It waits for prey in ambush and subdues it with venom.
behavior: >-
  Active in the warm season — in Georgia and Azerbaijan roughly from April to
  early October. In spring and autumn it is often diurnal; in summer heat it
  seeks shade and scrub. It hibernates in crevices and under stones. It is
  ovoviviparous: pregnancy lasts about three months; young are born in July–
  August (sometimes up to 10–17 individuals).
conservation: >-
  In the European regional assessment, the Eastern steppe viper is listed as “Vulnerable”. Over the last three generations it has lost more than 30% of its habitat, and what remains keeps breaking up into small, isolated patches. The main threat is the conversion of steppe and meadows into intensive farmland, along with overgrazing by livestock. It is rare in Georgia, and in the east of the country it was confirmed again after a 75-year gap. It keeps insect and rodent numbers in check, which makes it useful to nature.

faq:
  - question: How dangerous is an eastern steppe viper's bite?
    answer: >-
      Yes, it is venomous, though its danger is rated as moderate. It is less dangerous than the Levantine viper or the nose-horned viper. It attacks mainly when it feels threatened. Even so, if you are bitten, call 112 right away and get medical help.
  - question: Where is it found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The atlas map marks Kakheti and Shida Kartli. In Kakheti, it has been found on the Shiraki Plateau and around Dedoplistskaro and Khornabuji. There are no records of it from Samtskhe-Javakheti.
  - question: How does it differ from the Levantine and nose-horned vipers?
    answer: >-
      By size, by where they live and by the snout. The Eastern steppe viper is smaller, about 40–60 cm, and prefers open steppe. The Levantine viper is a large snake that lays eggs and lives in the dry eastern lowlands. The nose-horned viper has a horn-like growth on its snout and is linked to the highlands of the Lesser Caucasus.
  - question: Does it give live birth or lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      The Eastern steppe viper does not lay eggs. It gives birth to live young. Pregnancy lasts about three months, and the young are born in July–August.
  - question: Why is it Vulnerable?
    answer: >-
      In the European regional assessment, the Eastern steppe viper is listed as “Vulnerable”. Intensive development of steppe and meadows, along with overgrazing, is destroying the places where it lives. Its range is breaking up into separate patches, cut off from one another. In Georgia the Eastern steppe viper is rare, and its populations are small.
  - question: How do I identify it?
    answer: >-
      The Eastern steppe viper is a small, venomous steppe snake. Three clues help you recognise it: its small size, the zigzag along its back and the open steppe where it lives. In Georgia, this snake is rare.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
