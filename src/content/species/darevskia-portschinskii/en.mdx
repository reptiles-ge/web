commonName: "Portschinsky's Lizard"
location: "Central Lesser Caucasus — Kojori; hybrid zone in the Tana valley"
description: >-
  Portschinsky's Lizard is a non-venomous rock lizard confirmed in Georgia. It lives in the mountain forest of Trialeti but is not found across the whole country. You cannot identify it by colour alone.
overview: >-
  Portschinsky's lizard (Darevskia portschinskii) is one of the parent species of Dahl's rock lizard (D. dahli). Dahl's rock lizard reproduces without males, while Portschinsky's lizard has both males and females in the wild. It is a rock lizard with no venom and is not dangerous to people. Adults from Georgia usually measure about 5.4–5.8 cm from the head to the base of the tail. It lives only in the central part of the Lesser Caucasus, including Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan. The species was described in 1878, and Tbilisi, not Armenia, was given as the place the specimen came from. That does not mean it lives all over Georgia. Recent records name Kojori, and in the Tana Valley there is a spot where Portschinsky's lizard interbreeds with the obscure rock lizard (D. obscura). A 2026 Georgian checklist includes it as a confirmed species, not just a possible one. Its risk of extinction is low, and it is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List. It belongs to the true lizard family (Lacertidae), and in English it is called the Kura lizard or Portschinsky's lizard.
habitat: |-
  Portschinsky’s Lizard lives in rocky parts of mountain forests, on riverside cliffs and on dry, shrubby slopes. It does not occur in the damp forests of Colchis or in bare desert. It occurs in Georgia, and the DNA of a specimen collected in Kojori has also been studied. A 2026 list names the central Lesser Caucasus as its range, but this information is considered only moderately reliable. Do not assume it also lives in the damp forests of western Georgia, in Colchis, Adjara or the high mountains of Javakheti.

  It also lives alongside Dahl’s Rock Lizard (dahli). In 2008–2009, 82 sites where Dahl’s Rock Lizard occurred were surveyed. Portschinsky’s Lizard was found at 28 of them, mostly in the mountain forest of Trialeti. Their shared sites are often at about 800–1200 m above sea level. This is only the elevation of the shared sites, not the full elevation limits of its range in Georgia. For the species as a whole, 300–1700 m is known, but that figure also covers Armenia and Azerbaijan.

  Near Tbilisi, both lizards have been recorded at one rocky site since the 1960s. The site covers about 1 hectare and lies 1003–1042 m above sea level. Dahl’s Rock Lizard was about five times more numerous there. On the same rock, Dahl’s Rock Lizard stays closer to a water source. Portschinsky’s Lizard is spread more evenly and is more common even in places less suitable for it.

  The subspecies D. p. nigrita stands out with its darker back. It was described in 1976 from the Dmanisi area (Kvemo Kartli) and occurs in southeastern Georgia and northern Armenia. According to an old 2009 assessment, Gori is the western edge of the species’ range. The same assessment mentions a group cut off from the others on the middle Iori River. If the lizard has been seen somewhere in a region, that does not mean it is equally common across the whole region.
diet: >-
  Rock lizards usually catch small animals without a backbone: insects, spider-like animals and others with jointed legs. According to a 2010 study, Portschinsky's Lizard competes with Dahl's Rock Lizard for food and shelter. The same study did not note any major difference between their diets.
behavior: |-
  This rock lizard is active by day. It warms itself on stones and shelters in crevices. At one site near Tbilisi, it was observed from April to October, during its active periods in spring and autumn.

  It lays eggs, usually once a season. Earlier accounts give 2–5 eggs at a time, while lizards studied in Kojori had 1–3. Dahl's Rock Lizard has no males and reproduces without them. Males of Portschinsky's Lizard have been reported sometimes biting such lizards during mating. This does not give rise to a new group in which males and females breed together. If caught, it may lose its tail or bite you. Watch it, leave it where it is and do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  A 2009 assessment found that, worldwide, Portschinsky's Lizard faces a low risk of extinction because it has a wide range. The same assessment, however, says its numbers are declining. This assessment is old and also contains a mistake: it says the lizard reproduces without males, but that actually applies to Dahl's Rock Lizard. Portschinsky's Lizard is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List, while Dahl's Rock Lizard is listed there as Vulnerable. Georgia's 2026 checklist treats it as a confirmed species. In one small area, Portschinsky's Lizard meets the Obscure Rock Lizard and Valentin's Lizard. Genes have been described passing between them there. This makes the species harder to tell apart, but it is not a threat category. It is not on the CITES list, which covers international trade in wild animals. Do not catch wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and poses no health risk. If you pick it up, it may bite you, or its tail may break off. So leave it where it is and don't keep it: it is a wild animal.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small rock lizard that lives in the mountain forest of Trialeti. In the wild, Darevskia rock lizards are told apart by size, where they live, sex and the features of their scales. You cannot identify it by the colour of its back alone.
  traits:
    - >-
      It is a small lizard with a flattened body and well-developed legs. It lives on rocks and stones. It is neither a large ground-dwelling Lacerta lizard nor a Caucasian Agama (Paralaudakia). In lizards found in Georgia, head-and-body length is usually about 5.4–5.8 cm. The largest length in published data is about 67 mm. The Obscure Rock Lizard and Valentin's Lizard are considerably larger, so size helps you tell them apart. Its tail is longer than its body and breaks off easily. Do not pick the lizard up to count its scales.
    - >-
      Its back is gray, brownish or olive-colored, often with dark spots. Its belly may be yellowish. Descriptions say that the nigrita subspecies has a darker back. Other rock lizards have similar colors, so color alone may not be used to tell this species apart.
    - >-
      Dahl's Rock Lizard (D. dahli) is often found on the same rock, and in Georgia it is also similar in size to Portschinsky's Lizard. Portschinsky's Lizard has both males and females, while almost all Dahl's Rock Lizards are female. The two are reliably told apart by the scales on the head and by features around the anus. In Kojori, both lizards have also been identified by DNA: they are different species. Colour, however, will not help you tell them apart.
    - >-
      The Obscure Rock Lizard (D. obscura) is noticeably larger. The list names it as one of the possible species of the rudis group. Valentin's Lizard (D. valentini) is also larger and lives in the Javakheti highlands, for example near Lake Saghamo. The Mixed Rock Lizard (D. mixta) lives only in western Georgia. It is the second parent species of Dahl's Rock Lizard (D. dahli), and it is almost never found in the same places as Portschinsky's Lizard. In the Tana Valley, lizards of different species interbreed. So where these species meet there, do not tell them apart by colour alone.
    - >-
      Portschinsky's Lizard has both males and females. This sets it apart from Dahl's Rock Lizard (D. dahli), which reproduces without males. The 2026 list recognizes it as a confirmed species, not merely a possible one. When the species was described, Tbilisi was given as the specimen's locality, and recent records name Kojori. This does not mean it occurs throughout Georgia: it lives only in the central Lesser Caucasus, including Armenia and Azerbaijan.

faq:
  - question: Is the Kura lizard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 list confirms that it occurs in Georgia. When the species was described, Tbilisi was given as the place the specimen came from, while more recent records name Kojori. It is not found throughout the country, though, so don't expect to see it everywhere.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It has been found around Trialeti, in a rocky spot in mountain forest, at about 800–1,200 m above sea level. There it has also been seen together with Dahl's Rock Lizard. Recent reports also name Kojori. In the Tana Valley, its range meets that of the Obscure Rock Lizard (D. obscura). Do not look for it in Abkhazia, Adjara or the Colchis Lowland.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and poses no risk to human health. Its tail breaks off easily, though, so do not pick it up.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      You cannot identify Portschinsky's Lizard by colour alone. It is a small rock lizard, and it has both males and females. Among Dahl's Rock Lizards living on the same rock, by contrast, almost all are female. The Obscure Rock Lizard (D. obscura) and Valentin's Lizard (D. valentini) are much larger and live in other areas. The exception is the area around Tana, where their ranges border that of Portschinsky's Lizard.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adults found in Georgia have a body length of about 5.4–5.8 cm, measured from the head to the base of the tail. Published records give the longest body as about 67 mm. With the tail the lizard is longer, but the tail breaks off easily. So total length is not a reliable guide to size. To judge how big a lizard is, go by its body length, without the tail.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Rock lizards mainly catch small animals without a backbone. Portschinsky's Lizard is not venomous.
  - question: When is it active and how does it breed?
    answer: >-
      It is active during the day. In a study near Tbilisi, it was observed from April to October. It lays eggs, usually once per season. In Kojori, 1–3 eggs were recorded, while earlier descriptions give 2–5.
  - question: Is this D. dahli, D. obscura, or D. valentini?
    answer: >-
      No, Portschinsky's Lizard is a separate, confirmed species. Dahl's Rock Lizard (D. dahli) is often found with it on the same rock, and it reproduces without males. The Obscure Rock Lizard (D. obscura) and Valentin's Lizard (D. valentini) are larger. In the Tana Valley, there is a place where Portschinsky's Lizard interbreeds with the Obscure Rock Lizard. You cannot tell these lizards apart by colour.
