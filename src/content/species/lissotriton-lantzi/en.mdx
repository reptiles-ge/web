description: >-
  The Caucasian Smooth Newt is the smallest newt in Georgia. It is non-venomous, lives in ponds and lakes of the forest belt and is listed as a candidate species in the 2026 checklist.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian Smooth Newt (Lissotriton lantzi) is Georgia's smallest newt. It lives in ponds and lakes of the forest belt, has no venom and is not dangerous to people. Adults are usually about 7–11 cm long, and breeding males may reach about 12 cm. In the wild you can recognise it by its small size and smooth skin, so don't mistake it for banded or crested newts. The 2026 Georgian checklist lists it as a candidate species, which means it is not yet fully confirmed as a separate species. Within the group of close relatives of the smooth newt (Lissotriton vulgaris), it is a geographically isolated lineage. Older books called this newt Triturus vulgaris lantzi, and it was first described in 1914 from specimens obtained from Novorossiysk, Poti and Borjomi.
habitat: |-
  In Georgia this newt lives in lakes and ponds of the forest belt. It occurs in ponds with no fish or few fish, small lakes, ditches and slow water, often near deciduous or mixed woodland. On land it hides under stones, logs and fallen leaves.
  In the 2026 checklist, the DNA-confirmed specimen comes from Tbilisi, from Mtskemsi Lake (Tabaruki). DNA data from Bakuriani are also linked to this newt. This does not mean it lives only around Tbilisi.
  By 1996, 17 sites were known in Georgia, including Batumi, Sukhumi, Gagra, Pitsunda, Kutaisi and Bakuriani. The list also included Poti, Mukhuri, the Sakochavi lakes, Didi Mitarbi, the village of Tba, Kobuleti, Lagodekhi and Sataplia. Chiantba near Telavi, Satovle near Tbilisi and Ertatsminda on Trialeti were on it too. Even then, the newt was considered lost from some places in eastern Georgia, such as around Kvareli. By 2014, 42 sites were known in Georgia, stretching from the Colchis lowland through Imereti, Borjomi–Bakuriani, Trialeti and Manglisi to Kakheti. The atlas map marks Abkhazia, Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Shida Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti and Tbilisi.
  In Georgia it ranges from sea level to about 1,800 m. At higher elevations it has been seen at Bakuriani (about 1,760 m) and Kakhisi Lake. In the Russian Caucasus it is known from even higher sites, and its range continues into the North Caucasus. Armenia, Azerbaijan and north-eastern Turkey have old records. Reliable confirmation there in recent decades is scarce, and some populations are considered extinct.
diet: >-
  Adult newts feed on small invertebrates. In water they catch insect larvae, crustaceans and worms, while on land they eat invertebrates living in the soil and among fallen leaves. In captivity they are often fed chironomid larvae.
behavior: |-
  It stays in water while breeding and is often on land the rest of the year. It is described as more closely tied to water than common newts. In the north-western Caucasus it pairs up from late winter to early summer, often peaking in February–April. Higher up, this period comes later.
  Eggs are laid one by one on the leaves of water plants. Larvae grow in water and move onto land once they take on the adult form. In some populations, adult newts have been described that keep larval features, including gills.
  The skin releases a defensive substance, but it is not a medical risk to people. Do not pick it up or move it from one pond to another, because this easily spreads disease. If you do touch one, wash your hands. If you come across a newt, simply watch it and leave it where it is.
conservation: |-
  The Caucasian Smooth Newt is not on Georgia's Red List, although a 2014 review recommended adding it. It is, however, listed in the Red Data Book of Russia. It has no separate global assessment. L. vulgaris has been assessed and is considered at low risk of extinction. The main threats are the destruction or filling-in of ponds, releasing fish into ponds and lakes, deforestation and the terrarium trade. A local decline in eastern Georgia was already described in the 1990s.
  The newt occurs in Georgia, but it is not yet fully agreed whether it is a separate species within the L. vulgaris group. Some authors treat it as a full species, for example in a 2018 paper. Papers from 2020 and 2025 treat it as a subspecies, and for others lantzi is simply another name for L. vulgaris. For this reason, the 2026 Georgian checklist keeps L. lantzi as a candidate species.
interaction: |-
  The Caucasian Smooth Newt (Lissotriton lantzi) is not venomous to people and usually poses no danger. When someone approaches, it tries to escape or hide. In Georgia this newt is confirmed around Tbilisi, including at Lake Mtskemsi (Tabaruki), and it is also associated with Bakuriani. The 2026 Georgian checklist lists it as a candidate species: an isolated, separately evolved lineage within the L. vulgaris group.
  If you come across a newt at a forest pool or a small pond, it is best simply to watch it and leave it where it is. Picking it up is not a good idea: amphibian skin is very sensitive, and substances on human hands may harm the animal. If you do touch a Caucasian Smooth Newt, wash your hands well with soap and water. Until you have washed them, do not touch your eyes, mouth or face. Accidental contact is usually not a medical danger to people. Do not take the newt home or move it from one pond to another. It is especially important that moving amphibians between ponds does not spread diseases and the germs that cause them. The best way to protect a wild newt is to leave it in nature, where it belongs.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Georgia’s smallest newt, with smooth or finely granular skin. It has no pale flank stripe, and in the breeding season the male develops a low crest. To avoid confusing it with a banded or crested newt, look at the flank, the skin and the male’s crest.
  traits:
    - >-
      Adults are usually about 7–11 cm long, tail included. Breeding males have been described at up to about 12 cm. The body is slim and elongated, and in water the tail is flattened from side to side and about as long as the body. The skin is smooth or finely granular, while crested newts have coarse skin.
    - >-
      The back is olive, brownish or yellowish, with small dark specks or spots. The belly is yellow or orange. Males have large dark spots on the belly, females very small ones. There is no silvery stripe along the flank. That is the main difference from the Northern Banded Newt (Ommatotriton ophryticus), so look at the flank first.
    - >-
      In the breeding season the male grows a low crest along its back, not a tall comb. The crest is wavy or finely notched. The tail ends in a thin filament up to about 7 mm long. The toes have fringes of skin. The female has no such crest, is plainer in colour and has finer spots.
    - >-
      The Northern Banded Newt is larger and has a pale stripe along its flank. Its belly is unspotted, and the male's crest is very high and notched. The Eastern Crested Newt (Triturus karelinii) is also larger, but its skin is coarse. It has big dark spots on its belly, and the male has a high, notched crest. The Caucasian Salamander (Mertensiella caucasica) has yellow spots and a round, not flattened, tail. It lives along streams, not in ponds. The Caucasian Smooth Newt, by contrast, has neither a pale flank stripe nor a high crest.
    - >-
      The European smooth newt (L. vulgaris) does not occur in Georgia. In the wild, the Caucasian population looks like it. The reliable difference between them is more a matter of geography than of any single obvious feature. So the best clue for telling them apart is where you saw the newt.
