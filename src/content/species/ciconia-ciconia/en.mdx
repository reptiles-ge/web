commonName: White stork
location: Breeding migrant in Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli
description: >-
  A large white bird with black flight feathers and a red bill and legs.
  Confirmed in Georgia as a breeding migrant on the 2024 national bird
  checklist; nesting is limited to southern highland settlements.
overview: >-
  The white stork (Ciconia ciconia) is a large white bird with black flight
  feathers, confirmed in Georgia as a breeding migrant. The 2024 annotated
  checklist of the birds of Georgia codes it BB; M: a breeder that is absent in
  the non-breeding season, and also a bird of passage. Seasonality is classed as
  high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality is high (H), with photographs. The
  checklist lists the subspecies as C. c. ciconia and uses the English name
  Western White Stork. The breeding range in Georgia is narrow: Samtskhe–Javakheti
  and Kvemo Kartli in the 2024 census. It is not a threat to people. IUCN lists
  the species as Least Concern. Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution
  No. 190) still lists it as ლაკლაკი, category VU, criterion D1.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in the breeding season in settlements and nearby open
  wet ground, not in winter. The 2024 census (Budagashvili, Shoshitashvili &
  Uguzashvili) restricts breeding to Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli:
  mountain grasslands, subalpine lakes and wetlands close to villages. Every
  counted nest was inside a settlement — rooftops, pylons or similar supports,
  not remote forest. Fifty-nine settlements were surveyed; 43 had breeding
  pairs. Those two administrative regions are coloured on the atlas map from
  that paper. Other regions are not coloured here: birds on passage may appear
  more widely, but this page has no compiled list of passage localities. The
  full settlement table is in the paper’s supplement and is not copied here. A
  separate national elevation band in metres is not published on this page.
  Breeding in the Colchis lowlands or on the northern Greater Caucasus slope is
  not supported by these sources.
diet: >-
  There is no separate measured Georgian diet study on this page. At species
  level it forages in open, often wet ground: large invertebrates, amphibians,
  reptiles, small mammals, fish, and other prey it can catch. It probes soil or
  the water’s edge with the bill. That is the general trophic niche, not a
  weighed Georgian menu.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. It often soars in pairs or groups with the neck
  outstretched, not pulled into an S like a heron.   At the nest the main sound
  is bill-clattering, not a song. No Georgian recording is on this page. The
  clip is xeno-canto XC1103936 (Petter Westberg, Fulltofta, Hörby, Skåne,
  Sweden). It builds its own nest: a large stick pile on
  a village support, often with several pairs in one settlement. In the 2024
  census most of the 202 occupied nests held two or three nestlings; four
  nestlings were uncommon. Clutch size, incubation length and fledging age have
  not been measured separately for Georgia on this page. Species-level
  handbooks often give about three eggs, about a month of incubation and about
  two months to fledge — that is not a Georgian series. The 2024 checklist
  treats the species as a breeder and a migrant in Georgia, not as a winter
  visitor. Wintering sites of the local population are not mapped by satellite
  tracking on this page. At species level, western Palearctic birds mainly
  winter in Africa; that is not a confirmed route for Georgian birds. Exact
  arrival and departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated here.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Ciconia ciconia as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List (2025 assessment). The global range and population are large. In
  Georgia two assessments disagree and both should be read. The 2014 Red List
  (Resolution No. 190) still lists the species as ლაკლაკი, VU, criterion D1
  (small population) — that is the legal list among the sources used here. The
  2024 ornithological checklist assigns global and national category LC. The
  2024 census recorded 202 occupied nests, compared with 105 in 2018–2020; a
  40% increase in the 23 settlements counted in both periods was not
  statistically significant. Older figures are 74 occupied nests in 1984, about
  60 pairs in 1994–1995, and 104 in 2013–2014. The census paper flags future
  wind-energy development in Samtskhe–Javakheti as a risk to large soaring
  birds. Collision and electrocution on power lines are well known at species
  level; a separate Georgian casualty total is not given here. The EU Birds
  Directive does not apply in Georgia. Do not disturb wild birds or nests.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people and is not venomous. In Georgia it nests in
  villages, so close contact with people is normal. The bill is strong — do not
  approach, feed, or pick up nestlings. Moving a nest or taking it off a line
  is specialist work, not a casual intervention.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large white bird with black wing tips, a long red bill and red legs. In
    Georgia it is a breeding migrant on the 2024 checklist, nesting in two
    southern regions. The usual confusion is black stork and great white egret.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 100–115 cm at species level; wingspan is often given around
      155–215 cm. Mass is often about 2.3–4.5 kg. A separate measured series
      from Georgia is not given on this page. The body and neck are white; the
      flight feathers are black and obvious in the air. Adults have a red bill
      and red legs. Skin around the eye is dark. Females are on average a
      little smaller than males; sex is not reliably told by size in the field.
    - >-
      Juveniles have a dark or brownish-black bill, not clean red; the legs are
      also darker. That is the most useful age mark at close range. Flight
      silhouette: long outstretched neck, long legs trailing, broad wings with
      a black trailing edge. A heron tucks the neck in flight.
    - >-
      Black stork (Ciconia nigra) is also a breeding migrant on the 2024
      checklist (BB; M); the checklist codes it NT nationally, while the 2014
      Red List codes it VU under the Georgian name ყარყატი. The back, neck and
      wings are dark, the belly white; it nests in forest and river valleys,
      not on village roofs. Great white egret (Ardea alba) is all white, without
      black flight feathers, with a yellow bill and an S-shaped neck. Great
      white and Dalmatian pelicans are much larger, with a pouched bill.
      Oriental stork (Ciconia boyciana) is not on the 2024 checklist. This atlas
      does not yet have a separate black stork page.
    - >-
      Georgian names clash. The 2014 list calls C. ciconia ლაკლაკი and C. nigra
      ყარყატი. This page uses თეთრი ყარყატი as the main name because it states
      the colour; ლაკლაკი is the official alternative. Bare „ყარყატი“ in legal
      text means the black species. It is not a threat to people. A white
      black-winged bird with a red bill on a village pole is this species.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758, originally as Ardea ciconia. The 2024
    Georgian checklist follows the IOC World Bird List and the OSME Region List
    for names: order Ciconiiformes, family Ciconiidae, genus Ciconia. It is
    entered as Ciconia ciconia ciconia.
  - >-
    Widely recognised subspecies are C. c. ciconia (Europe, north-west Africa,
    western Asia) and C. c. asiatica (Central Asia). The nominate form is the
    one the 2024 checklist assigns to Georgia. Field identification is to
    species.
  - >-
    Georgian is თეთრი ყარყატი; the official 2014 Red List name is ლაკლაკი.
    English is white stork; the 2024 checklist uses Western White Stork to
    separate it from Ciconia boyciana.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: BB; M, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    status. The breeding regional map on this page rests on the same year’s
    separate census, not on a checklist gazetteer. Black stork is listed
    separately as BB; M.
faq:
  - question: Is the white stork found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed breeding bird and migrant (BB; M). It is absent in the
      non-breeding season.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      When breeding — in settlements of Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli,
      near open wet ground. The 2024 census counted 202 occupied nests in 43
      settlements. Other regions are not coloured on this page. Wider
      appearance on passage is possible, but there is no passage map here.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Large white body, black flight feathers, long red bill and legs. The neck
      is stretched out in flight. A black stork has a dark back. A great white
      egret lacks black wing tips and tucks the neck in flight.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 100–115 cm long at species level, with a wingspan often given
      around 155–215 cm. A separate measured series from Georgia is not given
      on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Prey it can catch in open, often wet ground: invertebrates, amphibians,
      and small vertebrates. Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia on
      this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      As a breeding migrant: present in the breeding season and on passage,
      absent in winter, according to the 2024 checklist. Exact arrival and
      departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated on this page.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. BB on the 2024 checklist means it is a confirmed breeder. The 2024
      census found breeding only in Samtskhe–Javakheti and Kvemo Kartli, on
      supports in settlements.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. It is globally Least Concern. The 2014 Red List lists it
      as VU (ლაკლაკი). The 2024 checklist codes it LC nationally. Do not
      disturb wild birds or nests.
