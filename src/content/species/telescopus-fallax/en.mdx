overview: |-
  The cat snake (Telescopus fallax) is a slender snake that is active mainly at night. In Georgia, it is found mostly in dry, rocky places in the east. It belongs to the Colubridae family. A 2026 Georgian checklist confirms the species’ presence, though the accuracy of information about its range is rated as moderate.
  According to a 2021 Georgian study, the cat snake is found in rocky or grassy places with a dry climate. This page does not provide a specific record of the snake from western Georgia or Colchis.
  The “Records by Region” table on this page’s map shows records in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti and Tbilisi. For Shida Kartli, only isolated sightings have been recorded so far; these are too few to shade the region on the map.
  Cat snakes living in the Caucasus are often assigned to the subspecies T. f. iberus. Tbilisi (“Tiflis”) is listed as the location of the specimen on which the description of this subspecies is based. The cat snake has venom fangs at the back of its mouth and uses its venom to subdue prey. It is not considered a significant threat to human health, but there is no need to pick it up.
  It is usually about 60–100 cm long. According to the IUCN, the species is of Least Concern.
habitat: |-
  In Georgia, the European cat snake lives mainly in dry, stony or grassy places. It may be found on rocky slopes, near crevices, in scrub, at open forest edges, in ruins and by stone walls. It climbs rocks and walls well. By day it often hides under stones, in crevices or under tree bark.
  The atlas map marks only the regions with enough records: Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti and Tbilisi. It has been photographed on Mtatsminda and on Mount Iagluja near Rustavi. There is no specific record of it from Samtskhe-Javakheti. Across the species' whole range, it has been seen at elevations of up to about 2,000 m; its elevation range in Georgia has not been established separately.
diet: |-
  Outside Georgia, the European cat snake hunts mainly lizards. It also catches small snakes, nestlings and small mammals. Around the Mediterranean it often eats geckos too, but it has not been confirmed that cat snakes living in Georgia eat them. It holds its prey with its body and subdues it with the venomous fangs at the back of its mouth.
behavior: |-
  The European cat snake hides by day and hunts in the evening and at night. Its large eyes and vertical pupils are linked to its activity in low light. It lays eggs, and reports about the species often mention 5–9 eggs. No separate data on its breeding or overwintering in Georgia have been published.
  When threatened, it usually tries to escape or hide. Sometimes it may curl up, breathe out loudly or bite in self-defence. So do not pick it up.
conservation: |-
  Worldwide, the European cat snake faces a low risk of extinction. Its presence in Georgia is confirmed in the 2026 Georgian checklist, but it is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. In Europe, it is listed in Appendix II of the Bern Convention and Annex IV of the EU Habitats Directive. However, EU laws do not automatically apply in Georgia.

  summary: >-
    The European cat snake has a slender body and large eyes, and it is active mainly at night. Its pupils are vertical, so you may mistake it for a viper. To identify it, don't rely on the pupils alone: look at its body shape, its smooth scales and the large scales on its head.
  traits:
    -  |-
      The European cat snake has large eyes with vertical pupils. In bright light, the pupils narrow to thin slits, like a cat's. The body is slender and almost cylindrical. The head is flat and oval and stands out from the neck, though not sharply.
      The scales on the back are smooth. Around the middle of the body, they are often arranged in about 19 rows. Large scales sit symmetrically on top of the head, while vipers often have smaller scales there. Do not pick up a snake to count its scales.
    -  |-
      The European cat snake is about 60–100 cm long. Its color varies: gray, sand colored, brownish or olive. Dark spots or bands run across its back and often become less distinct toward the rear of the body.
      A dark, slanting stripe runs from the eye toward the corner of the mouth. Many also have other dark markings on the head. The belly is pale, and some have small dark dots on it. In young snakes these markings are usually more distinct.
    -  |-
      The European cat snake has a slender body, large eyes and smooth back scales. The nose-horned viper (Vipera ammodytes transcaucasiana) and other vipers have thicker bodies and broader, triangular heads. A raised line runs down the middle of their back scales. The Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetinus) is an even thicker venomous snake, and its bite poses a serious risk to human health. The smooth snake (Coronella austriaca), steppe ratsnake (Elaphe dione) and Dahl's whip snake (Platyceps najadum) have round pupils. When identifying an unfamiliar snake, do not rely on just one feature.

facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1831 (Fleischmann), originally as Tarbophis fallax, from the
    eastern Adriatic. The Caucasian form iberus (Eichwald, 1831) has its type
    locality at Tbilisi (“Tiflis”) — iberus refers to ancient Caucasian Iberia,
    not the Iberian Peninsula. The 2026 checklist uses Telescopus fallax at
    species rank.
  - >-
    A vertical pupil is unusual among colubrids. Georgian vipers also have
    vertical pupils — the eye alone is not enough for identification.
  - >-
    A 2019 phylogenetic study notes that the T. fallax group needs revision;
    in practice Georgia is still treated as Telescopus fallax.
  - >-
    On the map’s records-by-region table, it is recorded in Kakheti,
    Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, and Tbilisi; Shida Kartli currently shows
    only individual records. Named photographs are from Mtatsminda and Iagluji
    Mountain near Rustavi.

  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: |-
