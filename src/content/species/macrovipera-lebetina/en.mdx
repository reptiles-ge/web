commonName: Levantine viper
location: Eastern Georgia
description: >-
  Eastern Georgia’s high-risk venomous snake — Levantine viper; do not confuse
  with Montpellier snake.
overview: >-
  The Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetinus) is a large venomous viper found
  in Georgia and is considered one of the country’s most medically significant
  snakes. It is mainly associated with dry and semi-arid habitats. In Georgia
  its range is no longer treated as limited only to the extreme south-east: in
  recent decades the species has also been confirmed in the Alazani valley and
  in Tbilisi. A natural range in western Georgia and in high mountain zones is
  not known. The accepted modern scientific name is Macrovipera lebetinus. Older
  literature uses names such as Vipera lebetina and Macrovipera lebetina, while
  Caucasian populations are usually assigned to M. l. obtusa. The species is
  confirmed in Georgia, including by genetic data. An adult Levantine viper is
  a large, heavy snake and commonly reaches about 1–1.5 m. It can sometimes be
  confused with the Montpellier snake and other large harmless snakes, so colour
  or size alone is not reliable for identification. A Levantine viper bite is a
  serious medical emergency. Do not approach the snake and do not try to catch
  or kill it. If bitten, call 112 immediately.
habitat: >-
  The Levantine viper is found mainly in dry, stony and rocky places. It is
  characteristic of arid zones along the lower Iori, Mtkvari and Alazani, in
  Shiraki, Gareji and the dry foothills of Kvemo Kartli. It is rare around
  Tbilisi and Rustavi. On this atlas map it is marked in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli
  and Tbilisi. Confirmed localities include the area around Kvareli and Nekresi
  Monastery, the Alazani valley and the Colored Desert. It shelters in rock
  crevices, spaces under stones and rodent burrows; it sometimes enters
  buildings. During strong summer heat it may also appear near water. Georgian
  sources give an upper range limit of about 1,500 m. No confirmed natural range
  is known in western Georgia, and the Greater Caucasus high mountains and
  Colchic forest are not its typical habitat.
diet: >-
  Adult Levantine vipers feed mainly on rodents, but may also take small birds,
  lizards and other small vertebrates. Young individuals more often hunt small
  lizards. The species mostly hunts by ambush. It sometimes climbs shrubs or
  low trees, including to catch birds.
behavior: >-
  In Transcaucasia the Levantine viper’s active season usually lasts from April
  to late October, though some sources describe emergence from late March. In
  spring and autumn it often moves by day, while in summer heat it is more
  active at dusk and at night. It spends winter in rock crevices or burrows. The
  Levantine viper lays eggs, which distinguishes it from Georgia’s other vipers.
  Mating mainly takes place in April–May. Georgian sources describe about 15–20
  eggs in summer. Hatchlings are about 23–25 cm long and emerge after roughly
  35–45 days. If you encounter a Levantine viper, keep a safe distance and do
  not try to catch it. The snake does not seek out people and usually defends
  itself only when it feels threatened.
conservation: >-
  The 2021 IUCN assessment treats the Levantine viper as Least Concern. It is
  not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List; among vipers, that list includes
  Dinnik’s viper and the Caucasus viper. Older Georgian encyclopedic sources
  sometimes link the Levantine viper with the Red Book, but that does not match
  the official 2014 list. Important threats include habitat conversion,
  urbanisation, overgrazing and deliberate killing by people. By hunting rodents,
  the Levantine viper plays an important role in the food webs of dry landscapes.

  summary: >-
    The Levantine viper is a heavy-bodied viper with a broad triangular head,
    a vertical pupil and keeled scales. In the dry lowlands of eastern Georgia
    it is sometimes confused with large harmless racers and the Montpellier snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is thick and heavy. The head is broad and triangular, clearly
      set off from the neck; the snout is blunt and has no horn. In the
      Caucasus, adult Levantine vipers commonly reach about 1–1.5 m. Reports of
      lengths up to 2 m exist, but such records are debated.
    - >-
      The pupil is a vertical slit. The dorsal scales have a distinct raised
      line down the middle, giving the surface a rough appearance. The head is
      also mostly covered with small scales, including above the eyes.
    - >-
      Colour may be grey, sandy or brownish. The back often has dark blotches
      or cross-bars, sometimes forming a zigzag-like pattern. The belly is paler
      and often covered with fine dark speckles. More uniform individuals also
      occur.
    - >-
      The Montpellier snake (Malpolon insignitus) has a round pupil and a
      marked “eyebrow” above the eye. It is slimmer than the Levantine viper
      and is mainly active by day. Large racers (Dolichophis) also have a round
      pupil, a more elongated body and fast movement — not the thick, heavy
      build of a Levantine viper.
    - >-
      The steppe viper also occurs in eastern Georgia, but is much smaller than
      the Levantine viper — usually about 40–60 cm long. The Transcaucasian
      long-nosed viper is easy to distinguish by its nose horn and is linked to
      more mountainous environments. Dinnik’s viper lives in the Greater
      Caucasus highlands and does not occur in the Levantine viper’s typical
      habitat.
faq:
  - question: Is the Levantine viper found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 Georgian checklist lists the Levantine viper as a
      biologically confirmed species in Georgia. Its natural range mainly covers
      the dry and semi-arid zones of eastern Georgia — it is not found
      throughout the whole country.
  - question: Are “gyurza”, “giurza” and გიუზა the same snake?
    answer: >-
      Yes — those spellings refer to the same species. In this atlas it is
      Macrovipera lebetinus (Levantine viper / გიურზა).
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, the Levantine viper is found mainly along the lower Iori,
      Mtkvari and Alazani, in Shiraki, Gareji and the dry foothills of Kvemo
      Kartli. It is rare around Tbilisi and Rustavi. On this atlas map the
      species is marked in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. Specific places
      include Kvareli, the area around Nekresi Monastery and the Colored Desert.
      A natural range in western Georgia is not confirmed.
  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      The Levantine viper is venomous and represents one of the highest medical
      risks among snakes found in Georgia. It is large and may inject a
      significant amount of venom when it bites. Other vipers are also
      venomous, but a Levantine viper bite is considered especially serious.
      It normally does not attack people and bites when it feels threatened,
      for example if someone comes too close, steps on it or tries to catch it.
      A bite requires immediate medical help and a call to 112.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
      If bitten by a Levantine viper, call 112 immediately. Lay the person down,
      keep them calm and try to limit movement. Do not cut the bite site, do
      not try to suck out venom and do not apply a tight tourniquet. Treatment
      must take place in a hospital. Antivenom, when needed, must be selected
      and used only by a doctor.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      The Levantine viper is a large, thick-bodied snake with a broad
      triangular head, a vertical pupil and keeled scales. It has no nose horn.
      Large racers and the Montpellier snake have a round pupil and a slimmer,
      more elongated body.
  - question: Is the Montpellier snake the same as the Levantine viper?
    answer: >-
      No. The Montpellier snake (Malpolon insignitus) is not the Levantine
      viper. It is a rear-fanged species and is placed in the Moderate risk
      category in this atlas. The Levantine viper has a broad triangular head,
      a vertical pupil and a heavy body. The Montpellier snake is slimmer, with
      a round pupil and a marked “eyebrow” above the eye. Compare both species
      profiles for reliable separation. For any suspicious snakebite, call 112
      immediately.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      In the Caucasus, adult Levantine vipers commonly reach about 1–1.5 m. It
      is Georgia’s largest viper. Reports of lengths up to 2 m exist, but such
      data are debated.
  - question: Does it lay eggs or give live birth?
    answer: >-
      The Levantine viper lays eggs, which distinguishes it from Georgia’s
      Vipera vipers — they give live birth. Mating mainly takes place in
      April–May, and Georgian sources describe about 15–20 eggs in summer.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Keep a safe distance, watch from afar and leave the snake where it is. Do
      not try to catch or kill it. If you give it space and do not disturb it,
      a Levantine viper will normally move away on its own.
