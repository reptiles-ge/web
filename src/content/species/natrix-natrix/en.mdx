commonName: Grass snake
location: Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti, Tbilisi
description: >-
  Non-venomous grass snake in Georgia — yellow collar; not a viper.
overview: >-
  The grass snake (Natrix natrix) is a nonvenomous snake found both in water and on land. Its best-known feature is a yellow, white or cream-colored “collar” behind its head, though this may be faint on darker snakes. In western Georgia, it is often mistaken for the Caucasian viper; near water, it is mistaken for the water snake (Natrix tessellata). Its presence is confirmed in Georgia’s 2026 species list. This page lists only the regions where records confirm its presence: Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti and Tbilisi. A single record of the species is enough to include a region in this list. Grass snakes living in Georgia belong to the subspecies Natrix natrix scutata. The striped form, formerly called N. n. persa, is now considered part of the same subspecies. The “big-headed grass snake” (Natrix megalocephala), once considered a separate species, is no longer treated as one. The grass snake is considered to be of Least Concern, and it is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  On this page, the species is listed only in regions where its presence is confirmed in the records table: Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti, and Tbilisi. A single record of the species is enough for a region to be included. It prefers marshes, floodplains, lakes, streams, damp forests, and riverside meadows. The 2026 list includes a specific record from eastern Georgia: the village of Tushurebi in Tianeti Municipality, Mtskheta — Mtianeti. Compared with the water snake, it more often chooses cool, shady places. In the Colchic forest, it has also been seen climbing trees, including to warm itself in the sun and to hunt tree frogs. It may also be found in gardens, parks, and agricultural areas if there are enough amphibians and damp places to shelter. The information used for this page does not establish the exact maximum elevation at which it occurs in Georgia.
diet: >-
  It feeds mainly on frogs and toads. It also catches fish, but relies on fish less than the water snake does. It does not strangle its prey; it swallows it alive. It hunts both in water and on land. It sometimes travels quite far from water in search of toads.
behavior: >-
  It is active during the warm season and moves around mainly by day. In very hot weather, it may also become active in the evening or at night. Its active season lasts longer in the western lowlands than in high mountain areas, but no single calendar for this season has been published for Georgia. It mates in spring. In summer, the female usually lays about 8–40 eggs in a warm, moist place, such as among rotting plants or in compost. The young hatch in late summer or early autumn. These details about breeding concern the species in general, not specifically those living in Georgia. When threatened, it may release a foul-smelling liquid from an opening under its tail, flatten its head or play dead. It does not chase people and usually avoids confrontation with them. Do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  According to the IUCN Red List, the grass snake is of Least Concern. It is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List. The species is also listed in Appendix III of the Bern Convention. In some places, it may be threatened by the loss of marshes and other wet areas, water pollution, deaths on roads, and people killing it after mistaking it for a venomous snake. It is common in some parts of the humid west.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a nonvenomous snake that spends time both in water and on land. The yellow or pale “collar” behind its head and its round pupil are the best clues for identifying it. In western Georgia, it may be mistaken for a Caucasian viper; near water, it may be mistaken for a water snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      Behind its head, it often has a yellow, white or cream-colored “collar”: two pale, crescent-shaped patches with a dark area behind them. This is one of the common grass snake’s most distinctive features. In very dark grass snakes, the collar may be faint or not visible at all.
    - >-
      Its back may be greenish, gray or brownish. Each scale on its back has a small raised line down the middle. Very dark, almost black grass snakes are also found in Colchis. Some grass snakes have two pale stripes running along their backs. These snakes were formerly known as “persa” and have also been recorded near Paliastomi. The pupil is round.
    - >-
      In Georgia, the common grass snake is a medium-sized snake, usually about 70–120 cm long. Females are larger than males. Grass snakes over 1.5 meters long are rarely found in other parts of Europe, but that length is not typical for them in Georgia. The head is only slightly distinct from the body and does not have the broad, sharply triangular shape characteristic of a viper.
    - >-
      The water snake (Natrix tessellata) has no pale “collar” behind its head. It often has spots arranged like a checkerboard on its back. It is more often found in open water or nearby and feeds mainly on fish. The common grass snake more often hunts frogs and other amphibians. It is also found in damp, shady places. In Georgia, the water snake may be easier to spot in semi-arid areas.
    - >-
      The Caucasian viper is venomous. It has a shorter, thicker body and vertical pupils. It lacks the pale “collar” typical of the common grass snake. The smooth snake has smooth scales and no collar. The glass lizard is not a snake: it has movable eyelids and ear openings. Do not pick up a snake you cannot identify.

faq:
  - question: Is the grass snake venomous?
    answer: >-
      No. The grass snake has no venom and is not dangerous to people. When
      threatened it may release a strong-smelling secretion, flatten its head, or
      feign death. Do not pick it up, especially in western Georgia, where from a
      distance it can be confused with the Caucasus viper.
  - question: How does it differ from the dice snake?
    answer: >-
      The grass snake often has a light-coloured “collar” behind its head. It is more often found in damp, shady places and feeds mainly on amphibians. The dice snake has no such “collar”. Its back often has markings like the squares on a chessboard. It is more closely tied to water and feeds mainly on fish.
  - question: How can I tell it from a viper?
    answer: >-
      Look for the yellow, white, or cream collar behind the head, a round pupil,
      and a relatively slender body. In Colchis the grass snake may be very dark,
      almost black. The Caucasus viper lacks the collar, has a shorter and
      heavier body, and has a vertical pupil. Do not rely on only one trait to
      identify an unknown snake.
  - question: Is the grass snake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The grass snake is a confirmed species in Georgia. The 2026 Georgian
      checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. Distribution on this page is
      Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe —
      Javakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti, and Tbilisi.
  - question: Where might I encounter it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      This species has been confirmed in Abkhazia, Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti, and Tbilisi. You may encounter it near marshes, lakes, streams, and rivers, and in damp forests.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It mainly eats frogs and toads. It also catches fish, though less often than the water snake. It swallows its prey alive.
  - question: Does it lay eggs or give live birth?
    answer: >-
      In general, this species mates in spring. In summer, the female usually lays about 8–40 eggs in a warm, damp place. The young hatch in late summer or early autumn. The sources used on this page do not say separately how many eggs females in Georgia lay.
  - question: What should I do if one comes into the yard?
    answer: >-
      Keep your distance from the snake and don’t pick it up. The common grass snake is not venomous and poses no danger to people. If you’re unsure what kind of snake it is, don’t approach it. Stay calm and let it move away on its own.
