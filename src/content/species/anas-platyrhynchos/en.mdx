commonName: Mallard
location: Year-round resident, migrant and winter visitor in Georgia
description: >-
  A dabbling duck of open water and wetlands. Present in Georgia all year: it
  breeds on the shallow highland lakes of the Javakheti Plateau and moves down
  to lowland wetlands and city ponds in winter. Harmless to people.
overview: >-
  The mallard (Anas platyrhynchos) is a medium-sized dabbling duck in the family
  Anatidae, and the wild duck people in Georgia see most often. The usual
  Georgian name is გარეული იხვი. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of
  Georgia confirms it — on high-quality evidence including photographs, ringing
  data and hunter-collected samples — as a breeding resident, a passage migrant
  and a winter visitor at the same time. Adults are about 50–65 cm long.
  Breeding males have a glossy green head and a yellow bill, females are mottled
  brown, and both sexes show a white-bordered blue-purple wing speculum.
  Breeding is centred on the Javakheti Plateau, where roughly 90% of Georgia’s
  breeding waterfowl nest; when those lakes freeze, numbers rise on lowland
  wetlands and urban ponds. It is not a threat to people. It is Least Concern
  both globally and nationally and is not on the 2014 Georgian Red List, but it
  remains a regulated game species.
habitat: >-
  It prefers open, shallow water: lakes, reservoirs, slow rivers, marshes,
  flooded fields and urban ponds. It is a dabbling duck — it takes food at the
  surface or by tipping forward rather than diving deep, so it is almost always
  close to the bank in shallow water. In Georgia it occurs from sea level to
  about 2,100 m.
  Breeding is centred on the shallow lakes of the Javakheti volcanic plateau:
  Madatapa, Bughdasheni, Khanchali, Saghamo, Paravani and Kartsakhi
  (Samtskhe–Javakheti). Madatapa lies at 2,100 m, covers 8.78 km² and is only
  1.8 m deep at most — that shallowness is what makes it suitable for dabbling
  ducks. Lewis et al. (2013) estimate 100–150 breeding pairs on Madatapa across
  five species combined (Spatula clypeata, Anas platyrhynchos, Spatula
  querquedula, Mareca strepera and Aythya ferina), so that figure is not for the
  mallard alone.
  Madatapa is frozen from November to April, so birds move down to the lowlands
  for winter. Average mid-winter duck counts on the Kolkheti Lowland Wetlands
  are around 50,000 birds — again across several species, not mallard alone. The
  same winter surveys also record the [Baltic gull](larus-fuscus) on Kolkheti and
  the Chorokhi delta.
  Photograph-backed Georgian records (iNaturalist) fill in that picture and show
  where people actually meet this duck: Lisi Lake, Turtle Lake, the Tbilisi Sea
  and the Vake Park pond in Tbilisi; Kumisi Reservoir, the Gardabani area,
  Rustavi and Jandari Lake in Kvemo Kartli; Batumi, the Chorokhi delta and Gonio
  in Adjara; Nadarbazevi Lake in Shida Kartli; Bazaleti Lake and Kazbegi
  municipality in Mtskheta-Mtianeti; Lopota Lake and the Sagarejo and Sighnaghi
  municipalities in Kakheti; Gagra, Pitsunda, Gudauta and Sukhumi in Abkhazia.
  Domestic and feral mallards use the same city lakes; those birds are the same
  species and often hybridise with wild stock.
diet: >-
  It takes whatever it can reach with its bill in shallow water: aquatic plants,
  seeds, invertebrates and small aquatic animals. The Georgian encyclopedia entry
  by Zhordania lists water plants, seeds, small crustaceans and molluscs. No
  separate weighed diet study has been published from Georgia. In parks it also
  takes bread from people, which is neither a wild diet nor good nutrition.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day and often stays in loose flocks outside the breeding
  season. Flight is fast and direct, with rapid wingbeats; in the air the
  white-bordered blue-purple wing panel (speculum) is the clearest mark. The
  female’s loud quack is the familiar duck call; males give quieter, rasping
  notes. The clip on this page is xeno-canto XC1094846 (Petter Westberg, Busör,
  Halland, Sweden) — a male call, not a Georgian recording.
  It nests on the ground near water, in grass and reeds; Zhordania notes that in
  Georgia it occasionally nests in trees and even on rooftops in cities. The
  Georgian encyclopedia gives 7–11 eggs laid from April with an incubation of 26
  days; wider-range handbooks give 8–13 eggs and 26–28 days.
  The seasonal rhythm is clearest on the Javakheti lakes. From late August into
  October local breeding ducks gather in large flocks to moult, joined by
  migrants from other breeding areas. The 2022 census counted at least 39,000
  waterfowl across the six Javakheti lakes; numbers rose from late August to the
  second half of October and fell away by the end of November. The mallard is one
  of the few most abundant species counted separately in that survey. After
  breeding, males enter eclipse plumage and look female-like for weeks.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Anas platyrhynchos as Least Concern on the
  IUCN Red List (2025 assessment), with a very large global range and
  population. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global and national
  category LC. Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Resolution No. 190) does not
  list the mallard.
  Hunting is regulated separately. The annex to Environment Minister’s Order
  No. 95 lists the mallard as a huntable migratory bird under two different
  regimes: in the Akhalkalaki, Ninotsminda, Tsalka and Dmanisi municipalities the
  season runs 10 September to 10 November with a daily limit of 5 birds, while in
  the rest of the country it runs 1 November to 1 March with a limit of 6. That
  split follows the ecology — birds mass on Javakheti in autumn and those lakes
  freeze in November, while the lowlands hold birds in winter. Hunting is
  prohibited in reserves and national parks, within 500 m of them, and inside the
  administrative boundaries of cities; destroying nests and collecting wild bird
  eggs are prohibited year-round. The rules are amended periodically, so the
  version in force should be checked before hunting.
  Wetland loss and disturbance are the main pressures. Disease has been studied
  directly in Georgia: in 2009–2011 only low-pathogenic avian influenza viruses
  were found in Javakheti and Kolkheti ducks, peaking during the autumn moult and
  migration period (6.3%) and again in winter on Kolkheti (3.15% on average). In
  2022–2023, 128 of 225 ducks trapped on Madatapa were positive for the influenza
  M gene, with a high co-infection rate; the authors describe Georgia as a mixing
  vessel for these viruses. No separate national population trend has been
  published for the Georgian mallard.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people: it does not bite, it is not venomous, and its
  defence is simply to fly off. Do not chase or hand-catch wild birds. Feeding
  bread in parks is common but is poor nutrition and fouls the water; leave wild
  flocks alone.
  One practical caution does apply. Because avian influenza is documented in
  Georgian wild ducks, do not pick up a sick or dead waterbird — report it to the
  environmental authorities. In Javakheti villages domestic geese, ducks and
  chickens use the same lakes every day, so limiting contact between backyard
  poultry and wild birds reduces the risk of the virus crossing over. Watching
  healthy ducks from a distance is safe.
  Domestic ducks are the same species and may look white, piebald, black or
  oversized — those birds are still mallards. Hybrid young with wild birds occur
  where domestic stock mixes freely.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium dabbling duck. Breeding males have a glossy green head, white
    neck-ring, chestnut breast and yellow bill. Females and eclipse males are
    mottled brown with an orange-and-dark bill. Both sexes show a blue-purple
    white-bordered wing speculum. In Georgia the usual confusion is other brown
    ducks and domestic park ducks.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 50–65 cm; wingspan often 81–98 cm. Handbooks give a mass of
      0.7–1.6 kg, the Georgian encyclopedia 0.8–2 kg. No separate measured series
      has been published from Georgia. Breeding male: bottle-green head, narrow white collar,
      purple-brown breast, grey body, black rear, curled central tail feathers,
      yellow bill. Female: cryptic mottled brown, darker crown and eye-stripe,
      bill mottled orange and dark. Both sexes have orange legs and a glossy
      blue-purple secondary panel (speculum) edged with white — clear in flight
      and often at rest when the wing is folded.
    - >-
      Juveniles resemble the female. After breeding, males enter eclipse plumage
      and look brown and female-like for weeks while they moult flight feathers;
      the yellow bill and (when visible) speculum still help. Domestic and feral
      birds may be white, black, piebald, or oversized; they remain this species
      and interbreed freely with wild mallards.
    - >-
      Eurasian teal (Anas crecca, ჭიკვარა) is much smaller, with a
      green-and-chestnut head pattern in males. Northern pintail (Anas acuta,
      კუდსადგისა იხვი) is longer-necked with a needle tail in males. Gadwall
      (Mareca strepera, რუხი იხვი) males are grey with a black rear and a white
      wing-patch, not a green head. Northern shoveler (Spatula clypeata,
      განიერნისკარტა იხვი) has a huge spatulate bill. Diving ducks (Aythya,
      ყვინთიები) sit lower in the water and dive, and lack the mallard’s
      tip-up feeding. Georgian names here follow the Ilia State University and
      SABUKO checklists and the annex to the hunting rules.
    - >-
      Identification also matters legally, because protected species share the
      same water. Georgia’s 2014 Red List includes ruddy shelduck (Tadorna
      ferruginea, VU), marbled duck (Marmaronetta angustirostris, VU), velvet
      scoter (Melanitta fusca, EN), white-headed duck (Oxyura leucocephala, EN)
      and lesser white-fronted goose (Anser erythropus, EN); hunting those is
      prohibited year-round. Common pochard (Aythya ferina) is on the huntable
      list, yet IUCN assesses it as globally Vulnerable.
    - >-
      It is harmless. A green-headed yellow-billed duck on open water in Georgia
      in spring is almost always this species. A mottled brown duck with a
      white-edged blue wing-panel is usually a female or eclipse mallard, or a
      closely related dabbling duck — check size and bill shape.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 in Systema Naturae. The 2024 Georgian
    checklist follows the OSME Region List for names: order Anseriformes,
    family Anatidae, genus Anas. An older synonym still seen in literature is
    Anas boschas. Field identification is to species.
  - >-
    Handbooks treat Caucasus birds as the nominate subspecies A. p.
    platyrhynchos (Holarctic except Greenland). The Greenland form A. p.
    conboschas does not occur here. Domestic ducks are derived from this
    species; “A. p. domesticus” is not a valid wild subspecies.
  - >-
    Georgian is გარეული იხვი (“wild duck”). English is mallard; older texts say
    wild duck. Russian is кряква. Related Red List names — წითელი იხვი and
    თეთრთვალა იხვი — refer to other species.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R; M; WV, data quality A, global and national Red
    List LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH; RD; CH (photograph; ringing
    data; collected by hunter). That is country-level status — the checklist
    publishes no municipality map, so the regional picture rests on named wetland
    studies and photograph-backed records.
  - >-
    Three bird migration corridors cross Georgia: the Central Asian, the East
    Africa–West Asia and the Mediterranean/Black Sea flyways. That is why a
    single Javakheti lake in autumn holds local breeders alongside ducks arriving
    from northern Europe and western Siberia. Birds trapped on Madatapa are
    ringed, which is where the checklist’s ringing-data (RD) evidence comes from.
faq:
  - question: Is the mallard found in Georgia, and when?
    answer: >-
      Yes, all year. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists
      it as a confirmed breeding resident, passage migrant and winter visitor
      (YR-R; M; WV). Numbers on the Javakheti lakes build from late August to the
      second half of October; those lakes then freeze and birds move down to the
      lowlands, where they are most numerous in winter.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Easiest on city water: Lisi Lake, Turtle Lake, the Tbilisi Sea and the Vake
      Park pond in Tbilisi. The breeding core is the Javakheti lakes (Madatapa,
      Bughdasheni, Khanchali, Saghamo, Paravani, Kartsakhi), and in winter the
      Kolkheti Lowland Wetlands. Photograph-backed records also come from Kumisi
      Reservoir, Jandari Lake, Batumi and the Chorokhi delta, Bazaleti and
      Nadarbazevi lakes, Lopota, and the Abkhazian coast.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Breeding male: green head, yellow bill, white collar, chestnut breast.
      Female: mottled brown with orange-and-dark bill. Both sexes show a
      blue-purple white-bordered wing speculum. Smaller teal and shovelers differ
      in size and bill shape.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 50–65 cm long with a wingspan of 81–98 cm. Handbooks give a mass of
      0.7–1.6 kg, the Georgian encyclopedia 0.8–2 kg. No separate measured series
      has been published from Georgia.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Aquatic plants, seeds, invertebrates, small crustaceans and molluscs, taken
      in shallow water at the surface or by tipping forward. No separate weighed
      diet study has been published from Georgia.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The breeding core is the Javakheti Plateau, where roughly 90% of the
      country’s breeding waterfowl nest. Madatapa was estimated to hold 100–150
      breeding pairs across five duck species combined, the mallard among them. No
      nationwide nest census has been published.
  - question: Can it be hunted?
    answer: >-
      Yes, it is a regulated game species. In the Akhalkalaki, Ninotsminda, Tsalka
      and Dmanisi municipalities the season runs 10 September to 10 November
      (daily limit 5); elsewhere it runs 1 November to 1 March (limit 6). Hunting
      is prohibited in reserves and national parks, within 500 m of them, and
      inside city boundaries. The rules change — check the version in force.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless and is not on the 2014 Georgian Red List; it is Least
      Concern both globally and nationally. Even so, do not pick up a sick or dead
      waterbird, because avian influenza is documented in Georgian ducks. Do not
      disturb nests. Ruddy shelduck, white-headed duck and velvet scoter, which
      share the same water, are protected species.
