commonName: Daghestanian Rock Lizard
location: Mtskheta-Mtianeti (Kazbegi, Khevsureti); records from Tusheti and Tbatana
description: >-
  The Daghestanian Rock Lizard is a non-venomous rock lizard. In Georgia it occurs in the north-eastern mountains, and you cannot tell it apart from the Caucasian Rock Lizard by colour.
overview: >-
  The Daghestanian Rock Lizard (Darevskia daghestanica) is a rock lizard of the true lizard family (Lacertidae). It has no venom and is harmless to people. Its head and body together are about 4.4–5.8 cm long, and the tail is often longer. You cannot tell it apart from the Caucasian Rock Lizard (Darevskia caucasica) by colour. In the past, the Daghestanian Rock Lizard was often considered a close relative of that species or one of its subspecies; it is now treated as a separate species. In Georgia it is confirmed in the northeastern mountains, and it has recently been seen in Tusheti and on Mount Tbatana in Akhmeta Municipality. Most field records come from Mtskheta-Mtianeti, from the mountains of Kazbegi and Khevsureti. A 2026 Georgian checklist also lists it as a confirmed species. It is not expected in the lowlands of western and southern Georgia, so do not look for it there.
habitat: |-
  The Daghestanian Rock Lizard lives in moderately dry, rocky places in the mountains: on cliffs, slopes and in river valleys. Its main range is the northern slope of the Greater Caucasus, from Dagestan to the Darial Gorge in North Ossetia. Farther south, it also occurs in Tusheti and Azerbaijan. In the upper Terek, its range joins that of the Caucasian Rock Lizard without a gap, and the two species may interbreed there. This marks where they meet; it does not mean the Daghestanian Rock Lizard has been confirmed separately in every local community there.

  In Georgia, it has recently been reported from two places: Tusheti and Mount Tbatana in Akhmeta Municipality. As of 2026, there are genetic data from Tusheti and, from Tbatana, an image of the head scales of a lizard found there. Both places are in the mountains of Kakheti, but there are only four records from Kakheti so far, so the map does not mark Kakheti as part of its range. More field records come from Mtskheta-Mtianeti: from Kazbegi and from the Andaki valley in Khevsureti, so the map shows Mtskheta-Mtianeti as its range. Kazbegi lies on the upper Terek, where the Caucasian Rock Lizard also lives, so lizards seen there are hard to identify from photos alone. None of this confirms that it lives in the Alazani Valley or on Kakheti’s dry plains.

  An isolated population lives in the Greater Liakhvi Valley, above Java (Dzau), on the road to the Roki Pass, cut off from the others to the southwest. It was described in 1963, and its presence was confirmed again in 2021. The Liakhvi population does not mean the lizard occurs throughout Shida Kartli.

  The Caucasian Rock Lizard and its close relatives occur at roughly 500–3,200 m. In forest without cliffs, the Daghestanian Rock Lizard has been found only in exceptional cases. Usually, look for it on cliffs or rocks.
diet: >-
  Caucasian rock lizards mostly eat small animals without backbones: spiders, beetles, flies, grasshopper-like insects and ants. The Daghestanian Rock Lizard probably feeds in about the same way.
behavior: |-
  This rock lizard is active during the day. Its flattened body helps it slip into crevices. It lives mainly on rocks but is also found away from them. It has also been recorded in forest, at a spot where there were rocks too. It can shed its tail, so a short or regrown tail does not mean you are looking at a different species.

  It lays eggs. In inland Dagestan, females with swollen bellies were caught from mid to late May. They laid eggs from late May to mid-June, 1–5 at a time. Under artificial conditions, the eggs took 36–51 days to hatch. These dates were recorded in Dagestan and have not been established for Tusheti.
conservation: >-
  According to the IUCN assessment published in 2009 (based on data through 2008), the species is considered of Least Concern globally: its population is probably large, and no rapid decline is apparent. It is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Decree No. 190); the Turkish, Dahl’s and Adjara lizards are the Darevskia species listed there. The 2026 list considers its presence confirmed by biological evidence, though information about its distribution is only moderately reliable. Large genetic differences have been described between groups living in different places, but the list does not use subspecies names. The isolated group in the Liakhvi area is found in a small area, so local threats there may differ. There is no separate national assessment. Do not take it from the wild.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is harmless to people. Still, do not pick it up: its tail can break off easily, and handling stresses the lizard. Colour alone cannot tell you the species, especially in Tusheti and near Tbatana, where the Caucasian Rock Lizard is also reported. So if you see one, photograph its back, its side and the scales on its head. Note where you saw it and at what elevation, then leave the lizard where it is.

  traits:
    - >-
      The body is slender and flattened, the head relatively narrow and flat, and the hind legs long. Where the Caucasian Rock Lizard also occurs, these features can help tell the two apart, but not at a glance from a photo. The scales on the back are smooth or slightly raised. Drawings of a lizard found at Tbatana show the head scales and the plates in front of the rear opening. In photos taken in the wild, these details are often not visible.
    - >-
      The back is often grayish brown, yellowish brown or ash gray. Two parallel rows of dark spots or a netlike pattern may run along the spine, though sometimes the pattern is faint or missing altogether. The pale stripes on the sides often stop short of the scales on top of the head. This is one of the species' identifying features, but not every lizard has it. You cannot tell rock lizard species (Darevskia) apart by color alone.
    - >-
      It is a small lizard. One lizard from Levashi in Dagestan measures 49 mm from head to body. Larger females from inland Dagestan measure 43.95–56.20 mm. The tail is often longer than the head and body combined. The Levashi lizard's tail is damaged, though, so its full length is unknown.
    - >-
      Color alone cannot tell the Caucasian Rock Lizard (D. caucasica) from the Daghestanian Rock Lizard. D. caucasica often has more distinct stripes along its sides. Where the two lizards meet, D. caucasica has more scales around the middle of its body and on its belly, and more pores on its thighs. Its head is also narrower and flatter. Derjugin's Lizard (D. derjugini) belongs to the same genus, but it lives on the ground in forests rather than on steep rock faces. The Mixed Rock Lizard (D. mixta) is associated with places in the west and center where the ranges of different species meet. It is not a substitute for the Tusheti lizard. In the wild, location is the most reliable clue for telling the Daghestanian and Caucasian Rock Lizards apart. The Caucasian Rock Lizard has a continuous range along the upper Terek River and has also been reported in Truso and Zakagori. The Daghestanian Rock Lizard has been reported in Tusheti, while near Tbatana the Caucasian Rock Lizard has been reported as well.
