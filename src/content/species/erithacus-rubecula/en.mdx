description: >-
  A small woodland songbird with an orange face and breast. Confirmed in Georgia
  as a year-round resident and breeder on the 2024 national bird checklist.
overview: >-
  The European robin (Erithacus rubecula) is a small Old World flycatcher
  (family Muscicapidae) confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident. The 2024
  annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia codes it YR-R: a breeder present
  throughout the year. Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A).
  Occurrence quality is high (H), with photographs. The usual Georgian name is
  გულწითელა. Adults are about 14 cm long at species level, olive-brown above
  with an orange face and breast. It is not a threat to people. IUCN lists the
  species as Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns the same category
  nationally.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in woodland understorey, forest edge, parks, gardens,
  and scrub where low cover meets small patches of bare ground. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level residency but does not publish a regional
  gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly across Georgia, including treeless
  high plateaus or open dry steppe without cover. Named record on this page:
  Batumi Botanical Garden, Adjara, 24 June 2016 (photograph by Boris Georgi,
  georgia.birding.day). The atlas map is empty until named administrative units
  are compiled from sources that actually name them. Species-level habitat is
  shady woodland, edge, gardens, and parks. A separate national elevation band
  for Georgia is not published here.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range it
  takes mainly ground invertebrates: earthworms, beetles, ants, and other
  insects; berries and seeds are added in winter. It often drops from a low
  perch onto prey or hops and pauses on the ground. That is species-level
  feeding, not a measured Georgian menu.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. The song is short, high, silvery phrases with pauses,
  given from a bush or a low branch. Across the range it sings for much of the
  year except during the midsummer moult; both sexes are territorial. Full song
  is most expected in Georgia in the breeding season, but the 2024 checklist
  treats the species as a year-round resident, so the bird can appear in winter
  too. Northern and eastern populations move south in winter; western birds are
  often resident. Do not read Georgia as a pure passage point on that list. The
  nest is low: a bank, roots, cavity, or similar crevice. Species-level clutches
  are often about 4–6 eggs, with incubation of about 13 days. Those figures are
  from the wider range, not a Georgian nest sample. The checklist treats the
  species as a breeder in Georgia. In Britain it often approaches people; on
  the continent, including the Caucasus, it is often more wary.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Erithacus rubecula as Least Concern on the
  IUCN Red List. The European population is large. BirdLife’s 2021 European Red
  List of Birds estimates 48,900–490,000 breeding pairs in Georgia (2013–2017,
  partial method; trend unknown). That is a wide range, not a precise census;
  the source given is EBBA Georgia (SABUKO, Ilia State University, PSOVI).
  EUNIS reports Least Concern in Europe and notes EU Birds Directive coverage;
  that instrument does not apply in Georgia. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red
  List (Government Resolution No. 190). The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns
  global and national Red List category LC. A Georgia-specific population trend
  is not quantified on this page. Do not catch or keep wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous or poisonous and is not a threat to people. Wild birds must
  not be captured. Keep distance from nests.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A sparrow-sized songbird with an orange face and breast, olive-brown back,
    and a pale belly. The sexes look alike in the field. Juveniles lack the
    orange breast. In Georgia it is a year-round resident on the 2024 checklist.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 14 cm and wingspan about 20–22 cm at species level. Mass is
      often about 14–21 g. The body is rounded, the bill fine and dark, the legs
      brownish. It has no large white wing patch and no long orange tail.
    - >-
      Adults have a continuous orange-rufous forehead, cheeks, throat, and
      breast. The back is olive-brown. A bluish-grey band often borders the
      breast; the belly is pale, whitest in the centre. The sexes are not
      separable in the field. Juveniles are brown and buff with dense pale
      spotting and no orange face and breast; the orange appears after the first
      moult.
    - >-
      Common nightingale (Luscinia megarhynchos) is a breeding migrant (BB; M)
      on the 2024 Georgian checklist: larger, plain brown, without an orange
      face. Bluethroat (Luscinia svecica) is a breeding migrant; adult males
      have a blue throat. Common redstart (Phoenicurus phoenicurus) and black
      redstart (Phoenicurus ochruros) show an orange tail; breeding male
      redstarts have a dark face, not a full orange face. White-throated robin
      (Irania gutturalis) is coded ND (status not defined) on that list. In this
      atlas რუხი გულწითელა is another name for yellowhammer (Emberiza
      citrinella), a yellow bunting, not this species. This atlas has a page for
      yellowhammer; it has a page for nightingale. It does not yet have separate
      pages for bluethroat or redstarts.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Leave it alone. A small rounded bird with an
      orange face and breast in Georgian woodland or a garden is this species; a
      spotted brown youngster is the same species without the orange.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Motacilla rubecula. The 2024 Georgian
    checklist follows IOC / OSME Region List names: order Passeriformes, family
    Muscicapidae, genus Erithacus. It was formerly placed with thrushes
    (Turdidae); current molecular taxonomy places it with Old World flycatchers.
    English names include European robin (2024 checklist: European Robin),
    robin, and robin redbreast.
  - >-
    Birds of the World accepts nine subspecies. Breeding birds of the Caucasus
    are placed in E. r. caucasicus (Buturlin, 1907; eastern Türkiye to the
    Caucasus): slightly browner above than the nominate, with rufous uppertail
    coverts and tail base. NPLG names Caucasian E. r. caucasicus and nominate
    E. r. rubecula for Georgia. E. r. hyrcanus is mapped by Birds of the World
    to southeastern Azerbaijan and northern Iran; it is not assigned to Georgia
    on this page. Field identification in Georgia is to species.
  - >-
    The usual Georgian name is გულწითელა; ბულწითელა is also used. რუხი გულწითელა
    in this atlas is yellowhammer (Emberiza citrinella), not this species.
    English is European robin. Russian зарянка; Turkish kızılgerdan.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R, data quality A, global and national Red List LC,
    occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    confirmation, not a regional map. Named photographic evidence on this page
    is Batumi Botanical Garden, 24 June 2016. Gallery photographs on this page
    were not taken in Georgia.
faq:
  - question: Is the European robin found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident (breeder, present throughout the year).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In woodland understorey, forest edge, parks, gardens, and scrub. A named
      photograph on this page is from Batumi Botanical Garden, 24 June 2016.
      This page does not colour a regional map. Occurrence is confirmed at
      country level, not by region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Adults have an orange face and breast, an olive-brown back, and a pale
      belly. The sexes look alike. Juveniles are spotted brown without an orange
      breast. Nightingales lack the orange face; redstarts show an orange tail.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 14 cm long at species level, with a wingspan of about 20–22 cm and
      mass often about 14–21 g. A separate measured series from Georgia is not
      given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Ground invertebrates (earthworms, insects) and, seasonally, berries or
      seeds. Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year, according to the 2024 checklist (YR-R). Song is strongest in the
      breeding season, but the bird can appear in woodland and gardens in any
      month.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Year-round resident on the 2024 checklist means it is accepted as a
      breeder and remains through the year.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. It is globally Least Concern and is not on Georgia’s 2014
      Red List. The 2024 checklist also codes it LC nationally. Do not catch
      wild birds.
