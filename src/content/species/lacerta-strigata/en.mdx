commonName: "Striped Lizard"
location: "Georgia — mainly semiarid areas"
description: >-
  A harmless lizard found in Georgia's semiarid habitats. Young lizards have
  five pale stripes; adult males are often green at the front.
overview: >-
  The striped lizard (Lacerta strigata) is a confirmed, harmless species in
  Georgia. It lives mainly in semiarid places. Young lizards have five pale
  stripes along the back and sides; adult males are often green at the front.
  Colour alone cannot reliably separate it from other green lizards.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, the striped lizard occurs in semiarid places; a genetically
  confirmed specimen comes from Kakheti. Typical habitats include steppe, semi-desert, open
  riverbanks, meadow and field edges, shelterbelts and shrubland. It takes cover
  beside shrubs, in dense grass, rodent burrows and rock piles.
diet: >-
  The striped lizard feeds mainly on small invertebrates, including beetles,
  spiders, snails, ants, flies and grasshoppers.
behavior: >-
  The striped lizard is active by day. Caucasian accounts describe adults
  emerging after winter often in March; in mild Georgian winters they may appear
  in February. Adults usually enter winter dormancy from September, while young
  may remain active into October or November.

  Mating starts in spring, and egg-laying is described from late spring to
  early summer. A female may lay two clutches in a season, each with about 6–11
  eggs.
conservation: >-
  In its 2008 global assessment, IUCN classified the striped lizard as Least
  Concern but assessed its overall population trend as decreasing. It is not
  included on Georgia's 2014 Red List. Habitat destruction is a documented
  threat to the species.
interaction: >-
  The striped lizard is not venomous and does not pose a danger to people. If
  picked up, it may bite defensively or shed its tail. In nature, it is best to
  leave it where it is and avoid disturbing it. There is no need to take a wild
  lizard home.

  summary: >-
    The striped lizard is a medium-sized lizard with well-developed limbs.
    Young individuals are usually brownish and have five pale stripes along the
    body. Adult males often become bright green, especially on the front of the
    body, while the rear can remain olive-brown. Do not rely on colour alone to
    separate it from other green lizards.
  traits:
    - >-
      The striped lizard has a robust build and well-developed legs. It has a
      distinct scale collar on the neck and larger belly plates arranged in
      several longitudinal rows.

      These small characters are hard to see at a distance. Without picking
      up the animal, overall size, colour, body pattern and habitat are more
      useful for identification.
    - >-
      Young striped lizards are olive-brown with five narrow pale stripes along
      the back and sides. They may also have small dark spots between the
      stripes. The stripes gradually fade with age, though females often retain
      traces for longer.

      Adult males are often bright green on the front of the body, while the
      rear and tail can remain olive-brown. During the breeding period, the
      head, throat and sides of the neck may become bluish. Females are usually
      paler and often keep spots or traces of stripes.
    - >-
      The sand lizard (Lacerta agilis) can resemble the striped lizard,
      especially when young, because both can have stripes. The sand lizard is
      widespread in western Georgia, while eastern records mainly point to sites
      above about 600–800 m.

      The striped lizard is more strongly linked to semiarid habitats. Colour
      alone is not a reliable way to separate the two species.
    - >-
      The Caucasian green lizard (Lacerta media) is another large green lizard
      that can resemble the striped lizard. In Georgia it is mainly associated
      with eastern semiarid areas, with separate records also known from the
      northwest.

      Where both species occur, pay attention to size, the pattern of young
      individuals and other distinguishing characters. Green colour alone is not
      enough.
    - >-
      The snake-eyed lizard (Ophisops elegans) is smaller than the striped
      lizard and mainly occurs in open, dry places in the southeast. Its eyelids
      are fused, so the eye can look permanently open.

      Rock lizards (Darevskia) are usually smaller and are often found on rocks
      and walls. They do not have the five-stripe pattern typical of young
      striped lizards. None of these lizards is venomous.

  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adults usually measure about 7–11 cm from snout to vent, excluding
      the tail. Total length including the tail can reach about 30 cm.
  - question: How does it differ from the sand lizard?
    answer: >-
      In eastern Georgia, the sand lizard (Lacerta agilis) is found mainly above
      about 600–800 m, while the striped lizard (Lacerta strigata) is linked to
      semiarid habitats. Young lizards of both species can be striped; neither
      elevation nor pattern alone is enough to identify one.
  - question: How does it differ from the Caucasian green lizard?
    answer: >-
      The Caucasian green lizard (Lacerta media) is a larger green lizard than
      the striped lizard and in Georgia is mainly associated with eastern
      semiarid areas. Adults can look similar in colour, so do not rely only on
      green colour when identifying them.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      By day, mostly in the warmer months. Caucasian accounts describe
      adults often appearing in March; in mild Georgian winters they may
      appear in February. Young lizards may remain active later in autumn.
