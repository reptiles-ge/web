location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A medium tree squirrel with a chestnut-grey back, a pale eye-ring, and a rusty
  to yellowish belly that is not white. It is native and resident in Georgia.
overview: >-
  The Caucasian squirrel (Sciurus anomalus) is a native tree squirrel in Georgia,
  with a chestnut-grey back and a rusty belly. It is a mammal in the squirrel
  family (Sciuridae) and lives here year-round. At species level total length is
  about 32–36 cm, the tail 13–18 cm, and mass about 250–410 g. It has no venom;
  it can bite if handled. IUCN lists it as Least Concern. Georgia’s 2014 Red List
  lists it as Vulnerable (VU, A1e) because of pressure from an introduced
  species.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in deciduous and mixed forest — beech, chestnut, oak,
  hazel — and in orchards and parks with mature trees. National Geographic
  Georgia places the Georgian range on the southern slopes of the Greater
  Caucasus and the northern slopes of the Lesser Caucasus. Koprowski and
  co-authors, summarising Caucasian literature, say beech forest up to about
  2,000 m is typical and that pine forest is often avoided; that is a
  Caucasus-level picture, not a measured Georgian elevation belt. The same review
  says that in Transcaucasia the species stays mainly at elevations where snow is
  uncommon. The Borjomi-Kharagauli photograph on this page shows an animal on a
  conifer branch — one observation, not a settlement of the habitat question.
  Named points: the type locality at the fortress of Sabeka on the Sulori River,
  about 25 km southwest of Kutaisi (Chaworth-Musters 1937); Borjomi-Kharagauli
  National Park, 4 June 2015 (photograph by ზაური ხაჩიძე on this page); Vake
  Park, Tbilisi, 9 February 2025 (photograph by Yuri Samoylov on this page).
  Bukhnikashvili, Kandaurov, Sheklashvili and Natradze 2023 compile 71 Georgian
  records from 1855–2022 — mostly museum and literature, plus two unpublished
  field records — without reprinting the full gazetteer here. This page does not
  colour administrative regions. Do not assume even density across the country.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range tree
  seeds are the main food: acorns, beechnuts, chestnuts, walnuts, and hazelnuts.
  Caucasian accounts also list apricot stones, fruit (apple, pear, peach, grape,
  plum), berries, mushrooms, and occasionally insects, eggs, or nestlings. It
  stores nuts in tree cavities, rock clefts, or buildings; Enukidze 1960 describes
  larders of up to 5–6 kg. In some areas it also buries seed in leaf litter.
  Forgotten stores can germinate — a seed-dispersal role, not specialised
  “gardening”. The realistic picture in Georgia is forest and orchard seed, but
  the local menu has not been measured here.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day and does not hibernate. Activity is often morning and late
  afternoon, with a midday pause in warm weather and a shift toward midday in
  winter. It forages in trees and on the ground and is more terrestrial than the
  Eurasian red squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris). Leaps between trees are uncommon in
  the Caucasian literature. Nests are usually tree hollows lined with moss and
  dry leaves, often 5–14 m up, with the entrance facing south or southeast;
  nests under rocks, roots, or buildings are also recorded. According to Enukidze
  1960, adults moult twice a year: spring (February–April) and autumn
  (September–October); the moult lasts longer above 1,500 m. Breeding can occur
  through much of the year; in Transcaucasia litters of 2–7 young are most often
  reported in late January–early February, April, and mid-July–late August.
  Those dates are regional, not a Georgian den sample. The female cares for the
  young. The call is a sharp metallic chit-chit-chit; Stroganova compared it with
  the green woodpecker. There is no Georgian recording on this page; the clip is
  xeno-canto XC961887 (Stuart Fisher, Sarıyer, Istanbul, 22 December 2008),
  marked uncertain by the recordist.
conservation: >-
  Yiğit, Kryštufek, Sozen, Bukhnikashvili and Shenbrot assess the species as
  Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (2016 assessment; 2017 errata version). The
  global range is wide; the main named threat is habitat destruction. Georgia’s
  2014 Red List (Government Resolution No. 190) lists it as Vulnerable (VU, A1e):
  under pressure from an introduced species. That introduced squirrel is Sciurus
  vulgaris, released onto the northern Greater Caucasus from the Altai in 1937
  and now sympatric with S. anomalus in parts of the Caucasus (Stroganova 1958;
  Vereshchagin 1967). Bukhnikashvili et al. 2023 also tabulate Georgian records
  of S. vulgaris (54 records in the same compilation). Field identification to
  species still matters. This atlas has no Eurasian red squirrel page yet. Local
  pressures also include forest loss and, historically, fur harvest; a current
  national trend is not measured on this page. Red List listing means the taxon
  is treated as protected in Georgia; do not treat IUCN LC as a licence to take
  it.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is not poisonous to touch. It can bite if handled or
  cornered. Any wild mammal can theoretically carry rabies; Georgian literature
  names fox, jackal, wolf, and dogs as the main sources, not this species. Do
  not feed or pick it up. In some parts of the Caucasus it enters nut and fruit
  orchards; that is a crop issue, not a medical one.

  summary: >-
    A medium tree squirrel with a chestnut-grey back, a pale eye-ring, and a
    rusty to yellowish belly. Native and resident in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      Total length about 32–36 cm, tail 13–18 cm, mass about 250–410 g. There is
      no obvious size or colour difference between males and females. Claws are
      relatively short for a tree squirrel. The ears may show small winter tufts
      (about 1.0–1.2 cm) in the north of the range; they are not the long tufts
      of S. vulgaris (about 2.5–3.5 cm where the two overlap). The
      Borjomi-Kharagauli photograph of 4 June 2015 on this page shows short,
      rounded ears without long tufts.
    - >-
      The back is buffy chestnut-grey in the nominate subspecies (S. a.
      anomalus), which is the form of Transcaucasia, Turkey, and Lesbos. A pale
      buff to yellow ring around the eye is usual. The belly is rust-chestnut to
      buff-yellow — not white. The tail is heavily furred and may be yellow-brown
      to deep red. Summer and winter coats are similar; the belly may look
      rustier in winter.
    - >-
      The only other Sciurus in Georgia is the introduced Eurasian red squirrel
      (S. vulgaris), tabulated in Bukhnikashvili et al. 2023. Distinguish it in
      the field by the belly: white or cream in vulgaris, rusty or yellowish in
      anomalus. Vulgaris is more strictly arboreal and, in winter in the overlap
      zone, has longer ear tufts. The edible dormouse (Glis glis) is smaller,
      grey, nocturnal, and lacks the long bushy squirrel tail. This atlas has no
      vulgaris or dormouse page yet.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. A daytime squirrel in Georgian woodland or a city park,
      with a rusty belly and no long ear tufts, is this species if the
      identification is to Sciurus. Do not handle it to check pads or teats.
facts:
  - >-
    Güldenstädt described it in 1785. The accepted name is Sciurus anomalus.
    Kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Rodentia, family
    Sciuridae, genus Sciurus. The authority is sometimes wrongly given as J. F.
    Gmelin; Koprowski and co-authors treat Güldenstädt, 1785 as correct. Sciurus
    persicus has been misapplied; that name traces to a dormouse description, not
    this squirrel.
  - >-
    Three subspecies are widely listed: S. a. anomalus (Transcaucasia, Turkey,
    Lesbos), S. a. syriacus (Levant), and S. a. pallescens (Iraq and Zagros
    Iran). Georgian animals belong with the nominate form. Field identification
    in Georgia is to species.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual name is კავკასიური ციყვი; სპარსული ციყვი is also used.
    In English: Caucasian squirrel or Persian squirrel; also golden squirrel,
    Transcaucasian squirrel, or Georgian squirrel in older literature. Generic
    ციყვი means squirrel.
faq:
  - question: Is the Caucasian squirrel found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is native and resident. The type locality was restricted to near
      Kutaisi. National mammal lists include it.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In deciduous and mixed forest and in orchards and parks with large trees.
      Named points include the type locality near Kutaisi; Borjomi-Kharagauli
      National Park (photograph, 4 June 2015); and Vake Park in Tbilisi
      (photograph, 9 February 2025). A 2023 compilation has 71 Georgian records
      from 1855–2022. This page does not map every region.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Medium tree squirrel, chestnut-grey back, pale eye-ring, rusty or yellowish
      belly — not white. Total length about 32–36 cm, tail 13–18 cm.
  - question: How does it differ from the introduced red squirrel?
    answer: >-
      The Eurasian red squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris) has a white or cream belly
      and, in winter where the two overlap, longer ear tufts. The Caucasian
      squirrel’s belly is rusty or yellowish.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly tree seeds and nuts. Diet has not been studied separately in Georgia
      on this page.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      By day, year-round. It does not hibernate. It is a resident and does not
      migrate like many birds.
  - question: Does it make a sound?
    answer: >-
      Yes. A sharp metallic chit-chit-chit. The clip on this page is from Turkey
      (xeno-canto XC961887) and is marked uncertain by the recordist; there is
      no Georgian recording here.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous. It can bite if handled. Do not pick it up.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      Globally Least Concern. On Georgia’s 2014 Red List it is Vulnerable because
      of an introduced squirrel. Do not treat IUCN LC as a licence to take it.
