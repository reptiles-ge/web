commonName: East Caucasian tur
location: High eastern Greater Caucasus
description: >-
  A large goat-like mammal of the eastern Greater Caucasus. Adult males carry
  heavy horns that form an open spiral. In Georgia it is tied to high rocky and
  alpine slopes.
overview: >-
  The East Caucasian tur (Capra cylindricornis), also called Daghestan tur or
  eastern tur, is a large wild goat in the family Bovidae. In Georgia it is
  mainly associated with the high mountains of the eastern Greater Caucasus.
  Sources name Tusheti, Khevsureti, the Kazbegi area, and the Lagodekhi part of
  the Caucasus.

  For identification, the heavy body, relatively short legs, and the large horns
  of adult males are especially useful. Unlike wild goat, the horn does not
  simply curve backward in a scimitar shape; it forms a more open spiral.

  According to IUCN, the species is globally Near Threatened, while Georgia’s
  2014 Red List lists the Daghestan tur as Vulnerable (VU).
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, the East Caucasian tur is associated with the high, rocky
  environments of the Greater Caucasus: alpine and subalpine meadows, scree,
  inaccessible slopes, and gorges.

  According to Tusheti Protected Landscape data, the summer range covers about
  330 km² and extends from about 2,800 m elevation to the highest peaks. Many
  animals remain in nival and subnival areas that are difficult to reach. Their
  summer appearance on alpine meadows may be limited by intensive sheep grazing.

  The species’ full range covers the eastern Greater Caucasus: Georgia,
  Azerbaijan, and the North Caucasus of Russia. Tur is especially associated with
  rugged relief, safer slopes near cliffs, and relatively undisturbed places.

  This does not mean the species is evenly distributed across every high-mountain
  region of Georgia.
diet: >-
  The East Caucasian tur is herbivorous. It feeds on grasses, alpine herbaceous
  plants, leaves, shoots, and low shrubs. Its diet changes with season and
  environment.

  In winter it may move to lower or less snowy slopes if snow cover and human
  disturbance allow.

  Detailed separate diet data for the Georgian population are not presented on
  this page.
behavior: >-
  East Caucasian tur is active by day, though in hot seasons it often spends the
  midday hours in relatively sheltered places.

  Group composition changes by season and sex. Females form separate groups with
  young, young males gather together, and adult males are often found alone or
  with other males.

  At species level, the mating season is mainly linked to December-January, and
  young are born in late spring. These data refer to the whole range, not to a
  full calendar for a specific Georgian population.

  Cloven hoofprints may indicate the presence of tur, but reliable identification
  from tracks alone is difficult.
conservation: >-
  According to the 2020 IUCN assessment, the East Caucasian tur is Near
  Threatened. Georgia’s 2014 Red List lists the Daghestan tur as Vulnerable
  (VU).

  The Tusheti Protected Landscape source estimates the local population at about
  750 individuals and treats it as stable, but this is not a modern total for all
  of Georgia.

  The main threats named are illegal hunting, overgrazing by livestock on
  subalpine and alpine meadows, habitat fragmentation, and human disturbance. The
  effects of tourism, helicopters, and ski infrastructure may also be important
  in specific places.

  Studies show that the species is especially associated with rugged, less
  disturbed relief close to cliffs.
interaction: >-
  The East Caucasian tur is not venomous and does not perceive people as prey.
  Risk mainly appears when a person gets too close to a group on a rocky slope,
  follows it for photographs, or lets a dog chase it.

  Observe from a safe distance, do not feed it, and do not follow it onto steep
  slopes. The species is listed on Georgia’s Red List, while illegal hunting and
  human disturbance are real threats.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large wild goat with short legs, a heavy body, and large open-spiralled
    horns in adult males. In Georgia it is mainly associated with high, rocky
    environments of the eastern Greater Caucasus.
  traits:
    - >-
      Size differs sharply by sex. According to general literature, female body
      length reaches about 138 cm, shoulder height about 85 cm, and mass about
      56 kg. An adult male may be about 190 cm long, up to 104 cm at the
      shoulder, and up to 140 kg.

      These figures describe general species data, not a separately measured
      population in Georgia.
    - >-
      Adult males have a short beard. Coat colour changes by season, from dark
      brown to lighter rusty-brown tones. Females and young are usually more
      uniformly brownish.

      Male horns are rounded at the base, grow upward and outward, then curve
      downward and inward to form an open spiral. Female horns are much shorter.
    - >-
      [Wild goat](capra-aegagrus) is a different wild goat. An adult male wild
      goat has long horns that curve farther backward in a scimitar shape, while
      the East Caucasian tur is more massive and male horns form an open spiral.

      Chamois (Rupicapra) is much smaller and has short, hooked horns.
      [Roe deer](capreolus-capreolus) and [red deer](cervus-elaphus) belong to
      the deer family: males grow annually renewed antlers, not the permanent
      horns typical of goat-like bovids.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. In field identification, the most practical marks are
      large size, a heavy goat-like body, rocky high-mountain habitat, and the
      open-spiralled horns of adult males.

faq:
  - question: Is the East Caucasian tur found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The East Caucasian tur is a high-mountain mammal of Georgia’s eastern
      Greater Caucasus. Sources name Tusheti, Khevsureti, the Kazbegi area, and
      the Lagodekhi part of the Caucasus. It does not occur throughout all of
      Georgia.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Most realistically, it can be seen from a distance on high rocky slopes in
      the alpine and subalpine zones. Tusheti data describe the summer range from
      about 2,800 m elevation to the highest peaks.

      Getting close to a group is not worth it: it disturbs the animals and
      increases risk for people in rocky terrain.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Adult males have large open-spiralled horns, a heavy body, and relatively
      short legs. Wild goat horns curve backward more like a scimitar, while
      chamois is much smaller and has short, hooked horns.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      General literature gives adult males up to about 104 cm at the shoulder and
      up to about 140 kg. Females are smaller, about 85 cm at the shoulder and up
      to 56 kg.

      A separate measurement series for the Georgian population is not presented
      on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It is herbivorous. It feeds on grasses, leaves, shoots, and low shrubs,
      depending on season and environment. It is not a predator.
  - question: Is it dangerous or venomous?
    answer: >-
      No. The East Caucasian tur is not venomous and does not perceive people as
      prey. Risk is mainly tied to getting too close on rocky slopes, chasing or
      disturbing an animal, or sending a dog toward it.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The East Caucasian tur is listed as Vulnerable (VU) on Georgia’s Red
      List, while IUCN assesses it globally as Near Threatened. Illegal hunting
      is named as one of its main threats.
