commonName: Urartian ratsnake
location: Eastern Georgia
description: >-
  The Urartian ratsnake (Elaphe urartica) is a large, spotted but non-venomous snake. It lives mainly in dry eastern Georgia and, from a distance, looks like the Levantine viper.
overview: |-
  The Urartian ratsnake (Elaphe urartica) is a large, spotted snake, but it is not venomous to humans. From head to tail tip it is usually about 80–120 cm long. In Georgia, it is known mainly from dry and semi-dry areas in the east. From a distance it can be mistaken for the Levantine viper, so watch any unfamiliar large snake from a safe distance.

  The Urartian ratsnake belongs to the family Colubridae. In 2019 it was split from Elaphe sauromates as a separate species, although the two snakes look very much alike. Georgia's current list does not include E. sauromates as a separate species living in the country. The 2026 list includes E. urartica, but still treats it as a species whose status is not yet fully settled. The reason is that the northwestern Caspian region, where the ranges of the two species come close to each other, has not yet been studied enough.
habitat: |-
  In Georgia, the Urartian ratsnake lives mainly in dry and fairly dry places in the east. It is found on dry grassy plains, in semi-desert, where woodland and open plains alternate, in open juniper woods, on rocky slopes and along the edges of dry forest. Only [Kakheti](/regions/kakheti) has enough records: in Dedoplistsqaro, Sagarejo, Kvareli and Lagodekhi municipalities. DNA-checked samples come from Chankani village in Dedoplistsqaro Municipality, and it is also known from Shiraki.

  In [Tbilisi](/regions/tbilisi) it has been seen a few times, including at Lisi Lake, but there are only a few such cases. Kvemo Kartli also has few observations: some have a hidden exact location with Kakheti named in the description, while others lie in Tbilisi's Isani–Samgori area, near the border. There are no reports from Samtskhe-Javakheti. To the west, older sources place the edge of its range towards the Trialeti and Tsalka plateaus, but these areas are not considered confirmed places where it lives. Across its whole range, including outside Georgia, it is known from places about 25 m below sea level up to 2,600 m.
diet: >-
  The Urartian ratsnake does not use venom: it holds its prey with its body, like other Elaphe snakes. These snakes eat rodents, small birds, chicks and eggs, and sometimes catch lizards too. They can also climb bushes and trees. But this applies to related species and does not mean that the Urartian ratsnake in Georgia feeds on exactly these things.
behavior: >-
  The Urartian ratsnake is active in the warm season and lays eggs. When it senses danger, it usually tries to escape. But if you catch it, this large snake may bite hard, so do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  According to the 2025 global assessment, the Urartian ratsnake (Elaphe urartica) faces a low risk of extinction. It is widespread and adapts to different environments, and threats mostly affect particular places. The same assessment says it is unknown whether its numbers are rising, falling or staying the same. Neither the Urartian ratsnake nor E. sauromates is on Georgia's 2014 Red List. The 2026 Georgian list includes the Urartian ratsnake, but as a candidate species that still needs final confirmation. The reason is that in the northwestern Caspian region it is not yet well studied where the boundary between the two species lies.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The Urartian ratsnake is a large, non-venomous snake. It has big, rounded spots along its back and a dark head. In Georgia, two things help most in identifying it: these spots and seeing the snake in a dry place in the east of the country. Unlike the Levantine viper, it has round pupils, but when [identifying a snake](/snakes/shxamiani-gvelis-amocnoba), do not rely on pupil shape alone.
  traits:
    - >-
      Its length is usually about 80–120 cm and rarely exceeds 1.2 m. The head is large compared with the body and stands out clearly from the neck. From the side, the nose often looks slightly hooked, and the pupil is round. In the middle of the body, the scales are arranged in 23–25 rows. The scales in 18–21 of these rows have a small raised line down the middle.
    - >-
      The back has about 50–65 large, round brown or black spots. Many of them have pale edges, and on the rear part of the body the spots may be more stretched from side to side. The base colour of the body is yellowish or whitish, rarely bright yellow. The top of the head is dark or almost black, and a dark stripe runs from behind the eye toward the corner of the mouth. The belly is whitish or pale yellow. Some snakes also have small spots on their sides, mottled like marble.
    - >-
      The [steppe ratsnake](elaphe-dione) (Elaphe dione) is also found in the same dry east, but its pattern is usually finer. It often shows four stripes running along the body and fairly small spots that cross from side to side. The [red-bellied racer](dolichophis-schmidti) (Dolichophis schmidti) is a slimmer snake, and adults often have a reddish belly. The [Levantine viper](macrovipera-lebetina) (Macrovipera lebetina) is venomous. Its body is heavier, and its pupil is vertical, like a narrow slit. The [smooth snake](coronella-austriaca) (Coronella austriaca) is much smaller and has smoother scales.
    - >-
      Elaphe sauromates looks very similar to the Urartian ratsnake, so it is hard to tell these two species apart by sight alone. In Georgia, local snakes from this group are now treated as E. urartica, while E. sauromates is found farther west. So where you saw the snake is a real help in identifying it. Do not pick up a snake to count its scales or look closely at other features.
    - >-
      It has no venom. When defending itself, it may give a painful bite, but the [bite](/snakes/gvelis-nakbeni) is not venomous. From afar it resembles a Levantine viper, so watch any unknown large, spotted snake from a safe distance. Do not go close just to check the eye pupil or other features.

faq:
  - question: Is the Urartian ratsnake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The Urartian ratsnake has been confirmed in Georgia and is included in the 2026 Georgian list. On that list, however, it is still a candidate species. It lives mainly in eastern Georgia and is not found throughout the country.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Mainly in eastern Georgia, in dry and relatively dry places. Only Kakheti has enough records: it has been seen in the Dedoplistsqaro, Sagarejo, Kvareli and Lagodekhi municipalities, and it is also known from Shiraki. It has been seen in Tbilisi only on a few separate occasions, there are few records from Kvemo Kartli, and none from Samtskhe-Javakheti. This snake is not associated with the humid areas of Colchis.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. The Urartian ratsnake has no venom. It may bite to defend itself, so do not pick it up. Because it can be mistaken for the Levantine viper, watch any unfamiliar large spotted snake from a safe distance. Do not go closer just to check the shape of its pupil.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      The Urartian ratsnake has a yellowish or whitish body with about 50–65 large, rounded spots on its back. The head is dark, and the pupil is round. From head to tail tip it is usually about 80–120 cm long. The Steppe ratsnake has a finer pattern and often has four stripes running along the body. The Levantine viper, by contrast, has a vertical pupil. But do not rely on a single feature to identify it.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Snakes that have been measured are about 80–120 cm long from head to tail tip and usually no longer than 1.2 m. Males have a relatively longer tail, while females have, on average, more scales on the belly. Still, these features are not enough to easily tell males from females in the wild.
  - question: How does it differ from Elaphe sauromates?
    answer: >-
      Elaphe urartica and Elaphe sauromates look very much alike, so it is hard to tell them apart reliably by appearance alone. Georgia's current list treats the snakes found here as E. urartica, while E. sauromates is found farther west. The 2026 list also follows this division. However, it still counts E. urartica only as a likely separate species. The reason is that the northwestern Caspian area, where the ranges of these two snakes come close to each other, has not yet been studied enough.
  - question: What does it eat, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: >-
      The Urartian ratsnake lays eggs. Its relatives, other Elaphe snakes, eat rodents, small birds and eggs.
