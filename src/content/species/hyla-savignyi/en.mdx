description: >-
  The Yellow-Lemon Treefrog is a small, non-venomous frog that lives in the dry south-east of Georgia. It does not occur throughout the country.
overview: >-
  The yellow-lemon treefrog (Hyla savignyi) is a non-venomous treefrog that lives in the dry south-east of Georgia. Adults are usually about 3–5 cm long, and it is not dangerous to people. It does not occur throughout Georgia. It lives in the Iori valley, around Vashlovani and south-east of Tbilisi, in steppe, scrub and near water. Don't look for it in humid Colchic forest; old records from western Georgia are considered errors. Elsewhere in the country, a treefrog you see is, as a rule, the eastern treefrog (Hyla orientalis). In eastern Georgia the ranges of the two treefrogs touch, but they hardly ever live in the same place. How much they interbreed along this boundary is not yet fully clear. Older books often called it Hyla arborea savignyi; today it is a separate species. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as a biologically confirmed species and lists the subspecies H. s. savignyi.
habitat: |-
  In Georgia, the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog prefers drier places to forest. It lives in steppe, shrubland and gardens, and near water: pools, ponds and slow streams. It may also turn up on dry slopes far from water. Within the country it occurs south-east of Tbilisi, including in Kakheti.

  A DNA-tested specimen comes from Dali Reservoir (Dedoplistskaro, Iori River). The frog is also known from the woods along the Iori and from Vashlovani Reserve. The Alazani valley is mentioned too, but without a precise location. South of Tbilisi there is an old, well-documented record. Treefrogs around Telavi show mixed features, so they are not counted as pure H. savignyi. Beyond Georgia, the range continues into Armenia, western Azerbaijan, Anatolia and the Near East.
diet: >-
  Adult treefrogs catch invertebrates, mainly insects. Tadpoles eat algae and plant debris.
behavior: |-
  By day it often sits on a leaf or a shrub, or hides under a stone or log, or in a burrow. It lays its eggs in small, mostly still water: puddles, ponds and sometimes slow brooks where grass and shrubs grow thick. During mating, males call, and their call differs from that of the Eastern Treefrog.

  If you come across one, watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up or move it from one body of water to another. It poses no medical risk to people, but wash your hands after touching it.
conservation: >-
  Globally, the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog is of least concern: it is widespread and thought to be numerous. It is not on Georgia's 2014 Red List, and within the country it is also considered of least concern. Numbers can still fall in particular places if the pools where it lays its eggs dry up or become polluted. Around Vashlovani and Chachuna, part of its habitat lies within protected areas.
interaction: |-
  The yellow-lemon treefrog (Hyla savignyi) is not venomous and poses no danger to people. When it sees a person, it usually tries to hide or escape. Even so, it is best not to pick it up, because amphibian skin is especially sensitive. In Georgia it lives mainly in the drier, open areas of the south-east: in shrubs, grassy places, gardens and near water. If you come across one in nature, it is better not to catch it or move it elsewhere.

  If you touch the treefrog, wash your hands well with soap and water. If its secretion accidentally gets into your eye and causes irritation, rinse the eye with clean water. If the irritation is strong or lasts long, seek medical help. Do not take the treefrog home or keep it as a pet. The best place for an amphibian found in nature is the place where you found it.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small treefrog with smooth skin and sticky pads on its toe tips. In Georgia it is most often confused with the Eastern Treefrog. To tell them apart, place is often more reliable than colour, so take careful note of where you saw it.
  traits:
    - >-
      Adults are usually about 3–5 cm long, with a short body and smooth skin. The toe tips end in rounded sticky pads. These pads are what set a treefrog apart from the Marsh Frog and from toads. So if you are not sure whether you are looking at a treefrog, check the toes first.
    - >-
      The back is mostly light green or yellowish, without spots. It can change colour with temperature and background. Many Yellow-Lemon Treefrogs have a dark stripe along the side that does not form a loop at the groin. Some Eastern Treefrogs have the same stripe, so you cannot tell the two treefrogs apart by this stripe alone.
    - >-
      The eyes are large, and the eardrum is smaller than the eye. A dark spot on the upper lip below the eye is usually absent. These features help, but in Georgia they are not enough on their own to tell the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog from the Eastern Treefrog.
    - >-
      The Eastern Treefrog (Hyla orientalis) belongs to the same genus and lives across most of Georgia, outside the range of the Yellow-Lemon Treefrog. The Yellow-Lemon Treefrog lives south-east of Tbilisi, in drier, treeless places. The two also call differently in the breeding season. Where their ranges meet, including around Telavi, a frog may look like a mix of both. In such places, do not rely on looks alone.
    - >-
      The Marsh Frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) is larger than a treefrog and stays in the water. It has no sticky pads on its toes. Toads, for their part, have warty skin and do not climb trees.
