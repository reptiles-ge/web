commonName: Red fox
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  The red fox is a medium-sized, dog-like predator. It usually has a rusty-red
  back, dark or black backs to the ears and lower legs, and a bushy tail that
  often has a white tip. In Georgia it lives year-round.
overview: >-
  The red fox (Vulpes vulpes) is a mammal in the dog family (Canidae). It is the
  largest species in the true fox genus (Vulpes) and one of the most widely
  distributed predatory mammals in the world. In Georgia it is a native species
  that lives here year-round.

  For the species as a whole, head-and-body length is about 45–90 cm, the tail
  is 30–55 cm, and weight is often 3–14 kg.

  The red fox is not venomous. The main possible risk to people is a bite and
  the risk of infectious diseases, including rabies. IUCN assesses the red fox
  globally as Least Concern, and it is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia, red foxes live in many kinds of places: woodland edges, scrub,
  grasslands, farmland, foothills and the edges of settlements.

  Worldwide, the species uses an even wider range of habitats, including
  forests, open country, mountains, coasts and city outskirts. According to
  IUCN, it occurs up to about 4,500 m across its whole range. The sources used
  on this page do not give a separate elevation range for Georgia.

  On this page's map, Kakheti, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Kvemo
  Kartli, Shida Kartli and Tbilisi are shaded as regions with records. These
  are the regions where enough public photo observations have built
  up. Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti, Imereti and Adjara have only individual records so
  far, so the map does not yet count them as regional distribution. Few or no
  records from a region do not mean that red foxes are absent there. The number
  of records shows how often people observe in an area, not how many foxes live
  there. The Georgian locality named in the sources is the Iori floodplain.
diet: >-
  The red fox is an omnivore: it eats many kinds of food and takes whatever is
  available. Small mammals, especially rodents, make up an important part of its
  diet. It also eats birds, insects, fruit and carrion, and near settlements it
  may eat food waste.

  In unprotected yards it sometimes takes poultry. The sources used on this page
  contain no quantitative diet study of foxes in Georgia, so this list gives a
  general picture, not a measured local diet.
behavior: >-
  The red fox is mainly active at dusk and at night. It can also appear by day,
  especially in quiet places or when food is plentiful. Being active by day is
  not, by itself, a sign of rabies.

  A fox may live in its own den or move into another animal's abandoned burrow.
  It may also shelter in a rock crevice. A family group forms during the
  breeding period; at other times red foxes often hunt alone.

  For the species as a whole, pregnancy lasts about 51–53 days, and often 4–6
  cubs are born, mostly in spring. These figures come from across the species'
  wide range, not from a separate study of foxes in Georgia.

  Fox tracks look like dog tracks but are often more compact. A white tail tip
  is typical of the red fox, but not every fox has one.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assesses the red fox globally as Least Concern and rates its overall
  population trend as stable. The species is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red
  List.

  In Georgia, the red fox is on the list of hunting species. The hunting season
  and other conditions are set by the official rules in force. They are not
  reproduced on this page, so check the current rules in an official source.

  Local pressures can include hunting, deaths on roads, and conflict with people
  over poultry. The sources used on this page do not separately assess how the
  number of foxes in Georgia is changing today.
interaction: >-
  The red fox is not venomous, but it can bite. In Georgia it is named, together
  with other wild predators, as a possible source of rabies. In the country as a
  whole, however, dogs, both owned and stray, are named as the main source of
  rabies. Do not feed a wild fox or pick it up. Rabies vaccination for domestic
  dogs is important.

  summary: >-
    The red fox is a medium-sized, dog-like predator. Useful features are a
    rusty-red back, dark or black backs to the ears and lower legs, and a long,
    bushy tail that often has a white tip. Colour varies between foxes, and not
    every fox has a white tail tip.
  traits:
    - >-
      The red fox has a slender muzzle, erect ears and vertically oval pupils.

      For the species as a whole, head-and-body length is about 45–90 cm, the
      tail 30–55 cm and shoulder height about 35–50 cm; weight is often 3–14 kg.
      Males are usually slightly larger than females.

      The sources used on this page include no separate, large set of
      measurements from foxes in Georgia.
    - >-
      A red fox usually has a rusty-red back, with a paler throat and belly. The
      backs of the ears and the lower legs are usually black.

      The tail is long and bushy. A white tail tip, when you can see it, is a
      good identification mark. Not every fox has one, though: some have a dark
      tail tip. Darker foxes and foxes of other colours also occur.
    - >-
      The golden jackal (Canis aureus) is taller and heavier than a red fox, and
      its colour is usually a more even yellowish grey. Its tail is relatively
      shorter and lacks the white tip that many red foxes have. The backs of a
      jackal's ears are golden-brown; a red fox's are black.

      The grey wolf (Canis lupus) is much larger than a red fox and has longer
      legs. Compared with a dog, a fox usually has a finer muzzle and smaller
      tracks, and it moves in a straighter line.

      The corsac fox (Vulpes corsac) has been recorded in dry areas of the
      northern Caucasus, but the data used on this page do not count it as part
      of Georgia's fauna.
    - >-
      The red fox is not venomous, but a bite or dangerous contact with its
      saliva can carry a risk of rabies. Do not touch a wild fox with your
      hands, especially if it is unusually fearless, confused or aggressive.

      Seeing a fox by day does not, by itself, mean that it is sick: a healthy
      fox may also be active in daylight.
  coloration: >-
    The colour of the upper parts varies between foxes, from pale yellowish red
    to deep reddish brown. The underside can be white, ashy grey or dark
    slate-grey. Three colour morphs are recognised in the species: red, cross
    and silver/black. A cross fox has reddish-brown fur with a black stripe
    running down the back and another across the shoulders. Silver foxes range
    from silvery to almost black. This description applies to the species as a
    whole, not specifically to foxes in Georgia.

facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758, originally as Canis vulpes. The accepted name
    is Vulpes vulpes. Order Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Canidae,
    genus Vulpes.
  - >-
    Caucasus literature historically names several subspecies, including V. v.
    caucasica (Dinnik, 1914) and V. v. alpherakyi (Satunin, 1906). Subspecies
    counts and boundaries are disputed; do not assign a Georgian individual to a
    subspecies in the field with confidence.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual names are მელა and ჩვეულებრივი მელა; some texts use
    წითელი მელა. In English: red fox; older literature also says common fox.
    Cross fox and silver fox are colour morphs of the same species.
  - >-
    IUCN lists Georgia as native. It is in the 2002
    annotated list of Georgia’s mammals. A 2007 list gives it as LC. That is
    country-level confirmation, not a regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the red fox found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The red fox is a native species in Georgia and lives here year-round.
      It is included in the country's mammal checklists and on the list of
      hunting species.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      You may see red foxes at woodland edges, in scrub, on farmland and
      grasslands, in the foothills and on the edges of settlements. In Georgia
      they occur year-round.

      The regions recorded in photo observations are Kakheti,
      Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Shida Kartli and
      Tbilisi. Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti, Imereti and Adjara have only individual
      records so far. See the map on this page for details. The Georgian
      locality named in the sources is the Iori floodplain.
  - question: How can I identify a red fox?
    answer: >-
      Look for a rusty-red back, dark backs to the ears, black lower legs, a
      slender muzzle and a long, bushy tail that often has a white tip.

      The golden jackal is more yellowish grey and taller than a red fox, with a
      relatively shorter tail. The grey wolf is much larger than a red fox and
      has longer legs.
  - question: How big is a red fox?
    answer: >-
      For the species as a whole, head-and-body length is about 45–90 cm, the
      tail is 30–55 cm, and weight is often 3–14 kg. The sources used on this
      page include no separate, large set of measurements from foxes in Georgia.
  - question: What does a red fox eat?
    answer: >-
      A red fox feeds mainly on small mammals, especially rodents. It also eats
      birds, insects, fruit and carrion. The sources on this page include no
      separate quantitative study of what foxes eat in Georgia.
  - question: When is a red fox active?
    answer: >-
      Red foxes are mainly active at night and at dusk, but they can also appear
      by day. In Georgia they live year-round and, unlike many birds, do not
      make regular seasonal migrations.
  - question: Is a red fox dangerous?
    answer: >-
      The red fox is not venomous, but it can bite. In Georgia it is named,
      together with other wild predators, as a possible source of rabies. Do not
      pick up a wild animal.

      If a fox bites you, wash the wound and seek medical care.
  - question: Is the red fox protected?
    answer: >-
      Globally, the red fox is Least Concern, and it is not included in
      Georgia's 2014 Red List. In Georgia it is on the list of hunting species.
