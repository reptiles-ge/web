overview: >-
  The Eurasian eagle-owl (Bubo bubo) is a large owl (family Strigidae) confirmed
  in Georgia as a year-round resident. It breeds here, and photographs confirm
  its presence. The Georgian name is ზარნაშო; ჩვეულებრივი ზარნაშო is also used.
  Little owl (Athene noctua) is a different species, not a synonym of this name.
  Adults are often about 58–71 cm long at species level, with long ear tufts and
  orange or golden-yellow eyes. It is not a threat to people. IUCN lists the
  species as Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns the same category
  nationally.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look on rocky ground, in ravines, at forest edge or near ruins —
  not evenly on treeless alpine plateaus or in the middle of closed dense
  forest. This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level resident status but does not publish a
  regional gazetteer. Georgian encyclopaedic texts call it a resident bird that
  occurs throughout the country; that is general wording, not a municipality
  list on this page. A 2025 Georgian study of barn-owl diet mentions eagle-owl as occupying different niches from
  barn owl, including dry steppe, semi-desert and dense forest; that is a
  methods note, not a map of confirmed localities. Species-level habitat is
  rocky relief with woodland or scrub, also forest edge, ravines, ruins and
  open hunting ground near the nest. Nesting is often on a cliff ledge, in a
  crevice or at a cave entrance. A separate national elevation band for Georgia
  is not published here. The atlas map is left uncoloured until named Georgian
  localities are taken from sources that actually list them.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range the
  main prey is mammals — from voles and rats to hares and, rarely, larger
  animals — and birds, including other raptors and owls. Reptiles, amphibians
  and large invertebrates are also taken. It hunts mainly in open or semi-open
  ground, on the ground or in the air. Prey is often swallowed whole;
  indigestible parts are later coughed up as pellets. That is species-level
  diet, not a measured Georgian menu.
behavior: >-
  It is mainly active at dusk and at night. By day it roosts in a rock crevice,
  in the canopy or in a cave; at dusk it may also sit on a prominent lookout.
  It is territorial and stays on site year-round; it is not a long-distance
  migrant. The male territorial call is a deep, even oohu; the female’s call is
  slightly higher. Pairs often duet. Nesting is on a cliff ledge, in a crevice
  or at a cave entrance; old nests of other large birds are sometimes used,
  rarely the ground. Almost no nest material is added. Species-level clutches
  are often one to four white eggs; the female incubates about 31–36 days while
  the male brings food. Young leave the nest after several weeks and hide on
  nearby ground. Those figures are from the wider range, not a Georgian nest
  sample on this page. The 2024 checklist treats the species as a breeding
  resident in Georgia. Do not handle it; birds may strike people near the nest.
conservation: >-
  It is assessed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. The 2024 Georgian checklist assigns LC globally and nationally. It is
  not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190); among owls that list
  includes barn owl (Tyto alba, EN D1). A Georgian population trend is not
  measured on this page. Documented pressures at species level include
  electrocution, collision with traffic and shooting; that is not a ranked
  national threat list for Georgia. It is listed on CITES Appendix II, so
  international trade is regulated. Do not catch wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people in ordinary encounters. It has large talons; do not approach or handle it. It may
  strike near a nest. Wild birds must not be taken.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Eurasia’s largest owl, with long ear tufts and orange eyes. Confirmed in
    Georgia as a year-round resident on the 2024 checklist. The usual confusion
    is long-eared owl, which is much smaller and slimmer.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length at species level is often about 58–71 cm, mass often about 1.55–4.2
      kg; females are typically heavier than males. Wingspan in species-level
      accounts is often about 1.5–1.8 m. The head is large; the ear tufts are
      long and obvious. The eyes are orange or golden-yellow, not dark. The bill
      is dark and hooked. The facial disc is tawny-brown with a dark “frame”.
      The chin and throat are often white. The body is stocky, the wings broad,
      the legs feathered.
    - >-
      The upperparts are black-brown and tawny, mottled and streaked. The
      breast has broad lengthways blotches; the belly has fine cross-barring.
      Colour varies geographically; field identification is to species, not
      subspecies. Juveniles are fluffier; ear tufts and eye colour are still
      diagnostic. The call is a deep oohu — not the tawny owl’s quavering hoot
      and not the scops owl’s single-note whistle.
    - >-
      Long-eared owl (Asio otus) is also a year-round resident on the 2024
      Georgian checklist; it too has ear tufts and orange eyes, but is much
      smaller and slimmer. This atlas does not yet have a separate page.
      Short-eared owl (Asio flammeus) is an open-country bird, often active by
      day, with short ear tufts; there is no separate page yet. Tawny owl
      (Strix aluco) is medium-sized, with no ear tufts and dark eyes; this
      atlas has a separate page. Little owl (Athene noctua) is small, with
      yellow eyes and no ear tufts; this atlas has a separate page. Eurasian
      scops owl (Otus scops) is small, tufted, and a breeding migrant in
      Georgia; this atlas has a separate page. Barn owl (Tyto alba) has a
      heart-shaped face and is pale; it is on the 2014 Red List and this atlas
      has a separate page. Tengmalm’s owl (Aegolius funereus) is a small forest
      owl with yellow eyes; this atlas has a separate page.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Do not handle it. A large owl with ear
      tufts and orange eyes in Georgia is this species, not little owl or tawny
      owl.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Strix bubo. Order Strigiformes, family
    Strigidae, genus Bubo. The English name is Eurasian Eagle Owl (2024
    checklist); world bird lists write Eurasian Eagle-Owl.
  - >-
    The subspecies expected in Georgia is B. b. interpositus (Rothschild &
    Hartert, 1910). Georgian dictionaries assign this form to Georgia
    (“southern eagle-owl”). The range of interpositus runs from Romania and
    southern Ukraine to Turkey, the Middle East and north-west Iran, including
    the Caucasus. Field identification is to species.
  - >-
    The usual Georgian name is ზარნაშო. ჩვეულებრივი ზარნაშო is also used.
    In Georgian, ჭოტი is sometimes wrongly treated as a synonym of eagle-owl —
    those are different species.
  - >-
    In Georgia it is a breeding resident, confirmed by photographs; no sound
    recording is part of that evidence. It is Least Concern both globally and
    nationally. That is country-level confirmation, not a regional map.
