commonName: Rock Dove
location: Year-round resident and breeder in Georgia
description: >-
  Rock Dove (Columba livia) breeds and remains in Georgia year round. Learn how to identify it, what the Georgian evidence shows, and how it differs from the woodpigeon.
overview: >-
  The Rock Dove (Columba livia) lives and breeds in Georgia year round. A typically coloured bird is blue-grey, with two black wingbars and a dark tail tip. City pigeons belong to the same species, and some descend from domestic pigeons. Plumage alone cannot tell whether a city bird has wild ancestry. It is not venomous.
habitat: >-
  The Rock Dove is also listed among the birds of the Alazani Valley. Rocky cliffs are the typical habitat of the wild form. Free-living city pigeons also use ledges on buildings and bridges and forage in open areas.
diet: >-
  It mainly eats seeds and grain found on the ground, sometimes fruit, and rarely invertebrates. In cities it also eats food left by people.
behavior: >-
  Active by day, it often walks and feeds in groups on the ground. Its call is a low coo. It nests on a cliff or building ledge or in a crevice. It usually lays two white eggs; both parents feed the chicks a milky food produced in their crops and regurgitated for them.
conservation: >-
  The 2024 scientific checklist gives the species global Least Concern (LC) status and a draft national LC assessment. The draft national assessment is not a legal protection status. Wild and domestic-derived pigeons can be hard to separate, so the number of city pigeons cannot be used to assess a genetically wild population.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous, but breathing dust from accumulated, dried droppings can cause infection. Leave nests and young undisturbed and do not catch wild birds.

identification:
  summary: >-
    In typical plumage, blue-grey with two black wingbars, a dark tail tip and often a green-purple sheen on the neck. Feral birds can be white, reddish, black or mottled; a bird without two wingbars can still be this species.
  traits:
    - >-
      About 30–36 cm long, with a stocky body, relatively small head and pointed wings. The two dark bars show even on a folded wing in typically coloured birds. Males and females look much alike.
    - >-
      The [common woodpigeon](columba-palumbus) is larger. Adults show white patches on the neck and broad white wing bands in flight. Juveniles may lack the neck patch, so check the wing too. The Stock Dove (Columba oenas) is closer in size but lacks the typical Rock Dove's two bold black wingbars. A Stock Dove profile is not yet available here.
    - >-
      The [European Turtle Dove](streptopelia-turtur) is slimmer, with a chestnut-and-black scaly pattern on the back. The Eurasian Collared Dove is paler and has a black half-collar on the nape; it does not yet have a profile here.
facts:
  - >-
    The accepted scientific name is Columba livia, in order Columbiformes and family Columbidae. Rock Pigeon is another English name. Feral Pigeon describes free-living descendants of domestic pigeons, not a separate species.
faq:
  - question: Does the Rock Dove occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 national scientific checklist lists it as a resident breeder present year round.
  - question: Where is it listed within Georgia?
    answer: >-
      It is listed among the birds of the Alazani Valley. City pigeons also occur in Georgia, although their wild ancestry cannot be established from location alone.
  - question: How can I tell it from a woodpigeon?
    answer: >-
      The woodpigeon is larger; adults have a white neck patch and a broad white wing band in flight. A typically coloured Rock Dove has two black wingbars.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      Adults are about 30–36 cm long.
  - question: Are city pigeons the same species?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Free-living city pigeons belong to Columba livia; some descend from domestic lines. Plumage alone does not prove wild ancestry.
  - question: What does it eat, and is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It mainly eats seeds and grain. It is not venomous, but avoid breathing dust from accumulated, dried droppings; leave nests and young undisturbed.
