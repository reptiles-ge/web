overview: >-
  The Caucasian green lizard (Lacerta media) is a large, harmless lacertid found
  in Georgia, mainly in the semiarid east. It belongs to the true lizard family
  (Lacertidae). It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. Other English
  names include medium lizard, three-lined lizard and Levant green lizard. The
  2026 Georgian checklist treats it as a candidate species: it is formally
  listed as a separate species, but the contact zone with the Lacerta trilineata
  group has not been studied well enough to settle rank. In this atlas it keeps
  its own profile and should not be treated as a settled species split. The
  nominotypical subspecies is L. m. media. The type locality is in Georgia: the
  lectotype is from the Kvabishevi valley near Borjomi, at about 1,025 m. IUCN
  lists it as Least Concern. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  It occurs in Georgia. Do not assume it in every region or in humid Colchic
  forest. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 place it widely in semiarid habitats of
  eastern Georgia and sporadically in the northwest, with medium distribution
  confidence. The checklist figure is from Tmogvi village (Aspindza, Samtskhe —
  Javakheti). Barcodes are from Tmogvi and Pantishara Gorge — Pantishara is in
  Kakheti, in the Dedoplistskaro area. This atlas map currently marks those
  named records; it does not mean the species is limited to two regions. No
  named northwest point is given on this page. The 2009 IUCN account names only
  northeastern Georgia; that is narrower than the 2026 checklist and is treated
  here as an older summary. The species was described in 1920 from Caucasian
  “green lizard” material; the syntype series included the Chorokh valley
  between Batumi and Artvin, Borjomi, the environs of Tbilisi, the Araks valley
  and other sites. Doronin and Doronina (2019) place the lectotype in the
  Kvabishevi valley. Look for it in bushy semiarid ground, river edges, and
  garden or field margins — not on barren desert. An Iranian synthesis (Šmíd et
  al. 2014) says the species avoids dry or arid habitats and uses grassy,
  shrubby, wooded valleys, field edges and gardens. IUCN lists woodland with
  scrub, grassland, rocky ground and cultivated land, and ties southern
  populations to moister sites. Species-level elevation is often given as about
  600–2,000 m; a measured Georgian range is not published on this page.
  Dagestan records run from 840–2,250 m — that is a Russian measurement, not a
  Georgian limit.
diet: >-
  This page has no Georgian stomach-content study. At species level it eats
  small invertebrates, mainly arthropods, and rarely a small vertebrate. That is
  a range-wide description, not a menu for every Georgian site.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. It is a large, fast ground Lacerta, often basking by
  bushes, stones or walls. A Georgian hourly and seasonal calendar has not been
  published separately. In a highland Turkish population activity is described
  from about mid-May to mid-September — that is a local window, not a Georgian
  calendar. At species level IUCN reports two clutches a year of about 9–18 eggs
  and maturity at about two years. Armenian populations have been noted with two
  clutches a year. Georgian clutch size and incubation are not measured here. If
  grabbed, the tail may break and the lizard may bite. Watch it and leave it
  where it is. Do not pick it up.
conservation: >-
  IUCN assessed it as Least Concern in 2009 (Agasyan and co-authors) because of
  a wide range and a presumably large population. It is not on Georgia’s 2014
  Red List (Resolution No. 190). Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 treat it as a
  candidate: separate status is supported indirectly by phylogeny and parapatry
  in western Anatolia, but the contact zone with the L. trilineata group remains
  unexplored (Ahmadzadeh et al. 2013). Cytochrome b difference from L.
  trilineata reaches 6.5% — that does not confirm species rank in this atlas.
  Cyrén raised it to species in 1933; later authors often kept it as a
  subspecies of L. trilineata until Schmidtler (1986) restored species rank.
  Local threats in Georgia are not measured on this page; habitat conversion is
  an obvious pressure. It is not listed on CITES. Do not catch wild lizards.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and poses no medical risk. If handled it may bite, and the
  tail may break. Leave it where it is. Do not keep a wild animal.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large ground Lacerta. Juveniles often show three longitudinal stripes;
    adults are often green. It is a candidate — colour alone is not enough.
  traits:
    - >-
      A robust lacertid with well-developed limbs — not a Darevskia rock lizard
      and not a skink. Adults are often plain green or olive-green; females may
      be more olive-brown, with lateral spots that fade with age. In Iranian
      material, adult males lack pale dorsal lines or spots. At species level
      snout–vent length is often about 10–12 cm; total length with the tail can
      reach about 40–50 cm. This page has no Georgian measurement series.
    - >-
      Juveniles in the L. trilineata complex often have three longitudinal
      stripes — hence the English name three-lined lizard. Young striped lizards
      (Lacerta strigata) usually have five narrow pale stripes. Green adults are
      easy to confuse; stripes fade with age. Colour alone is not enough.
    - >-
      The striped lizard (Lacerta strigata) is confirmed in Georgia and is
      described from semiarid habitats throughout the country. L. media is
      larger, a candidate, and mainly eastern semiarid, with sporadic records in
      the northwest. Where both occur, compare size, juvenile stripe count and
      the two profiles.
    - >-
      The sand lizard (Lacerta agilis) is widespread in western Georgia; in the
      east the 2026 checklist places it only above about 600–800 m. It is
      smaller. Young can be striped, but it is not the large green Lacerta of
      this complex.
    - >-
      The snake-eyed lizard (Ophisops elegans) is smaller, lives on open dry
      ground in the southeast, and has fused eyelids — the eye looks lidless.
      Rock lizards (Darevskia) are smaller and usually on rock or walls. None of
      these lizards is venomous.
