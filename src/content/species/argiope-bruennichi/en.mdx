commonName: Wasp spider
location: Western and eastern lowlands
description: >-
  A yellow-and-black banded spider that builds an orb web. It is native in
  Georgia and is not a medical risk to people.
overview: >-
  The wasp spider (Argiope bruennichi) is a spider that builds an orb web. In
  Georgian it is called არგიოპა, and also ბრუნიქის არგიოპა (“Brünnich's
  argiope”). Females are especially easy to recognize: their abdomen has bold
  yellow and black bands. These bands are where the English name “wasp spider”
  comes from.

  The species is widely distributed in Georgia and is considered native. It is
  described as a very common species in Georgia. This does not mean that it
  occurs in equal numbers in every region of the country: how many wasp spiders
  live in a given place depends on the local environment and habitat.

  The female's body is about 10–22 mm long, while the male is much smaller,
  about 7–8 mm. These measurements do not include the legs.

  The wasp spider has venom and uses it mainly to subdue prey, especially
  insects. For people, its bite is usually not considered a medically
  significant danger. Even so, do not handle the spider.

  Argiope bruennichi has not been assessed by the IUCN. The species is not
  included in Georgia's 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  The wasp spider is often found in low grass and meadows, where the female
  builds her orb web close to the ground. Its habitat is described above all as
  humid meadow vegetation. This is a general description of the species, not an
  elevation belt defined for Georgia.

  In Georgia, records of Argiope bruennichi are known from Tbilisi, Kvemo
  Kartli, Kakheti, Adjara, Guria, Imereti, Mtskheta–Mtianeti, Samegrelo and
  Abkhazia. Individual records also exist from Samtskhe–Javakheti, Shida Kartli
  and Racha–Lechkhumi–Kvemo Svaneti.

  This list does not mean that the wasp spider cannot occur in other regions.
  Likewise, “widely distributed in Georgia” does not mean that the wasp spider
  occurs equally in every habitat and at every elevation belt in the country.
diet: >-
  The wasp spider catches its prey in an orb web. It feeds mainly on various
  insects, including wasps, flies, grasshoppers and other fairly large
  invertebrates that get caught in the web. The spider usually wraps captured
  prey in silk first and then bites it.

  This describes the species in general. The wasp spider does eat insects, but
  it is wrong to treat it as a deliberate “biological control” tool against
  garden pests.
behavior: >-
  By day the female often sits in the centre of the web, head down. A white
  zigzag band, the stabilimentum, is often visible in the middle of the web. Why
  the spider makes this band is not fully settled. It may help attract prey,
  make the web more visible, or help other animals notice the web.

  Adult females become especially easy to spot in the second half of summer. In
  Georgia, for example, there is a record from 20 July 2006 near David Gareja.

  The female lays her eggs in a large, brown sac that looks like paper. The sac
  is flask-shaped and hangs in the vegetation near the web. The young spiders
  hatch before winter but do not leave the sac: they spend the winter inside it
  and come out in spring.

  The male is much smaller than the female. During the mating period he often
  appears near her web. The female may eat the male during mating. This is
  general species behaviour and has not been separately confirmed by observation
  in Georgia.

  There is no need to handle the wasp spider — it is better to watch it calmly
  at its web.
conservation: >-
  The IUCN has not assessed Argiope bruennichi globally, so its status is NE —
  Not Evaluated. At national level the species is assessed as LC*, but this is
  not an official state Red List status.

  Argiope bruennichi is not included in Georgia's 2014 Red List. Among
  arachnids, that list includes the fen raft spider (Dolomedes plantarius),
  which has VU — Vulnerable status.

  In northern Europe, the wasp spider has expanded its range in recent decades,
  spreading into new areas. Genetic studies treat the Caucasus as one of the
  important regions of diversity for this species. The samples behind this
  conclusion include samples from Georgia.

  Do not catch wild spiders; leave them in their natural environment.
interaction: >-
  The spider has venom for prey. It is not a medical risk to people: bites are
  rare and usually only local pain or redness. This is not a viper bite. An
  allergic reaction to any bite is possible; if breathing, the face, or general
  condition worsens, call 112. Do not handle it. A web in garden grass in summer
  is typical; destroy it only if it blocks a path — the spider is not a pest.

      mm. Legs are not included in this measurement. These are general species

    - >-
      On the upper side of the female's abdomen, bold white and yellow
      cross-bands stand out on a black or dark background. These bands give the
      species its typical “wasp-like” look.

      The male is much less colourful: mostly silvery-grey or yellowish, with

    - >-
      The wasp spider builds a large orb web, often in grass and fairly close to
      the ground. A white zigzag band, the stabilimentum, is often visible in
      the middle of the web. An adult female usually sits in the centre of the
      web, head down. A web like this, with its zigzag band and a head-down
      female, is one of the easiest ways to recognize the wasp spider in the
      field.
    - >-
      Georgia has another species in the same genus — Argiope lobata. Females of
      both species are large and sit in the centre of the web, so size and web
      posture are not enough to tell them apart. The female Argiope lobata has
      pronounced side projections, or lobes, on the abdomen. Its abdomen is a
      plain silvery grey, sometimes with brownish colour around the lobes. It
      does not have the bold yellow-and-black “wasp-like” bands typical of
      female Argiope bruennichi.

      These features apply mainly to females. Males of both species are small,
      with a plain yellowish or silvery-grey abdomen. Specialists tell the males
      apart by the structure of the pedipalp, the male's mating organ.

      Other orb-weaving spiders, including species of the genus Araneus, often
      have a cross-shaped or different pattern on the abdomen.

      The wasp spider is not a wasp: it has eight legs and no wings, and it
      usually sits in its own web.
    - >-
      The [lobed argiope (Argiope lobata)](argiope-lobata) has its own profile
      in this atlas.
  coloration: >-
    The female's cephalothorax is silvery and shiny. The upper side of her
    abdomen carries many white or yellow cross-stripes, and these stripes give
    the spider its wasp-like look. The underside of the abdomen is dark brown,
    with yellow lengthwise stripes and a few spots. The legs are yellowish with
    brown rings.

    The male is far less colourful: his abdomen is a single shiny colour,
    silvery grey or yellowish.

faq:
  - question: Is the wasp spider found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The wasp spider (Argiope bruennichi) is confirmed in Georgia and is
      considered a native species. This is an assessment for the country as a
      whole. It does not mean that the wasp spider is equally distributed in
      every region or habitat.
  - question: Where can you see the wasp spider in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The wasp spider is recorded in Tbilisi, Kvemo Kartli,
      Kakheti, Adjara, Guria, Imereti, Mtskheta–Mtianeti, Samegrelo–Zemo Svaneti
      and Abkhazia. Individual records are also known from Samtskhe–Javakheti,
      Shida Kartli and Racha–Lechkhumi–Kvemo Svaneti, but those regions do
      not yet have enough records to be shaded on the map.

      You are most likely to see it in summer in grassy places and meadows. At
      that time adult females become more conspicuous.
  - question: How can you recognize the wasp spider?
    answer: >-
      The female is easy to recognize by the bold yellow-and-black cross-bands
      on her abdomen. Her orb web often has a white zigzag band as well. The
      male is much smaller and plainer in colour.

      In Georgia the wasp spider can be confused with the lobed argiope (Argiope
      lobata). The lobed argiope female is also large and sits in the centre of
      the web. The difference is that she has pronounced lobes on the sides of
      the abdomen, and her abdomen is plain silvery grey, without the
      characteristic yellow-and-black bands. Males of both species are small and
      plain, so this difference applies mainly to females.
  - question: How big is the wasp spider?
    answer: >-
      The female's body length is about 10–22 mm, while the male is about 7–8

  - question: Is the wasp spider venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      Yes, the wasp spider has venom, which it uses to subdue prey. For people,
      its bite is not considered a medically significant danger. Bites are rare
      and usually cause only a local reaction.

      Do not handle the spider. If strong or unusual symptoms occur, call 112.
  - question: When can you see the wasp spider?
    answer: >-
      Adult females become especially conspicuous in the second half of summer.
      In Georgia, a July record is also known from the David Gareja area.
  - question: Is the wasp spider protected?
    answer: >-
