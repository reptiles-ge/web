description: >-
  Eastern Georgia’s high-risk venomous snake — Levantine viper; do not confuse
  with Montpellier snake.
overview: >-
  The Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetinus) is a large [venomous
  viper](/venomous-snakes) found in Georgia and is considered one of the
  country’s most medically significant snakes. It is mainly associated with dry
  and semi-arid habitats. In Georgia
  its range is no longer treated as limited only to the extreme south-east: in
  recent decades the species has also been confirmed in the Alazani valley and
  in Tbilisi. A natural range in western Georgia and in high mountain zones is
  not known. The accepted modern scientific name is Macrovipera lebetinus. Older
  literature uses names such as Vipera lebetina and Macrovipera lebetina, while
  Caucasian populations are usually assigned to M. l. obtusa. The species is
  confirmed in Georgia, including by genetic data. An adult Levantine viper is
  a large, heavy snake and commonly reaches about 1–1.5 m. It can sometimes be
  confused with the [Montpellier snake](malpolon-insignitus) and other large
  harmless snakes, so colour or size alone is not reliable for identification. A
  [Levantine viper bite](/snakes/giurzas-nakbeni) is a serious medical emergency.
  Do not approach the snake and do not try to catch or kill it. If bitten, call
  112 immediately.

  Levantine viper venom is associated with the haemorrhagic, coagulation-related
  and local tissue-damaging effects typical of medically important vipers. That
  is why a bite remains high-risk even if the first symptoms look limited. It is
  not a home-treatment situation: do not cut the bite, do not suck out venom, do
  not apply a tight tourniquet and do not lose time on folk remedies — call 112
  and use the [snakebite guide](/snakes/gvelis-nakbeni).
habitat: |-
  The gyurza is found in dry, rocky places. In Georgia, its typical habitat includes dry areas along the lower reaches of the Iori, Mtkvari and Alazani rivers, Shiraki, Gareji and the dry foothills of [Kvemo Kartli](/regions/kvemo-kartli). It is rare around [Tbilisi](/regions/tbilisi) and Rustavi. On this atlas’s map, the gyurza is marked in [Kakheti](/regions/kakheti), Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. Confirmed locations include Kvareli, the area around Nekresi Monastery, the Alazani Valley and the Colorful Desert.
  In Tbilisi, only a few isolated records appear on the map. Records associated with Rustavi mostly concern dry areas of Kvemo Kartli along the Mtkvari River. The atlas lists the Tbilisi Botanical Garden, Tabori, Nadzaladevi and Saburtalo for Tbilisi, and Rustavi, Gardabani and the Jandari Reservoir in the direction of Rustavi. Such a record confirms that a gyurza could have been encountered at a particular place in a particular year. It does not mean the snake is common or frequently seen in every part of the city.
  The gyurza shelters in rock crevices, spaces under stones and rodent burrows; it sometimes enters buildings. During intense summer heat, it may appear near water. According to Georgian sources, it is found up to an altitude of about 1,500 meters. Its natural range has not been confirmed in western Georgia. The high mountains of the Greater Caucasus and the Colchic forest are not its typical habitat.
diet: >-
  Adult gyurza snakes eat mainly rodents, but they also catch small birds, lizards and other small animals with backbones. Young gyurza snakes hunt small lizards more often. The gyurza usually hunts by ambush. It sometimes climbs a bush or a low tree, including to catch birds.
behavior: >-
  In Transcaucasia the Levantine viper’s active season usually lasts from April
  to late October, though some sources describe emergence from late March. In
  spring and autumn it often moves by day, while in summer heat it is more
  active at dusk and at night. It spends winter in rock crevices or burrows. In
  practical terms, “when is the Levantine viper active?” depends on weather and
  daily temperature: during cooler parts of the season it may be seen by day,
  while in very hot periods encounters are more likely in the evening, at night
  or near water. The
  Levantine viper lays eggs, which distinguishes it from Georgia’s other vipers.
  Mating mainly takes place in April–May. Georgian sources describe about 15–20
  eggs in summer. Hatchlings are about 23–25 cm long and emerge after roughly
  35–45 days. If you encounter a Levantine viper, keep a safe distance and do
  not try to catch it. The snake does not seek out people and usually defends
  itself only when it feels threatened.
conservation: >-
  The 2021 IUCN assessment treats the Levantine viper as Least Concern. It is
  not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List; among vipers, that list includes
  [Dinnik’s viper](vipera-dinniki) and the [Caucasus viper](vipera-kaznakovi).
  Older Georgian encyclopedic sources sometimes link the Levantine viper with
  the Red Book, but that does not match the official 2014 list. Important
  threats include habitat conversion,
  urbanisation, overgrazing and deliberate killing by people. By hunting rodents,
  the Levantine viper plays an important role in the food webs of dry landscapes.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The gyurza is a thick-bodied viper with a broad, triangular head and vertical pupils. Its scales have a raised ridge down the middle. In the dry lowlands of eastern Georgia, it is sometimes mistaken for large, nonvenomous racer snakes or the lizard-eating snake.
  traits:
    - >-
      The gyurza has a thick, heavy body. Its broad, triangular head is clearly distinct from its neck. Its snout is blunt, and it has no horn. In the Caucasus, adult gyurzas are often about 1–1.5 metres long. There are also reports of gyurzas reaching 2 metres, but these reports are disputed.
    - >-
      The pupil is shaped like a vertical slit. A prominent raised line runs down the middle of the scales on its back, making the back look rough. The scales on its head, including those above the eyes, are mostly small.
    - >-
      It may be gray, sand-colored or brownish. Its back often has dark spots or bands running across it. These sometimes form a zigzag pattern. Its belly is lighter and often covered with small dark speckles. Some have a more uniform color.
    - >-
      [The common lizard-eating snake](malpolon-insignitus) (Malpolon insignitus) has round pupils and a distinct “eyebrow” above each eye. It is slimmer than the gyurza and is active mainly during the day. [Large racers (Dolichophis)](dolichophis-schmidti) also have round pupils and more elongated bodies. They move quickly and do not have the gyurza’s thick, bulky build.
    - >-
      The [steppe viper](vipera-renardi) is also found in eastern Georgia, but it is much smaller than the gyurza, usually measuring about 40–60 cm long. The [nose-horned viper](vipera-transcaucasiana) is easy to recognize by the horn on its nose and is found in more mountainous areas. [Dinnik’s viper](vipera-dinniki) lives in the high mountains of the Greater Caucasus and is not found in the places where the gyurza usually lives.
faq:
  - question: Is the Levantine viper found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 Georgian checklist lists the Levantine viper as a
      biologically confirmed species in Georgia. Its natural range mainly covers
      the dry and semi-arid zones of eastern Georgia — it is not found
      throughout the whole country.
  - question: Are “gyurza”, “giurza” and გიუზა the same snake?
    answer: >-
      Yes — those spellings refer to the same species. In this atlas it is
      Macrovipera lebetinus (Levantine viper / გიურზა).
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, the Levantine viper is found mainly along the lower Iori,
      Mtkvari and Alazani, in Shiraki, Gareji and the dry foothills of Kvemo
      Kartli. It is rare around Tbilisi and Rustavi. On this atlas map the
      species is marked in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli and Tbilisi. Specific places
      include Kvareli, the area around Nekresi Monastery and the Colored Desert.
      A natural range in western Georgia is not confirmed.
  - question: Does the Levantine viper occur in Tbilisi or Rustavi?
    answer: >-
      Yes, but rarely and mostly in places linked to dry, open or rocky habitat.
      This atlas includes Tbilisi records from Tbilisi Botanical Garden, Tabori,
      Nadzaladevi and Saburtalo; in the Rustavi direction it includes Rustavi,
      Gardabani and Jandari Reservoir. These are confirmed places and years, not
      evidence that the species is common in every urban district.
  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to humans?
    answer: >-
      The Levantine viper is venomous and represents one of the highest medical
      risks among snakes found in Georgia. It is large and may inject a
      significant amount of venom when it bites. Its venom is associated with
      bleeding, disturbed blood clotting and local tissue damage. Other vipers
      are also venomous, but a Levantine viper bite is considered especially
      serious. It normally does not attack people and bites when it feels
      threatened, for example if someone comes too close, steps on it or tries
      to catch it. A bite requires immediate medical help and a call to 112.
  - question: What should I do if bitten?
    answer: >-
      If bitten by a Levantine viper, call 112 immediately. Lay the person down,
      keep them calm and try to limit movement. Do not cut the bite site, do
      not try to suck out venom and do not apply a tight tourniquet. Treatment
      must take place in a hospital. Antivenom, when needed, must be selected
      and used only by a doctor.
  - question: When is the Levantine viper active?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia and Transcaucasia it is usually active from April to late
      October; some sources describe emergence from late March. In spring and
      autumn it often moves by day, while in summer heat it is more likely at
      dusk or at night. Activity always depends on weather, temperature and the
      specific site.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      The gyurza is a large, thick-bodied snake. It has a broad, triangular head and vertically elongated pupils. Its scales have a raised ridge down the middle. It has no horn on its nose. Large racers and the lizard-eating snake have round pupils and slimmer, more elongated bodies.
  - question: Is the Montpellier snake the same as the Levantine viper?
    answer: >-
      No. The Montpellier snake (Malpolon insignitus) is not the Levantine
      viper. It is a rear-fanged species and is placed in the Moderate risk
      category in this atlas. The Levantine viper has a broad triangular head,
      a vertical pupil and a heavy body. The Montpellier snake is slimmer, with
      a round pupil and a marked “eyebrow” above the eye. Compare both species
      profiles for reliable separation. For any suspicious snakebite, call 112
      immediately.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      In the Caucasus, adult Levantine vipers commonly reach about 1–1.5 m. It
      is Georgia’s largest viper. Reports of lengths up to 2 m exist, but such
      data are debated.
  - question: Does it lay eggs or give live birth?
    answer: >-
      The Levantine viper lays eggs, which distinguishes it from Georgia’s
      Vipera vipers — they give live birth. Mating mainly takes place in
      April–May, and Georgian sources describe about 15–20 eggs in summer.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: >-
      Watch the snake from a safe distance and leave it where it is. Do not try to catch or kill it. If you give it way and do not disturb it, the gyurza will usually move away from you on its own.
