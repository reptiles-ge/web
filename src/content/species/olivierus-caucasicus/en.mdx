overview: >-
  The Caucasian scorpion (Olivierus caucasicus) is a buthid scorpion. The
  species was originally described from Georgia: the type locality is given as
  Tiflis, present-day Tbilisi.

  Older literature often treats this species as Mesobuthus caucasicus or under
  still older combinations. Current catalogues accept Olivierus caucasicus, while
  the 2018 revision separates the Caucasian taxon from the older broad Central
  Asian concept.

  On this page, only Tbilisi is mapped for Georgia because that is the precise
  locality confirmed by the sources. A general country-level record for Georgia
  is not expanded into a country-wide range claim.
habitat: >-
  Detailed, locally verified habitat data inside Georgia are still insufficient
  for this page. The type locality is Tbilisi, and the material discussed in the
  2018 revision belongs to the Caucasus and neighbouring dry regions.

  Habitat is therefore described cautiously: warm, dry, rocky or open places are
  plausible, with scorpions sheltering during the day. This does not mean the
  species occurs in every part of Tbilisi or in every dry habitat in Georgia.
diet: >-
  The diet of this species has not been described separately in detail for
  Georgia. Like other scorpions, the Caucasian scorpion is a predatory arthropod
  and likely feeds mainly on small invertebrates.

  It uses pincers to hold prey and the sting and venom to subdue it. This general
  scorpion biology is not a substitute for a Georgia-specific diet study.
behavior: >-
  Scorpions are mostly secretive. During the day they often hide under stones,
  in cracks, or in other protected places, while activity increases in the
  evening and at night.

  The Caucasian scorpion does not seek out people. Stings usually happen
  accidentally when a scorpion is touched, pressed, or disturbed in shelter.

interaction: >-
  The Caucasian scorpion is venomous. That does not mean it seeks people, but a
  sting can be painful and should be treated with caution.

  Do not pick it up. If pain after a sting is strong, the affected person is a
  child, allergic or other systemic symptoms develop, or the situation is
  unclear, call 112 and seek medical care.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium to fairly large yellowish-brown buthid scorpion with two pincers
    and a segmented tail ending in a sting. Exact identification from colour or a
    distant photo is not reliable.
  traits:
    - >-
      In the 2018 revision, adult males are about 50-55 mm long and females are
      58-75 mm long. This makes it larger and more conspicuous than Georgia's
      small Euscorpius species.
    - >-
      The description gives a yellowish-brown body, dark pigmentation on the
      carapace and tergites, and a dark anterior part of the fifth tail segment.
    - >-
      Many Buthidae scorpions have relatively slender pincers and a well
      developed tail. This is useful context, but final identification is better
      checked by a specialist.
    - >-
      When comparing it with the [mottled scorpion](mesobuthus-eupeus), size,
      pigmentation details, and taxonomic context may matter. A "yellow
      scorpion" label is not enough.
    - >-
      The [Italian scorpion](euscorpius-italicus) and [Mingrelian
      scorpion](euscorpius-mingrelicus) belong to another family, Euscorpiidae.
      Pincer build, tail proportions, and habitat context should be considered
      together.
facts:
  - >-
    The accepted scientific name is Olivierus caucasicus (Nordmann, 1840). The
    protonym is Androctonus caucasicus Nordmann, 1840.
  - >-
    In the 2018 revision, Mesobuthus caucasicus sensu stricto is restricted to
    the Caucasus; many older Central Asian records are assigned to other taxa.
  - >-
    The type locality is Georgia, Tiflis, now Tbilisi. This page uses only that
    confirmed administrative unit on the regional map.
  - >-
    Source-supported global range framing includes Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia,
    Iran, the northern Caucasus in Russia, Turkey, and Ukraine with uncertainty.
  - >-
    The English common name used here is a descriptive "Caucasian scorpion."
    Scientific sources often rely on the Latin name rather than a fixed English
    common name.
