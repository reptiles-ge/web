commonName: Eastern Montpellier Snake
location: South-eastern Georgia
description: >-
  The Eastern Montpellier Snake is a large daytime snake with raised “brows” and a round pupil. It is not a viper, but it does have venomous fangs at the back of its mouth. In Georgia it is confirmed in the dry south-east.
overview: >-
  The Eastern Montpellier Snake (Malpolon insignitus) is a large daytime snake, but it is not a viper: its venomous fangs sit at the back of the mouth. It lives in the dry east of Georgia. According to 2021 data, it occurs only in the semi-arid south-east, so do not look for it all over the country. A DNA-checked specimen comes from Chachuna Managed Reserve in Kakheti. It is usually less dangerous to people than a viper, but take any bite seriously. Worldwide it is listed as of least concern. Georgia's 2014 Red List rates it as vulnerable, under the older name Malpolon monspessulanus. The 2026 Georgian checklist includes it as a candidate species: whether it is a separate species is not yet settled. It belongs to a group of snakes closely related to M. monspessulanus.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia you may meet it in dry open country of the south-east: steppe,
  semi-desert, stony slopes, and sparse dry scrub. The named modern locality is
  Chachuna Managed Reserve on the Iori plateau in Kakheti. The 2021 survey
  groups it with the Levantine viper and the Urartian ratsnake — species of
  south-eastern semi-arid landscapes only. Do not look for it in western
  Georgia, Colchis forest, or the high mountains. This atlas map shows Kakheti
  and does not mean the species is evenly present across that region. This page
  has no named modern locality in other administrative regions. Species-level
  literature often cites about 0–1,400 m; a Georgia-only elevation range has
  not been published here. The wider range runs from the eastern Mediterranean
  and North Africa into the Caucasus; the type locality is Egypt, not Georgia.
diet: >-
  The snake's Georgian name comes from hunting lizards. Elsewhere in its range it mainly catches lizards, but it also eats other snakes, small mammals and birds. Its venomous fangs sit at the back of the mouth, and it uses them to overpower prey. These fangs are not meant for attacking people.
behavior: >-
  It hunts by day. It is a fast snake with good eyesight. Like most snakes, it is more often on the move early in the morning and before sunset. In Georgia it is expected to be active in the warm season, in dry, open places. It lays eggs. When threatened, it usually flees. If you corner it, it may raise the front of its body, puff itself up and hiss. This is self-defence, not a sign of kinship with cobras.
conservation: >-
  In Georgia this snake is considered Vulnerable: it is on the 2014 Red List because its range is small and fragmented. The list still uses the older name Malpolon monspessulanus and has not yet been updated to M. insignitus, but in practice it covers the same snakes in Georgia. Globally, the 2021 assessment rates it as of least concern, because it is widespread. Its “candidate” status in the 2026 checklist is about whether it is a separate species, not about how threatened it is. The same checklist rates the reliability of its distribution data as medium. Locally, it is threatened by the development of dry land, deaths on roads and killing by people who mistake it for a Levantine viper. Do not pick it up.
interaction: >-
  It is moderately dangerous to people, but it is not a viper: its venom fangs sit at the back of the mouth. So a brief bite may inject no venom at all, while a long hold can. A bite usually causes pain, swelling and redness only at the bite site. Effects on the whole body are rare and have been described mainly after bites of the western Malpolon monspessulanus. Do not pick it up. A vertical, slit-like pupil or a broad, triangular head may point to a Levantine viper. Other vipers and the European cat snake also have a vertical pupil. So give the snake room. If you are bitten, call 112.

  summary: >-
    A large, fast snake that is active by day. The first things you notice are its raised “brows” and round pupil. Head shape and pupil set it apart from the Levantine viper. It is not a viper: its venomous fangs sit at the back of the mouth. Check these features from a distance.
  traits:
    - >-
      The raised scales above the eye look like a “brow” and give the snake an “angry” look. The pupil is round, while a viper’s is a vertical slit. The body is slender but firm, and the head is narrow and elongated. The scales on the back are smooth, often in 17–19 rows at mid-body. Do not pick the snake up to count its scales.
    - >-
      The base colour is olive, grey or brownish, with a pale belly. Males often have a more even back with narrow pale stripes, while females have a more spotted or chequered pattern. Juveniles are more spotted. Males of the western M. monspessulanus have a dark “saddle”, but males of this species usually lack it.
    - >-
      The Levantine viper (Macrovipera lebetinus) is a venomous viper, and its bite is a serious health risk. It is heavier-bodied, with a broad triangular head, a vertical pupil and a thin ridge running down the middle of each scale. The red-bellied racer (Dolichophis schmidti) is non-venomous and has no raised brow. Adults often have a red or orange belly. The spotted whip snake (Hemorrhois ravergieri) lacks the raised brow too, but has diamond-shaped blotches along its whole back. Check these features from a distance: never move closer to a snake to see its pupil or scales.
    - >-
      If a slender snake has a vertical pupil, it is not an Eastern Montpellier Snake: it may be a viper, a Levantine viper or a European cat snake. Large harmless racers are fast too, but they lack the raised Malpolon “brow”.
    - >-
      According to 2021 data, in Georgia it lives only in the semi-arid south-east, so do not look for it across the whole country. facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1827 (Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire) as Coluber insignitus; the type
    locality is Egypt. Older Georgian and Soviet literature usually used
    Malpolon monspessulanus. A 2006 study treats the eastern form as a
    separate species, M. insignitus. The 2026 checklist leaves full species
    rank as a candidate: the contact zone in north-eastern Morocco has not been
    studied at population level, and a hybrid has been reported.
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist barcode is from Chachuna (CaBOL-ID 1027416). That paper
    assigns Georgian material to the nominotypical subspecies M. i. insignitus;
    Other taxonomic catalogues often place Caucasian populations in M. i.
    fuscus.
    This page does not settle the subspecies.
  - >-
    The Georgian name reflects hunting lizards. The English name distinguishes
    it from western M. monspessulanus. It is not a cobra: raising the front of
    the body is a defensive display.
faq:
  - question: Is the eastern Montpellier snake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. This snake is included in the 2026 checklist. A DNA-checked specimen comes from Chachuna Managed Reserve. It does not occur across the whole country, though, so don't expect to find it everywhere.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia, it lives only in semi-arid, open country in the south-east. Among recent sightings, the one locality known precisely is in Kakheti, around Chachuna. No such locality has been named in other regions. Do not look for it in western Georgia, in the Colchis forests or in the high mountains.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      It is moderately dangerous to people, but it is not a viper: its venomous teeth sit at the back of its mouth. A bite usually affects only the bitten area and is milder than a viper bite. If a snake has a vertical pupil, it may be a Levantine viper, another viper or a European cat snake. Do not pick it up. Still, take any bite seriously and call 112.
  - question: How do I tell it from the Levantine viper?
    answer: >-
      By its head, pupil and scales. This snake has a narrow head, a round pupil and raised "brows", and its scales are smooth. The Levantine viper has a thick, heavy body, a broad triangular head and a vertical pupil, and each of its scales has a thin ridge down the middle. If you are not sure which snake it is, keep your distance.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Up to about 1.5–2 m: that is the length often given for males across the species' whole range. A 2021 review of Georgia's snakes, based on 1977 data, places it among medium-sized snakes. Snakes in that group reach a maximum body length of 70–120 cm. So 1.5–2 m applies to the species as a whole, while 70–120 cm comes from the Georgian review.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It hunts lizards, and that is where its Georgian name, "lizard-eating snake", comes from. In other parts of its range it mainly catches lizards, but it also eats other snakes, small mammals and birds.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is a day-active snake that lays eggs. In Georgia it can be expected to be active in the warm season, in dry, open places, more often in the morning and towards evening.
  - question: What should I do if I meet one?
    answer: >-
      Watch it from a distance, take a photo and leave it alone. Do not pick it up. If the snake has a vertical pupil or a broad, triangular head, it may be a Levantine viper, so give it room to go. If you are bitten, call 112.
