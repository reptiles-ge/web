description: >-
  A large burrowing wolf spider confirmed in Georgia. The name “tarantula” here
  means a large Lycosa wolf spider, not a tropical pet tarantula.
overview: >-
  The South Russian tarantula or Songarian wolf spider (Lycosa singoriensis) is
  a wolf spider in the family Lycosidae. The accepted name is Lycosa
  singoriensis (Laxmann, 1770); the World Spider Catalog treats it as accepted.
  Historical synonyms include Aranea singoriensis and other older combinations,
  but the current name is Lycosa singoriensis. The word tarantula can mislead:
  this is an araneomorph wolf spider, not a theraphosid pet tarantula. Nentwig
  et al. give male body length as 14–27 mm and female as 18–31 mm. Otto 2022
  lists the species for Georgia, and Seropian et al. 2023 publish a record from
  [Kvemo Kartli](/regions/kvemo-kartli). That confirms presence; it does not
  mean even density across all Georgian regions.
habitat: >-
  The species is tied to dry, open landscapes: steppe or semi-steppe grassland,
  dry plains, and soil where it can dig a burrow. That ecology fits the dry
  plains and foothills of Kvemo Kartli, but this atlas colours only the region
  with a named sourced record. A country checklist confirms presence in Georgia;
  it is not a claim that the spider should be expected in every region.
diet: >-
  This is an active predator, not an orb-web spider. It takes prey, mainly
  insects and other small invertebrates, on the ground or near the burrow
  entrance. No separate Georgian diet series is cited on this page, so the prey
  wording stays at the general Lycosidae ecological level.
behavior: >-
  Large Lycosa species often live in silk-lined soil burrows and hunt from the
  entrance. As in other wolf spiders, the female carries the egg sac on the
  spinnerets, and newly hatched spiderlings spend some time on the mother’s
  back. A full Georgian seasonal calendar is not available here. If you meet one
  in a dry open place, do not handle it.
conservation: >-
  IUCN has not assessed the species. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. This
  page has no data measuring a Georgian population trend. The record matters
  because “tarantula” is often used vaguely online; here the name is attached to
  a specific sourced species.
interaction: >-
  The spider has venom for prey. For people, it is not usually treated as a
  medically significant species. A [bite](/spiders/obobis-nakbeni) is possible
  and can hurt, especially if the spider is pressed or picked up, so handling is
  not recommended. Do not catch it or force a defensive bite. If pain, swelling,
  breathing difficulty, dizziness, or general condition worsens after a bite,
  call 112.

  traits:
    - >-
      At species level Nentwig et al. give male body length as 14–27 mm and
      female body length as 18–31 mm. Legs are not included.
    - >-
      The body is hairy, the legs are ringed, and the abdomen is dark and
      spotted. Wolf spiders have a distinctive eye arrangement, with the large
      middle pair visible from the front.
    - >-
      The web is not an orb web. A large yellow-and-black spider sitting in an
      orb web points toward [Argiope](argiope-bruennichi), not Lycosa.
    - >-
      The [karakurt](latrodectus-tredecimguttatus) (Latrodectus
      tredecimguttatus) is a small glossy cobweb spider with a globular abdomen.
      The South Russian tarantula is a strong ground-living wolf spider. Neither
      should be handled, but they are not the same risk category.

faq:
  - question: Is the South Russian tarantula found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Otto 2022 lists the species for Georgia, and Seropian et al. 2023

  - question: Is this the same as a pet tarantula?
    answer: >-
      No. Here “tarantula” means Lycosa singoriensis, a large wolf spider. It is

    answer: >-

  - question: Is it dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      It is not usually treated as a medically significant species. A bite is
      possible and can be painful, so handling is not recommended. If serious
      symptoms develop, call 112.
  - question: Where should I look for it?
    answer: >-
      In dry open places with soil suitable for burrows. The sourced mapped
      region in this atlas is Kvemo Kartli; other regions are not coloured on
      this page.
  - question: How do I tell it from Argiope?
    answer: >-
      Argiope sits in a large orb web and is often boldly yellow and black.
      Lycosa singoriensis is a ground-living wolf spider and does not build a
      capture orb web.
