commonName: "Eastern Crested Newt"
location: "forest ponds and lakes"
description: >-
  A harmless crested newt with coarse skin and a dark-spotted belly. Confirmed
  in western Georgia and the forest belt of eastern Georgia.
overview: >-
  The eastern crested newt (Triturus karelinii) is a harmless crested newt and
  is biologically confirmed in Georgia. It belongs to the salamander family
  Salamandridae. It is not dangerous to people: it has no venomous bite. Adults
  usually reach about 15–18 cm; separate Georgian measurements are not given on
  this page. In the field this is Georgia’s largest newt — coarse-skinned, with
  a dark-spotted belly — not the smooth newt and not the banded newt. The 2026
  Georgian checklist treats it as a member of the T. cristatus complex. English
  names include eastern crested newt, southern crested newt and Karelin’s
  crested newt. IUCN lists it as Least Concern. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red
  List.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia this is a forest-belt newt of ponds, small lakes and slow water.
  When breeding, look for it in fish-free or lightly stocked ponds, small lakes,
  slow stream pools and sometimes ditches. On land it hides under stones, logs
  and leaf litter. It tolerates drier ground better than Georgia’s other newts;
  a 1996 review calls it a rather xerophilous form. In the humid forests of
  western Transcaucasia it is rare and populations are usually small. The same
  review treats it as absent from the extreme south-west of Georgia. The Batumi
  record (Djanashvili 1956) is regarded as erroneous.

  The 2026 checklist states: throughout western Georgia and the forest belt of
  eastern Georgia. Barcoded specimens are from Gogni and Tskaltubo (Imereti).
  The checklist figure is Tkibuli. Distribution confidence in the checklist is
  medium. This atlas map follows named literature sites and does not mean the
  species is equally present today in every highlighted region.

  Tarkhnishvili 1996 lists 33 Georgian localities, including Poti, Senaki,
  Sukhumi, Gagra, Pskhu, Ritsa, Kutaisi, Ajameti, Kobuleti, Baniskhevi, Borjomi,
  Bakuriani, Didi Mitarbi, Surami, the Tana valley, the Liakhvi reserve,
  Ertatsminda, Pitsesi Lake (Kaspi), Betania, Akhaldaba Lake near Tbilisi,
  Tsodoreti, Saguramo, Satovle, Chili-Tba, Chiantba near Telavi, Batsara,
  Lagodekhi, Lake Duruji and Bursa valley (Kvareli). Also named are Sakochavi
  Lakes, the village Tba, Kodiani and Ajara (?). This page has no separately
  named site for Guria or Racha — Lechkhumi — Kvemo Svaneti. The map marks
  Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti,
  Shida Kartli, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Kakheti and Tbilisi.

  At species level the elevation reaches about 2,100 m in the southern Caucasus.
  A separate elevation table for every Georgian population is not given here.
  Under the current species concept the wider range is Crimea, the Caucasus and
  the southern Caspian shore. The type locality is in Iran (Gulf of Gorgan), not
  Georgia.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. At species level,
  aquatic adults take water invertebrates — snails, crustaceans, insect larvae —
  and sometimes terrestrial invertebrates that have fallen in. On land they take
  worms, slugs, spiders and insects. Larvae start on tiny crustaceans, then take
  larger prey. Do not treat this as a measured menu for every Georgian
  population.
behavior: >-
  It is in water when breeding; at other times it is often on land. Activity is
  mainly at night. A full calendar for every Georgian population is not given
  here. At species level, hibernation usually runs from September–October to
  March, on land or sometimes in deep still water. Breeding is March–May at low
  elevation and May–July in the highlands; after a warm winter it may start in
  February. Eggs are wrapped singly in water plants; species-level clutches of
  about 100–250 eggs are described. In Georgia, AmphibiaWeb places the peak of
  larval hatching from late May through June. Metamorphosis is August–October;
  some larvae overwinter in water.

  If you find one, watch it and leave it where it is. Do not pick it up or move
  it between water bodies — that spreads fungal disease. Skin glands produce a
  defensive secretion; the orange, spotted belly is a warning colour. Wash your
  hands after any contact. It is not a medical risk to people.
conservation: >-
  The 2026 checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. IUCN (2023) lists it
  as Least Concern; that assessment covers today’s T. karelinii (Crimea,
  Caucasus, Caspian), not the Balkan T. ivanbureschi. It is not on Georgia’s
  2014 Red List; the only amphibians there are the Caucasian salamander and the
  Syrian spadefoot. AmphibiaWeb notes decline on the Black Sea coast and a
  severe status at some western Caucasus sites; that is a regional remark, not
  Georgia’s official category. Main threats are pond loss or infilling, fish
  stocking, forest cutting and pollution. Do not collect it.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is medically harmless to people. Skin secretion can
  irritate. Do not pick it up.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Georgia’s largest newt. The skin is coarse. The belly is yellow or orange
    with large dark spots. Breeding males grow a high, jagged crest. Do not
    confuse it with the smooth newt or the banded newt.
  traits:
    - >-
      The body is stocky, not slender. The skin is coarse and granular. In water
      the tail is flattened from side to side. Adults usually reach about 15–18
      cm in total length; females are often slightly larger than males. Separate
      Georgian measurements are not given on this page.
    - >-
      The back is brown to dull grey with darker patches. The belly and throat
      are yellow or orange with black spots; the spots may merge so that part of
      the belly looks black. There is no pale flank stripe. Females often have a
      narrow yellow line along the middle of the back.
    - >-
      In the breeding season the male grows a high, jagged crest from the back
      onto the tail. The gap in the crest at the tail base is often weak or
      absent — unlike the northern crested newt (T. cristatus), which does not
      occur in Georgia. Females lack this crest.
    - >-
      Lantz’s smooth newt (Lissotriton lantzi) is smaller (usually about 7–11
      cm), with smoother skin and only a low male crest. The northern banded
      newt (Ommatotriton ophryticus) has a pale flank stripe and an unspotted
      belly. The Caucasian salamander (Mertensiella caucasica) has yellow spots,
      a round tail in cross-section, and lives by streams, not ponds.
    - >-
      Older books listed Triturus cristatus karelinii. Today T. karelinii is a
      separate species of Crimea, the Caucasus and the southern Caspian. Balkan
      and western Anatolian crested newts are T. ivanbureschi and T. anatolicus
      and do not occur in Georgia. In the field they are almost impossible to
      tell apart by looks; in Georgia geography is enough.

    answer: >-

      the wooded belt of the east. It is scarce in humid western forest. The map
      marks several regions, not only Imereti. Not every old site has been
      rechecked.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      No. It has no venom and is harmless to people. Skin secretion can
      irritate. Do not pick it up. Wash your hands after contact.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A large, coarse-skinned newt with a yellow or orange belly marked with
      dark spots. It has no pale flank stripe. Breeding males have a high,
      jagged crest. The smooth newt is smaller; the banded newt has a flank
      stripe and an unspotted belly.

    answer: >-
