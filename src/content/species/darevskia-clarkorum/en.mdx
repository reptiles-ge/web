overview: >-
  Clark's rock lizard (Darevskia clarkorum) is a rock lizard that lives in Adjara, in southwestern Georgia. A 2009 global assessment classed it as endangered, and Georgia's 2014 Red List places it in the same category. It has no venom and is not dangerous to people. It belongs to the true lizard family (Lacertidae). Georgia's 2026 species checklist treats it as a confirmed species. The new DNA data and images of its head scales come from the Charnali Valley in Khelvachauri Municipality. This means it lives in Adjara, but not that it occurs in every valley of the region or across Georgia. The species was first described not in Georgia but in Turkey: at the Cankurtaran Pass between Hopa and Borçka, in Artvin. Georgia's 2014 Red List lists it under the name “Turkish lizard”, while the 2026 checklist calls it the Lazian lizard. In English it is known as Clark's rock lizard or Clark's lizard.
habitat: |-
  Clark's Rock Lizard lives in moist, shrubby places near streams, among stones, protruding rocks and loose rock. It also occurs in rocky mountain meadows. In Adjara it lives on the slopes of a wooded valley and along stony streams, not in dry steppe.

  In Georgia, its DNA data and an image published in a 2026 work come from the Colchian Charnali Valley. A 2009 assessment also names the western slopes of Mtirala in Adjara, near Batumi: a small patch isolated from the species' main range. The records do not show whether Charnali and Mtirala are one and the same place. A 2021 note says the lizard may reach as far north as the Kintrishi Valley. That report rests on personal communication, and the Kintrishi Valley is not named in the checklist. Information on its range is moderately reliable and does not give a picture of its distribution across the whole country. Do not assume it lives in Kakheti, Javakheti or the Greater Caucasus.

  Sources give elevations that do not match. A 2009 assessment gives 1,500–2,200 m, a 1997 description about 900–1,700 m. Turkish data include lower places too, at about 450–2,250 m.

  The 2009 assessment, based on a 1998 work, also mentions Armenia. The 2026 checklist and other recent reviews do not list Armenia among the countries where this lizard lives. So do not assume it occurs in Armenia.
diet: >-
  It probably eats small insects and similar small animals, like other Darevskia rock lizards. It catches prey with its jaws, without venom. In Turkey, one was once recorded eating a plant, but that tells us nothing about its diet in Georgia.
behavior: |-
  It is active by day and lives on rocks. It warms itself on stones and shelters in crevices. In one study in northeastern Turkey, the lizards were caught between 08:30 and 18:30, often in cloudy or rainy weather.

  It lays eggs. Lizards studied in Turkey start breeding at 1–3 years of age, depending on the elevation where they live. The oldest age reported for them is 10–12 years. All these figures are approximate and refer to lizards in Turkey. They are not averages for lizards in Georgia, and they do not show what hours of the day the lizards are active here. If you catch one, it may lose its tail and may also bite you. So don't pick it up: watch it and leave it where it is.
conservation: |-
  In a 2009 global assessment, Clark's Rock Lizard was classed as Endangered. At the time, its known range covered less than 5,000 km², it had been recorded at fewer than five sites, and overgrazing was degrading its habitat. That assessment is now old. Georgia's 2014 Red List (Resolution No. 190) also lists it as Endangered, based on the same assessment. A note in the resolution, however, says it is considered Vulnerable worldwide, which does not match the 2009 assessment.

  The same 2009 assessment treats the Charnali form as a separate species, Darevskia dryada, and lists it as Critically Endangered. In the 2026 checklist, Clark's Rock Lizard is a biologically confirmed species, and dryada is its subspecies. Even so, the 2009 assessment points to a serious threat to the lizards at Charnali. In 2019, the number of lizards in one study plot at Charnali was estimated at about 256 ± 48. This covers only part of the valley and is not a count for the whole country. Construction and habitat loss were named there as local threats. Clark's Rock Lizard is not listed under CITES. Do not catch wild lizards.

  traits:
    - >-
      This is a rock lizard of the genus Darevskia. Its legs are well developed, and it lives on stones and cliffs. It is not one of the large, ground-dwelling Lacerta lizards. In lizards studied in Turkey, the back is ivy green, light green, brown or grayish brown, often with small dark dots. A dark stripe runs along the side of the head, near the temple. This stripe may carry several spots with pale centers. There may also be a single blue spot near a front leg. The belly is often bright yellow. This look is typical, but color alone will not tell you which species you are seeing.
    - >-
      Many lizards were measured in Turkey. In approximate figures, head and body length was 4.4–6.7 cm, 5.9 cm on average, and one male measured 18.7 cm in total, including the tail. The first measurements gave a slightly higher length. The tail is often longer than the body. A 2021 study found that the dryada form from Charnali is larger than the typical clarkorum form: its males have longer heads and more scales on the back.
    - >-
      In the 2026 list, the lizards of southwestern Georgia are still listed as D. c. dryada. This is a geographic subspecies: a form of the same species that lives in a particular area, not a separate species. A 2021 study treats dryada as a distinct group and most likely a subspecies. A 2002 work, by contrast, regarded it as a form linked to environmental conditions. Figures in one of the studies show the head scales and the plates in front of the vent in lizards from Charnali. In the field, colour alone will not settle this question.
    - >-
      The Ajarian Rock Lizard (D. adjarica) is listed as a candidate species from the parvula group. The DNA data given for it in the same list come from Shuakhevi, not Charnali. Derjugin's Lizard (D. derjugini) is a smaller forest species: it lives on the ground among leaves and grass and is not a typical rock lizard. The Mixed Rock Lizard (D. mixta) is a close relative, but the list does not include it as a species found in Adjara. In southwestern Georgia, do not identify lizards by color alone.
    - >-
      The 2026 list treats Clark's Rock Lizard as a biologically confirmed species. The recent DNA data and images of its head scales come from the Charnali Valley in Khelvachauri Municipality. These records place it in Adjara, but that does not mean it lives in every valley there. Do not assume it occurs across all of Georgia. The same list names the Ajarian Rock Lizard (D. adjarica) as a candidate species, but its DNA data come from Shuakhevi, not Charnali. When identifying a lizard, also consider which valley you saw it in.

  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-

The Ajarian Rock Lizard (D. adjarica) belongs to the parvula group and is found in Shuakhevi, while Derjugin's Lizard (D. derjugini) lives on the forest floor. In the list’s illustrations, the scales on the head are a more reliable clue than the color of the back.
