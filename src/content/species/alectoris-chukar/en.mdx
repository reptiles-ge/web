overview: >-
  The Chukar Partridge (Alectoris chukar) is a ground-dwelling bird in the pheasant family, Phasianidae. Georgia's 2024 annotated bird checklist confirms it as a breeding, year-round resident, supported by photographs, sound recordings and a hunter-collected bird. In this page's map table, Kakheti and Tbilisi have confirmed distribution status; Mtskheta–Mtianeti and Samtskhe–Javakheti have individual records only. The National Wildlife Agency also names the Iori Plateau, upper Mtkvari valley and Kvernaki Ridge. Adults are about 34–38 cm long. A red bill, pale throat enclosed by a black band and strongly barred flanks are the most useful marks together. It poses no direct danger to people. Its global IUCN category is Least Concern; Georgian sources disagree about the national category, as explained below.
habitat: >-
  The map treats only regions with confirmed status in its records table as distribution: Kakheti and Tbilisi qualify, while Mtskheta–Mtianeti and Samtskhe–Javakheti have records only. This regional picture comes from public iNaturalist observations, not a complete historical range. The National Wildlife Agency describes the Iori Plateau, upper Mtkvari valley, Kvernaki Ridge and both Caucasus ranges; the Georgian Encyclopaedia names further ridges. These literature reports do not automatically add regions to the map. In the field, look for Chukar on dry, rocky slopes with scattered shrubs. This page has no verified Georgia-specific elevational limit. Give any nest space.
diet: >-
  It eats plant seeds, shoots, buds and insects. This page does not present a separate study of how much each makes up of its diet in different parts of Georgia.
behavior: >-
  Chukars usually walk or run over rocky slopes rather than fly; when startled, they take a short, powerful flight. Their repeated, loud chuck-like calls can help with identification. The audio on this page is a flight call recorded by Stanislas Wroza on Lesvos, Greece, not in Georgia. The 2024 checklist confirms breeding and year-round presence in Georgia, without giving a local activity calendar. According to the National Wildlife Agency, laying begins in April on the Iori Plateau and may start later at higher sites. Nests are on the ground. The agency's average of 7–12 eggs and incubation of 22–25 days are general species figures, not measurements from all Georgian nests.
conservation: >-
  The 2016 IUCN global assessment classifies Chukar as Least Concern (LC). Georgia's 2024 bird checklist and the National Wildlife Agency also give LC as the national category; the agency says the species is not on the Georgian Red List. Yet a table in the 2023 Chachuna Managed Reserve plan gives the national category as Endangered (EN). The reason for this conflict has not been established here, so neither category should be treated as a settled legal designation. The agency lists poaching and habitat degradation and fragmentation as local threats. These sources do not establish a national population trend.
interaction: >-
  The chukar poses no danger to people. Watch it from a distance, and do not catch the bird or its chicks. Old information about hunting does not mean that hunting it is currently permitted: the chukar is not on the list of game birds published by the Department of Environmental Supervision. Before deciding to hunt or take a chukar, check the current rules with the relevant authority.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a medium-sized, stocky bird that lives on the ground. Its beak and legs are red. A black stripe runs from the eye along the sides of the neck, framing its pale throat. Bold black stripes mark the pale feathers on its flanks. Together, these features are especially helpful for identifying it in rocky places.
  traits:
    - >-
      An adult bird is about 34–38 cm long. This is a general size for the species, not a range based on individual birds measured in Georgia. Its back is gray-brown, its throat is pale, and its sides have black stripes. Males are slightly larger on average, but size alone is not a reliable way to tell a bird’s sex. The bold markings typical of adults may be less noticeable on young birds.
    - >-
      The [quail](coturnix-coturnix) (Coturnix coturnix) is much smaller than the chukar. It has mottled stripes on its back and usually hides in grass. The chukar has a red beak and a black band around its pale throat. The [pheasant](phasianus-colchicus) (Phasianus colchicus) is larger, and adults have long tails. The grey partridge (Perdix perdix) is also found in Georgia. It, too, is a short-tailed bird that lives on the ground, but unlike the chukar, it has no black ring around its throat or bold vertical stripes on its sides. The atlas does not yet have a separate page for the grey partridge.
