commonName: "Mediterranean Tortoise"
location: "Eastern Georgia (T. g. ibera); west — rare (T. g. nikolskii)"
description: >-
  Georgia’s only land tortoise. Not venomous; a high-domed shell and a spur on
  each thigh.
overview: >-
  The Mediterranean tortoise (Testudo graeca) is Georgia’s only land tortoise.
  Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 list it as a native, biologically confirmed species.
  It has no venom. T. g. ibera is widespread in eastern Georgia; T. g. nikolskii
  was described from western Georgia and is considered rare. In measured eastern
  Georgian animals, mean straight carapace length is 17.9 cm in males
  (15.2–19.8 cm) and 21.0 cm in females (15.0–25.0 cm); an older Georgian maximum
  is 26 cm. Georgia’s 2014 Red List treats it as Vulnerable (VU). IUCN also lists
  the species as VU; that global assessment dates from 1996. A barcoded specimen
  is from Tbilisi — a locality, not a full-country map.
habitat: >-
  It lives on land in open, dry country: steppe, shrub, semi-desert, and the
  edge of light woodland. It is not a pond turtle. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026:
  T. g. ibera is widespread in eastern Georgia; T. g. nikolskii was described
  from the west and is rare. Named points cited by Türkozan et al. 2023 from
  Chkhikvadze 2009 and Auer & Herz 2021 are Gori (Shida Kartli), Tbilisi,
  Bolnisi (Kvemo Kartli), and Vashlovani National Park (Kakheti, near the
  Azerbaijan border). Muskhelishvili 1970 gives 800–1000 m — a literature
  elevation, not a range re-measured for this atlas. Records near Kobuleti may
  be translocated animals; Adjara is not coloured on this map. The Tbilisi
  barcode is one specimen site. Do not assume even density across the country,
  including humid Colchic forest. A municipality-by-municipality list is not
  compiled here.
diet: >-
  It is mainly herbivorous: grasses, herbs, and flowers. In eastern parts of
  the species range, animal matter is recorded only rarely — that is not a
  Georgian stomach study. A separate diet analysis for Georgia is not published
  on this page. Do not feed wild animals.
behavior: >-
  It is terrestrial. In warm weather it is active by day; in heat it often
  shelters at the base of shrubs, in burrows, or under other cover. It does not
  swim like Georgia’s pond, Caspian, or slider turtles. An hourly or monthly
  calendar has not been published for Georgia on this page. Nearby Caucasus
  literature (Dagestan, Armenia) describes activity from about March to October
  — an analogue, not a Georgian field calendar. It lays eggs. For eastern
  subspecies clades, females produce 1–3 clutches a year, often in May–July,
  with 2–19 eggs per clutch — a species-level range, not a Georgian count.
  Overwintering (brumation) is documented in northern and montane populations
  of the species; Georgian dates are not given separately here. Do not pick it
  up.
conservation: >-
  A native, biologically confirmed species. Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Resolution
  No. 190) lists it as Vulnerable (VU), citing IUCN as the source of the
  category. Globally IUCN also lists it as VU (assessment 1996); the European
  regional assessment is VU (2009). TFTSG 2023 notes that the global assessment
  is outdated. It is on CITES Appendix II as a member of Testudinidae — take
  and trade of wild animals is regulated. T. g. nikolskii is treated as rare on
  the checklist; a separate national category for that subspecies is not cited
  here. At species level the main pressures are habitat loss, fire, agriculture,
  and collection for pets or trade. A national population trend is not
  quantified on this page. Do not take a wild tortoise home.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and does not hunt people. It may bite if handled. The shell
  and legs are easily damaged. Step aside if it is on a road, and leave it
  where it is.

identification:
  summary: >-
    Georgia’s only land tortoise. A high-domed shell, columnar legs, and thigh
    spurs separate it from pond turtles.
  traits:
    - >-
      High-domed shell, not the flatter shell of a freshwater turtle. Colour
      ranges from yellowish-brown to dark brown, often with darker marks on the
      scutes. The exact pattern varies among individuals and is not enough to
      assign a subspecies in the field.
    - >-
      Columnar legs with five claws in front and four behind. No webbed swimming
      feet. The head is rounded; the mouth is a horny beak, with no teeth.
    - >-
      Usually one small conical keratinous spur on each thigh — the
      “spur-thighed” field mark. Georgia has no other land tortoise, so shell
      and legs are enough to separate it from water turtles.
    - >-
      In eastern Georgia (Auer & Herz 2021) mean carapace length is 17.9 cm in
      males and 21.0 cm in females. Females are generally larger; males have a
      longer tail and a concave plastron. Juveniles are smaller, often with a
      stronger contrast in the pattern.
    - >-
      The European pond turtle (Emys orbicularis), Caspian turtle (Mauremys
      caspica), and introduced slider (Trachemys scripta) are freshwater
      species: flatter shells, webbed feet, often basking on banks. T. s.
      elegans has a broad red stripe behind the eye. Testudo hermanni does not
      occur in Georgia.
    - >-
      The checklist keeps two subspecies: T. g. ibera (east) and T. g. nikolskii
      (west, rare). Van der Kuyl et al. 2005 distinguish only graeca and ibera
      clearly; TFTSG 2023 treats nikolskii as a synonym of ibera. Tarkhnishvili
      et al. 2026 keep the name for phenotype and a distinct range; genetic
      distinction is not yet demonstrated. Do not assign a wild Georgian animal
      to a subspecies with certainty.
faq:
  - question: Is it found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Tarkhnishvili et al. 2026 list it as a native, biologically confirmed
      species. It is Georgia’s only land tortoise.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Mainly in dry, open country in the east. Named points include Gori,
      Tbilisi, Bolnisi, and Vashlovani. It is rare in the west. The map does not
      cover the whole country.
  - question: Is this a water turtle?
    answer: >-
      No. It is a land tortoise. Pond, Caspian, and slider turtles are
      freshwater species.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      A high-domed shell, columnar unwebbed legs, and often a spur on each
      thigh. It walks slowly on land and does not bask on pond banks the way
      water turtles do.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      In eastern Georgia carapace length is often about 15–25 cm; females are
      larger on average. Muskhelishvili 1970 cites a maximum of 26 cm.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It has no venom. It is harmless to people if left alone. It may bite if
      handled. Do not take it home.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly grasses and herbaceous plants. Diet has not been measured
      separately for Georgia on this page.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      Georgia’s 2014 Red List lists it as Vulnerable (VU). It is on CITES
      Appendix II. Collecting wild animals is prohibited or tightly restricted.
