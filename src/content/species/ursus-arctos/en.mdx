commonName: Brown bear
location: Year-round in Georgia
description: >-
  A large omnivore with a shoulder hump, rounded ears, and long front claws. It
  is a native resident in Georgia and is on the national Red List.
overview: >-
  The brown bear (Ursus arctos) is a mammal in the bear family (Ursidae) and a
  native resident in Georgia. It appears on national mammal checklists. At
  species level, head–body length is about 1–2.8 m, the tail is short, and mass
  ranges from tens of kilograms to several hundred; southern Eurasian mountain
  bears are usually lighter than coastal Alaskan bears. A measured Georgian
  series is not published on this page. It has no venom. The human risk is an
  encounter, a bite, and rarely serious injury — not poison. IUCN lists the
  species globally as Least Concern. Georgia’s 2014 Red List treats it as
  Endangered (EN).
habitat: >-
  In Georgia it lives mainly in mountain forest where people have limited
  access. Lortkipanidze (2010) writes that it has been lost from lowland forest
  and many accessible areas, and that three areas remain: the Greater Caucasus,
  the Lesser Caucasus, and the far east of the country — Vashlovani National
  Park and its surroundings, in semi-arid country, not only high mountain
  forest. This page does not map administrative regions. This page includes a
  photograph from 20 January 2023 from Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park (ზაური
  ხაჩიძე), a photograph from 4 October 2023 from Tusheti Protected Areas (მირზა
  ხინკილაძე), a photograph from 24 May 2025 (თომა შენგელიძე), and a photograph
  from 30 July 2020 (ნიკა პაპოშვილი); the last two photos have no locality.
  Named protected areas
  with genetic or field monitoring are Borjomi-Kharagauli (2004, 2014, 2022),
  forest around Bakuriani (2004–2006), Algeti National Park, Mtirala,
  Kintrishi, Machakhela, and Vashlovani (2022 samples; 2023–2024 DNA analysis).
  The 2006 Trialeti report found bears extremely rare or locally extinct in the
  Tetritskaro sub-area. Do not assume even density across the whole country,
  including treeless steppe and settled lowlands. A Georgia-specific elevation
  belt is not published on this page.
diet: >-
  It is an omnivore. There is no separate national diet study on this page. The
  2006 Trialeti and Borjomi-Kharagauli report links autumn concentrations to
  fruiting and mast trees, including chestnut. At species level it eats grasses,
  roots, berries, nuts and beech or chestnut mast, insects, carrion, and, when
  it can, ungulates. In conflict areas it takes beehives, crops, and livestock —
  that is local damage, not a complete menu.
behavior: >-
  It often moves at dusk and at night, especially near people; in remote forest
  it also appears by day. In winter, when food is scarce, it dens — a deep
  sleep, not true hibernation as in some rodents; it can wake. The exact
  calendar has not been measured the same way in every Georgian population; do
  not assume the same denning length in milder Vashlovani winters. At species
  level pregnancy involves delayed implantation; cubs are born in the den,
  usually in winter, often one to several; they stay with the female for about
  two years. Those figures come from the wider range, not a Georgian den
  sample. Tracks are plantigrade: five toes, long claws on the front foot; the
  hind print is more like a human foot than a wolf print. Vocalisations are
  uncommon near people and no Georgian recording is on this page; the clip is
  xeno-canto XC1168186 (Stanislas Wroza, Kuusamo–Rinne, Finland, 24 May 2026),
  a male grunting and sniffing a female.
conservation: >-
  McLellan and co-authors assess Ursus arctos as Least Concern on the IUCN Red
  List (2017); the global trend is stable, but southern fragmented populations
  can be threatened separately. The 2017 IUCN assessment treats the Lesser
  Caucasus population (south and eastern Armenia into north-western Iran) as
  Endangered; that is a regional assessment, not Georgia’s Red List entry. In
  Georgia, Government Resolution No. 190 of 2014 lists the species as EN,
  criterion C2(aI), for a small, fragmented population. Hunting is not allowed.
  The Georgian population is on CITES Appendix II (Appendix I applies to other
  countries and to U. a. isabellinus). Lortkipanidze (2010) estimates a minimum
  of about 450 animals on about 34,000 km² of habitat, at about 13 / 1,000 km²
  in central Georgia; that is a minimum, not a full census. In 2014
  Borjomi-Kharagauli held 38–47 individuals (95% CI). In 2022–2024 sampling,
  228 unique genotypes were identified from Adjara parks, Borjomi-Kharagauli,
  Algeti, and Vashlovani — a sample from selected protected areas, not a
  national total; much of the Greater Caucasus is outside that series. Named
  pressures are illegal hunting, habitat fragmentation, and conflict with
  beekeepers and livestock keepers.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and is not treated as food. It is a large animal: it can bite
  or strike if you meet it suddenly, especially a female with cubs or an animal
  at a carcass. Do not approach, feed, or let a dog chase it. Make noise on
  trails so you do not surprise it. Store camp food securely. Damage to hives
  and livestock is real in some villages, especially around Borjomi-Kharagauli —
  that is conflict, not evidence that the bear hunts people. Any wild mammal can
  theoretically carry rabies. Do not handle wild animals.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large, stocky mammal with a shoulder hump, rounded ears, a short tail,
    and long front claws. It is the only bear in Georgia.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level head–body is about 1–2.8 m, the tail about 6–21 cm,
      shoulder height often about 90–150 cm, and mass spans a wide range —
      southern Eurasian bears are usually much lighter than coastal Alaskan
      males. Males are larger than females. There is no separate measured
      Georgian series on this page. The profile is often slightly dished, the
      ears small and rounded, and the tail almost lost in the fur.
    - >-
      Fur ranges from cream-sandy to dark brown or almost black; some
      individuals have a paler patch on the throat or chest. The Georgian name
      refers to the brown colour, not a separate species. The shoulder hump is
      muscle used for digging and running and separates it from a wolf in the
      field. Front claws are longer than the hind claws and do not retract like
      a cat’s.
    - >-
      No other bear species occurs in Georgia. Polar bear and American black
      bear are absent. A wolf is slimmer, with longer legs and a bushy tail,
      and no shoulder hump. This atlas has a wolf page. Wild boar tracks are
      cloven, not five-toed; this atlas has a wild boar page. A cub can look
      like a dog at a distance — the female is nearby.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. Do not approach. In forest, a large five-toed print
      with long claws, droppings with berries or plant remains, or dug ground
      is more likely this species than a wolf.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758. The accepted name is Ursus arctos. Kingdom
    Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Carnivora, suborder
    Caniformia, family Ursidae, genus Ursus.
  - >-
    Several subspecies names have been used in the Caucasus, including
    U. a. syriacus, caucasicus, dinniki, and lasistanicus. Murtskhvaladze and
    co-authors (2010), using mitochondrial and microsatellite data, conclude
    that Caucasian bears belong to the nominal species Ursus arctos. Maternal
    lineages differ between the Greater and Lesser Caucasus; male movement,
    likely via the Likhi Ridge, maintains gene flow. Field identification is to
    species.
  - >-
    In Georgian the name is მურა დათვი; often simply დათვი. In English, brown
    bear; inland North American populations are often called grizzly — the same
    species, a different geographic name.
  - >-
    It is in Bukhnikashvili and Kandaurov’s 2002 annotated mammal list. Eliava
    et al. 2007 include it in national biodiversity data. Lortkipanidze 2010
    confirms the modern range in the South Caucasus. That is country-level
    confirmation plus the named points above, not a complete regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the brown bear found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is a native resident. It is on national mammal checklists and on
      the 2014 Red List.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In mountain forest with few people, and in the semi-arid Vashlovani area.
      Named protected areas include Borjomi-Kharagauli, Mtirala, Kintrishi,
      Machakhela, Algeti, and Vashlovani. This page includes photographs from
      20 January 2023 from Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park and from 4 October
      2023 from Tusheti Protected Areas — observations, not guaranteed
      sightings. The Greater Caucasus is also in the range, but this
      page does not map every municipality. Do not treat open lowland as
      typical habitat.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A large body with a shoulder hump, rounded ears, a short tail, and long
      front claws. There is no other bear in Georgia. A wolf is slimmer and
      dog-like.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      At species level, head–body is about 1–2.8 m. Southern Eurasian mountain
      bears are usually lighter than coastal Alaskan bears. A measured Georgian
      series is not on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It is an omnivore: plants, berries, chestnut and other mast, insects,
      carrion, and sometimes ungulates. In some villages — hives and livestock.
      The full Georgian menu has not been measured here.
  - question: When is it active?
    answer: >-
      It is resident and does not migrate like many birds. It dens in winter;
      exact months are not published the same way for every Georgian
      population. Signs are more often found in summer and autumn.
  - question: Can you hear it?
    answer: >-
      Rarely near people. The clip on this page is from Finland (xeno-canto
      XC1168186); there is no Georgian recording here.
  - question: Is it dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It is not venomous, but it is large and strong. Do not approach,
      especially a female with cubs. Do not feed it.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia it is EN on the Red List; hunting is not allowed. Globally
      IUCN lists it as Least Concern.
