commonName: Dahl's whip snake
location: Eastern and southern Georgia; local western records
description: >-
  Non-venomous Dahl’s whip snake in Georgia — extremely slender; not a viper.
overview: |-
  Dahl's whip snake (Platyceps najadum) is a non-venomous, very slender snake. It belongs to the Colubridae family of snakes. This snake is linked to dry climates. In Georgia, it is found mostly in dry, rocky places in the east and south; in eastern Georgia, it is mainly known from rocky and grassy places.
  An adult snake is usually about 70–100 cm long and rarely exceeds 1 meter. According to 2026 data, its presence in Georgia is confirmed; a modern specimen is from Tmogvi in Aspindza Municipality.
  It is not considered to be at high risk of threat.
habitat: |-
  In Georgia, Dahl’s whip snake may be found on dry, rocky slopes, near cliffs, in scrub, at forest edges, on dry meadows, and near land used for farming. It shelters in cracks between stones and in hollows in rock. In winter, it has also been seen in structures built by people.

  This species is linked to drier places, especially rocky and grassy areas in eastern Georgia. One modern record gives Tmogvi as the location. Older museum records also mention Tbilisi and Borjomi.

  Its [distribution](/snakes/gavrtseleba) is confirmed in Kakheti, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Adjara, Tbilisi, Shida Kartli, and Imereti. In Racha-Lechkhumi and Kvemo Svaneti, as well as in Abkhazia, isolated records are known, but its distribution in these areas has not yet been confirmed. There are also modern records from Imereti. Old museum records do not show today’s picture precisely.

  Across the wider area where this species lives, it has been recorded at elevations of up to about 2,000 meters.
diet: >-
  In other places, the reddish-brown racer mainly hunts lizards, but it also catches small mammals, birds, and other snakes. It does not use venom when hunting and relies on speed.
behavior: |-
  In other parts of its range, the Caspian whipsnake is active during the day. It moves quickly and often warms itself in the sun.
  When threatened, it usually tries to escape. If it has no way out, it may raise the front part of its body and open its mouth.
  It lays eggs. This species is often described as laying 3–16 eggs.
  In 2026, one Caspian whipsnake in Georgia was found in a shared winter shelter together with 133 [red-bellied racers](dolichophis-schmidti) and one [dice snake](natrix-tessellata). The shelter was in a reinforced-concrete septic tank. This was only one case and does not mean that this species usually spends the winter with other snakes.
conservation: |-
  According to the available assessment, the Caspian whipsnake is in a low-risk group for extinction. It is not included in Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
  In Europe, this species is protected under Appendix II of the Bern Convention and Annex IV of the EU Habitats Directive. However, these EU rules do not automatically apply in Georgia.
  In the 2026 Georgian list, the species’ presence is confirmed, while the accuracy of data about its distribution is rated as moderate.
interaction: |-
  Dahl’s whip snake is not venomous and is not dangerous to people. Do not pick it up — a large snake may [bite you](/snakes/gvelis-nakbeni) in self-defense, and the bite may be painful.
  If the black part of a snake’s eye is vertically elongated, it is not Dahl’s whip snake. Watch an unfamiliar snake from a safe distance, leave it a way to move away, and take photos from afar.

  traits:
    - >-
      The body is very slender and looks like a whip. The head is narrow, the eyes are relatively large, and the pupils are round. The scales on the back are smooth, and the tail is long. Around the middle of the body, the scales are usually arranged in 19 rows, rarely in 17. Do not pick up a snake to count its scales.
    -  |-
      The body is usually olive-colored, gray, or rusty brown. On the neck and the front part of the body, it has dark spots with light edges. Toward the back of the body, the spots become smaller and often disappear completely, so the rear part of the back may be almost uniform in color.
      In young individuals, the spots on the neck are often more clearly visible. Sometimes a light-colored stripe can be seen in front of and behind the eye, running from top to bottom. The belly is light-colored.

      The [red-bellied racer](dolichophis-schmidti) (Dolichophis schmidti) is larger-bodied and, as an adult, is often almost entirely reddish.     - >-

If a snake's pupils are stretched from top to bottom, it is not Dahl’s whip snake, but that does not mean it must be a viper or a gyurza. In Georgia, the cat-eyed snake also has pupils like this. Do not approach an unknown snake.

facts:
  - >-
    Described in 1831 (Eichwald), originally as Tyria najadum; the type
    locality is Baku, not Georgia. Older literature often used Coluber najadum
    or Zamenis dahlii. The modern genus Platyceps became standard after Schätti
    & Monsch 2004. Caucasian animals belong to the nominotypical subspecies
    (P. n. najadum).
  - >-
    The 2026 checklist specimen and COI barcode are from Tmogvi (Aspindza;
    CaBOL-ID 1027410). The same paper notes about 6.65% COI difference between
    Georgian and Armenian sequences — a reason to revisit taxonomy, not a
    completed species split.
  - >-
    The Georgian name reflects the olive–buckthorn dorsal tone. The English
    name whip snake refers to the extremely slender, whip-like body. Light-edged
    dark neck spots are the main field mark.
faq:
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous to humans?
    answer: |-
      No. The reddish whip snake has no venom and is not dangerous to human health. When it feels threatened, it usually tries to escape, but if you pick it up, it may bite you.
      If the snake’s pupil is vertical, meaning it looks like a thin line, it is not a reddish whip snake. In that case, do not go near it and leave the snake a way to move away.
  - question: Is Dahl’s whip snake found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. In Georgia’s 2026 list, the dice snake is included as a confirmed species. A modern record says it was seen around Tmogvi. It is not found equally everywhere in Georgia.
  - question: Where might I encounter it in Georgia?
    answer: |-
      Dahl’s whip snake is found mainly in dry, rocky or grassy places in eastern and southern Georgia. One modern record gives the location as the area around Tmogvi in Samtskhe-Javakheti.
      Its distribution has been confirmed in Kakheti, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Adjara, Tbilisi, Shida Kartli and Imereti. In Racha-Lechkhumi and Kvemo Svaneti, as well as in Abkhazia, there are only isolated records, so its distribution in these regions cannot yet be considered confirmed.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: |-
      A very thin, whip-like body, round eye pupil, smooth scales, and dark spots with pale edges can help with identification. These spots are on the neck and the front part of the body, while the rear part is often almost plain.
      In the variegated racer, the spots run along a much larger part of the back. If a snake has a vertical pupil, it is not the tawny racer. Still, when identifying an unfamiliar snake, do not rely on just one feature.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: |-
      An adult Dahl’s whip snake is usually 70–100 cm long and rarely exceeds 1 meter. Across the wider area where this snake occurs, larger snakes are also known, about 1.35–1.40 m long.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      In other places where this snake lives, it mainly catches lizards, but it also hunts small mammals, birds, and other snakes. It has no venom and catches prey using speed.
  - question: When is it active, and does it lay eggs?
    answer: |-
      In other places where this snake is found, it is active during the day.
      This snake lays eggs. Snakes of this species often lay 3–16 eggs. In Georgia, one such snake was seen in winter sheltering in a human-built structure together with other non-venomous snakes.
  - question: What should I do if I encounter one?
    answer: |-
      Watch the snake from a safe distance, take a photo, and let it move away. Do not pick it up.
      If the snake’s pupil is vertical, it is not a dice snake. Venomous vipers and other snakes can also have this kind of pupil. So you cannot tell which snake it is by pupil shape alone.
