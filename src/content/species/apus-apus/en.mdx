commonName: Common swift
location: Breeding migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A dark aerial bird with long scythe-shaped wings and a short forked tail.
  Confirmed in Georgia as a breeding migrant on the 2024 national bird
  checklist; it does not stay the winter.
overview: >-
  The common swift (Apus apus) is a medium-small aerial insectivore (family
  Apodidae) confirmed in Georgia as a breeding migrant. The 2024 annotated
  checklist of the birds of Georgia codes it BB; M: a breeder that is absent
  in the non-breeding season, and also a bird of passage. Seasonality is
  classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence quality is high (H), with
  photographs. SABUKO lists the Georgian name ნამგალა. Adults are about
  16–17 cm long at species level, sooty brown-black, with long crescent wings
  and a short forked tail. It is not a threat to people. IUCN lists the
  species as Least Concern globally. The 2024 checklist assigns the same
  category nationally. The 2021 European Red List of Birds assesses it as Near
  Threatened in Europe.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia look for it in the breeding season and on passage, not in winter
  on the 2024 checklist. This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality
  map. The checklist confirms country-level breeding-migrant status but does
  not publish a regional gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly across
  Georgia, including treeless high plateaus far from nest sites. NPLG states
  that it breeds throughout Georgia; that is a general secondary claim, not a
  municipality table, and is not used here to colour regions. Species-level
  habitat is open air over towns, villages, cliffs, and open country where
  flying insects are available. Nesting is typically in cavities: under roof
  tiles, in wall cracks, on buildings, and in rock crevices; natural tree holes
  are used less often today. Foraging may be far from the nest when weather or
  insect density requires it. A separate national elevation band for Georgia is
  not published here. The atlas map is empty until named Georgian localities
  are compiled from sources that actually list them.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. Across the range it
  feeds almost exclusively on flying insects and other aerial arthropods caught
  in flight — often called “aerial plankton”. Prey groups in species-level
  literature commonly include flies, beetles, bugs, and flying ants. It drinks
  and gathers nest material on the wing. That is the general trophic niche, not
  a measured Georgian menu.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day and spends almost its entire life in the air outside the
  nest. Flight is fast and stiff-winged, with deep beats and long glides; the
  silhouette looks like an anchor or crescent. The usual call is a loud, shrill
  scream, often from screaming parties circling near nesting buildings in
  summer evenings. No Georgian recording is on this page. Nesting is colonial
  when sites allow. Species-level clutches are often two or three white eggs;
  incubation and nestling periods are commonly given around two and a half to
  about six weeks depending on weather and food; chicks can slow growth when
  insects are scarce. Those figures are from the wider range, not a Georgian
  nest sample on this page. Adults usually leave breeding areas earlier than
  the young. The 2024 checklist treats the species as a breeder and migrant in
  Georgia, not as a winter visitor. At species level, western Palearctic birds
  winter mainly in Africa south of the Sahara; that is not a satellite-tracked
  Georgian route on this page. Exact arrival and departure dates for Georgia
  are not tabulated here. Grounded birds struggle to take off from flat ground
  because the legs are very short; do not leave a grounded swift on open
  pavement if it appears uninjured — contact a wildlife rehabilitator or lift
  it carefully onto a high open perch so it can launch, without forcing
  handling.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Apus apus as Least Concern on the IUCN Red
  List. The global population is large. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist
  assigns global and national Red List category LC. It is not on Georgia’s
  2014 Red List (Government Resolution No. 190). Independently, the 2021
  European Red List of Birds assesses the species as Near Threatened in Europe
  (criteria A2bc+3bc+4bc), with a decreasing European trend; that regional
  assessment is not the same as Georgia’s national LC code. A Georgia-specific
  population trend is not quantified on this page. Documented pressures at
  species level include loss of nest cavities during building renovation,
  fewer flying insects, and collision or trapping at nest sites; that is not a
  ranked national threat list for Georgia. The EU Birds Directive does not
  apply in Georgia. Do not block nest holes in the breeding season without
  providing alternatives. Do not catch wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not venomous and is harmless. Wild birds must not be kept. A grounded
  swift is usually unable to take off from flat ground; seek local wildlife
  advice rather than keeping it as a pet.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A sooty dark bird with long scythe-shaped wings and a short forked tail,
    almost always seen in flight. In Georgia it is a breeding migrant on the
    2024 checklist, not a winter bird. Usual confusion is swallows/martins and
    other swifts.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length about 16–17 cm at species level; wingspan is often given around
      38–48 cm depending on source; mass is often about 35–50 g. A separate
      measured series from Georgia is not given on this page. The body is
      cigar-shaped; the wings are long, narrow, and crescent-shaped; the tail
      is short and forked. Plumage is sooty brown to blackish; a small pale
      throat patch is present but often hard to see against the sky. Sexes look
      alike in the field. Juveniles are similar, sometimes with slightly paler
      scaling.
    - >-
      Swallows and martins (Hirundinidae) also catch insects in the air but
      have different silhouettes: usually pale underparts, shorter broader
      wings relative to body, and often a more fluttering flight. Barn swallow
      has a long deeply forked tail with streamers; house martin has a white
      rump. Swifts have uniformly dark underparts apart from the small throat
      patch, stiffer wingbeats, and an anchor-like outline. The screaming call
      is unlike swallow twittering.
    - >-
      Alpine swift (Tachymarptis melba), Georgian მეკირია / თეთრმუცელა ნამგალა,
      is also a breeding migrant on the 2024 checklist (BB; M). It is clearly
      larger, with a white belly and throat contrasting with a dark breast
      band. Pallid swift (Apus pallidus) is listed as casual (Cas) with
      photographs; it is paler sandy-brown with a larger white throat and is
      hard to separate without good light — treat as rare in Georgia. Little
      swift (Apus affinis) is listed with undetermined status (ND) on that
      checklist. This atlas does not yet have separate pages for those
      species.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. A dark crescent-winged bird screaming over
      Georgian towns in summer is this species far more often than pallid
      swift. Leave nests and fledglings alone unless a bird is grounded and
      needs help to launch.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Hirundo apus. The 2024 Georgian checklist
    follows the IOC World Bird List and the OSME Region List for names. IOC and
    GBIF place swifts in order Apodiformes, family Apodidae, genus Apus; SABUKO’s
    table lists Caprimulgiformes for the same family. English name on the
    checklist is Common Swift. Older synonyms include Cypselus apus.
  - >-
    Two subspecies are widely recognised: nominate A. a. apus (Europe and much
    of western Asia) and A. a. pekinensis (more easterly Asia). NPLG assigns
    the nominate form to Georgia. Field identification is to species. A
    measured Georgian specimen series assigned to subspecies is not given on
    this page.
  - >-
    Georgian is ნამგალა (SABUKO); the name refers to the scythe-like wing.
    Alpine swift is მეკირია (or თეთრმუცელა ნამგალა), not the same bird. English
    names in use are common swift and Eurasian swift.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: BB; M, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH (photograph). That is country-level
    confirmation, not a regional map. Alpine swift is listed separately as
    BB; M; pallid swift as casual.
faq:
  - question: Is the common swift found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed breeding bird and migrant (BB; M). It is absent in the
      non-breeding season.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In the warmer months, often over towns and villages with cavity nest
      sites, and in open air where insects are flying. This page has no named
      municipality list and does not colour the regional map. Presence is
      confirmed at country level, not mapped region by region here.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Long scythe-shaped wings, short forked tail, sooty dark plumage, almost
      always in flight. Look for the scream near nesting buildings. Swallows
      usually show pale underparts; alpine swift is larger with a white belly.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 16–17 cm long at species level, with a wingspan often given around
      38–48 cm. A separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this
      page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Flying insects and other aerial arthropods caught in flight. Diet has not
      been studied separately in Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      As a breeding migrant: present in the breeding season and on passage,
      absent in winter, according to the 2024 checklist. Exact arrival and
      departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated on this page.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. BB on the 2024 checklist means it is a confirmed breeder. Nests are
      usually in building cavities or rock crevices. A settlement-by-settlement
      nest census is not published on this page.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. It is globally Least Concern and coded LC nationally on
      the 2024 checklist. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List. Europe-wide it
      is Near Threatened on the 2021 European Red List of Birds. Do not catch
      wild birds or seal nest holes in the breeding season without alternatives.
