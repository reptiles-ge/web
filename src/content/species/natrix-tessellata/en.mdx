overview: >-
  The dice snake (Natrix tessellata) is a nonvenomous snake that lives both in water and on land. It is especially common near rivers, lakes, reservoirs and other bodies of water. It has dark, square spots on its back but lacks the yellow “collar” typical of the grass snake. Its presence is confirmed in the 2026 Georgian species list. On this page, only regions with confirmed records are listed as places where it occurs: Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti and Tbilisi. There are also records from Shida Kartli and Racha — Lechkhumi — Kvemo Svaneti, but these regions are not currently counted as places where the species occurs. In the semi-dry eastern areas, the dice snake is often easier to spot than the grass snake (Natrix natrix). In some wetter western areas, the reverse is true. Adults are usually about 70–110 cm long, though a large female may exceed 1.2 metres. The species is assessed as Least Concern and is not on Georgia’s Red List.
habitat: >-
  This page includes only regions where records confirm the water snake’s presence as part of its range: Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti, Mtskheta — Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti and Tbilisi. There are records of the snake in Shida Kartli and Racha — Lechkhumi — Kvemo Svaneti, but this page does not include those regions in its range. In Georgia, the water snake is usually found near water — rivers, streams, lakes, reservoirs, canals and ponds. It is often seen on rocks, muddy banks or branches hanging over the water. It may also be found on farmland or in other places changed by people if there is enough water and food nearby. In western Georgia, it is also found in the Colchian marshes and near bodies of water along the Black Sea coast. The material used for this page mentions Kolkheti National Park and Charnali Gorge in Adjara; there are also records from Lisi Lake in Tbilisi. Compared with the common grass snake, the water snake more often chooses open, rocky and partly dry banks. According to the Georgian data used for this page, it is usually found at elevations up to about 600 metres in western Georgia and 900 metres in eastern Georgia. Rare records reach about 1,100 metres.
diet: >-
  It feeds mainly on fish but also catches amphibians, including frogs and toads. It may eat young fish too. Along the Caucasus coast, gobies have been described as important prey. It swallows its prey alive and hunts both in the water and near the shore. The sources used for this page do not include a separate study with figures on this species’ diet in Georgia.
behavior: >-
  It is active in the warm season and moves mostly by day. It often swims or
  basks on banks, stones, and branches over the water. In the cold period it
  overwinters near water in dry burrows and crevices. The exact timing of
  overwintering and activity depends on local climate and elevation. It lays
  eggs. Across the species' range, mating usually occurs in spring, and eggs are
  laid in summer in warm, moist places such as soil, rotting vegetation, or gaps
  in stone walls. Young hatch in late summer or early autumn. When threatened it
  may release a foul cloacal secretion or feign death. It has no venom. If you
  encounter one, observe from a distance and leave it in place.
conservation: >-
  The IUCN Red List assesses the dice snake as Least Concern. It is not included
  in Georgia's 2014 Red List. The species is listed in Appendix II of the Bern
  Convention. In regions confirmed by the records table it is locally common in
  suitable aquatic habitats. Possible pressures include water pollution, bank
  alteration, and killing by people because it is often mistaken for a venomous
  snake. The sources used on this page do not separately assess an overall
  quantitative population trend for Georgia.

identification:
  summary: >-
    The dice snake is a nonvenomous snake that lives both in water and on land. Its dark, square spots on the back, lack of a yellow “collar,” and close connection to water are especially helpful for identifying it.
  traits:
    - >-
      Its back is olive, gray or brownish, with dark markings that are often square or arranged like a checkerboard. It usually lacks the yellow “collar” behind the head that is characteristic of the common grass snake.
    - >-
      Its head is relatively narrow and elongated, and its pupils are round. The scales on its back have a small raised ridge down the middle. Its belly is often yellow or orange, with black spots or dots.
    - >-
      Adult water snakes are usually about 70–110 cm long. A large female can be over 1.2 metres long. Females are often larger than males, and the markings on young snakes may stand out more.
    - >-
      It often swims or basks in the sun on the shore, on rocks, or on branches hanging over the water. Water snakes are often found near water, but that alone is not enough to identify one with certainty. Also look for a checkerboard-like pattern on its back and the absence of a yellow “collar.”
    - >-
      The common grass snake (Natrix natrix) often has a yellow or pale “collar” behind its head. The water snake usually has no such collar. The Caucasian viper (Vipera kaznakovi) is venomous, has a stockier body and has vertically elongated pupils. Pseudopus apodus is not a snake — it has movable eyelids and ear openings. Do not pick up an unfamiliar snake.

faq:
  - question: Is the dice snake venomous?
    answer: >-
      No. The dice snake has no venom and is not dangerous to people. If handled,
      it may release a foul-smelling secretion or feign death. Do not pick it up
      — it is better to observe from a distance and leave it in place.
  - question: How does it differ from the grass snake?
    answer: >-
      The dice snake often has dark checkered blotches on the back and is more
      closely tied to water. The grass snake often has a yellow or pale collar
      behind the head and is especially common in the humid west. Diet also
      differs: the dice snake feeds mainly on fish, while the grass snake relies
      more on amphibians.
  - question: Does the dice snake occur in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The dice snake is a confirmed species in Georgia. The 2026 Georgian
      checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. On this page, only regions
      whose status is confirmed in the records-by-region table count as
      distribution.
  - question: Where can I find it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The records table confirms distribution in Abkhazia, Samegrelo — Zemo
      Svaneti, Guria, Adjara, Imereti, Samtskhe — Javakheti, Mtskheta —
      Mtianeti, Kvemo Kartli, Kakheti, and Tbilisi. Shida Kartli and Racha —
      Lechkhumi — Kvemo Svaneti are recorded-only. By habitat it occurs near
      rivers, lakes, streams, canals, reservoirs, and other water bodies.
  - question: How can I tell it from a viper?
    answer: >-
      Look for dark checkered blotches on the back, no yellow collar, a
      relatively narrow head, and a round pupil. It is often in or near water.
      Vipers have a heavier build and vertical pupil, but do not rely on a single
      trait to identify an unknown snake.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      Adult water snakes are usually about 70–110 cm long. A large female can be more than 1.2 metres long. The sources used for this page do not separately provide measurements from a large number of water snakes living in Georgia.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It feeds mainly on fish. It also catches frogs and toads, and may eat young fish. It swallows its prey alive. The sources used for this page do not include a separate study showing how much of each type of food it eats in Georgia.
  - question: What should I do if I find one?
    answer: >-
      Watch it from a distance and leave it where it is. The water snake is not venomous, so there is no need to catch or kill it. If you are not sure what kind of snake it is, do not approach it, even to check the shape of its pupils or head.
