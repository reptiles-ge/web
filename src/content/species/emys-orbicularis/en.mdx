commonName: "European Pond Turtle"
location: "Western and eastern Georgia"
description: >-
  A native freshwater turtle with a low shell. Not a land tortoise, and it lacks
  a red ear stripe.
overview: >-
  The European pond turtle (Emys orbicularis) is Georgia’s native freshwater
  turtle — not a land tortoise and not the introduced slider. The 2026 Georgian
  checklist treats it as biologically confirmed. In western Georgia it lists
  E. o. orbicularis; in the east, E. o. persica. It belongs to Emydidae. It has
  no venom. In Caucasian forms the shell is often under 16 cm; adults are
  medium-sized at species level. IUCN lists it as Near Threatened. It is not on
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
habitat: >-
  It lives in still or slow fresh water — ponds, lakes, marshes, oxbows, and
  slow rivers, often with vegetated banks. Females nest on land, usually on
  sandy, sunny slopes away from the water. In western Georgia a named point is
  Poti: that is the 2026 checklist figure-specimen locality, not a map of all
  Samegrelo. In the same Colchic coastal complex, Georgia’s Black Sea wetlands
  review names the beach and dunes by Churia Fen as a nesting area; the
  Paliastomi basin sits in the same wetland belt. For eastern Georgia the
  checklist assigns a subspecies but this page has no municipality list. The
  range map shades only Samegrelo — Zemo Svaneti, following the specimen
  region; the east is unshaded because no named eastern locality is given here.
  An elevation range in metres for Georgia is not published on this page. Across
  Europe the species is mainly a lowland animal and rarely reaches high
  mountains.
diet: >-
  Omnivorous. It usually feeds in water: invertebrates, small fish, amphibians,
  and plant material. At species level, juveniles eat more animal prey; adults
  take more plants. A Georgian stomach or faeces study is not cited here.
behavior: >-
  By day it often basks on banks, logs, or aquatic plants; it dives when
  disturbed. Webbed feet make it a swimmer, not a land tortoise, though females
  come ashore to nest and animals sometimes walk between waterbodies. At species
  level it often overwinters in mud or on the bottom; months are not measured
  for Georgia on this page. Females lay eggs in a terrestrial nest. The European
  IUCN account gives about one to three clutches a year, typically about six to
  nine eggs; those figures are range-wide. Sex can depend on incubation
  temperature. Do not take wild turtles.
conservation: >-
  Native and biologically confirmed. The global IUCN assessment is from 1996
  (Near Threatened) and is annotated as needing an update. The 2024 European
  regional assessment is also NT, citing habitat loss and other pressures. The
  1996 global account lists Georgia as a native country. The species is not on
  Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Decree no. 190); Testudo graeca is. CITES does not
  list the Georgian population; Appendix III covers only Ukraine. The EU
  Habitats Directive does not apply in Georgia. Realistic western-coast
  pressures include wetland drainage, shoreline change, and road kill during
  nesting movements; a national trend is not measured here. Subspecies: the
  checklist uses E. o. orbicularis in the west and E. o. persica in the east.
  E. o. colchica and E. o. iberica, described for the southern Caucasus, are
  treated there as synonyms of orbicularis and persica. TTWG 2021 and ITIS treat
  colchica as a junior synonym of the nominate subspecies; persica is accepted.
  Tuniyev’s Caucasian list still treats colchica as a Colchis endemic
  subspecies — that disagreement stands, and field photos should not be used to
  assign subspecies.
interaction: >-
  It has no venom and does not hunt people. It may bite or scratch if picked up.
  The shell and skin can carry bacteria; wash your hands. Do not take wild
  turtles home.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A native water turtle with a low, often dark shell and yellow speckling. A
    high dome means tortoise; a red stripe behind the eye means the introduced
    slider.
  traits:
    - >-
      The upper shell (carapace) is low and oval, not a high dome. Colour is
      usually dark olive, brown, or black, often with yellow dots or streaks.
      The lower shell (plastron) is paler. Emys has a movable hinge on the
      plastron; the Caspian turtle (Mauremys) has a practically rigid plastron.
    - >-
      Head, neck, and legs are dark with yellow dots or short streaks. There is
      no broad red stripe behind the eye. The feet are webbed. For Kura-basin
      Caucasian forms Fritz (1998) gives a maximum shell length of about 18 cm,
      usually under 16 cm; a separate measured series for western Georgian
      nominate animals is not on this page. Males are often smaller than
      females, with a longer tail and a more concave plastron.
    - >-
      The Mediterranean tortoise (Testudo graeca) is terrestrial: high-domed
      shell and columnar legs without webbing. The pond slider (Trachemys
      scripta, usually T. s. elegans) is introduced; it has a broad red stripe
      behind the eye and often bolder shell striping. The Caspian turtle
      (Mauremys caspica) is recorded from southeastern Georgia; it often has
      pale lengthwise neck stripes and a more striped than speckled shell.
    - >-
      It is not venomous. Watch and do not pick it up. In city ponds, rule out
      the slider first, especially near Tbilisi and Batumi.    - >-
      The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as a biologically confirmed native
      species — not a land tortoise and not the introduced slider. In western
      Georgia it lists E. o. orbicularis; in the east, E. o. persica. In the
      west, named points include Poti and Colchic coastal wetlands. In the
      east the checklist assigns a subspecies, but this page does not shade
      eastern regions on the map.

faq:
  - question: Is the European pond turtle found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2026 Georgian checklist treats it as a biologically confirmed
      native species.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In freshwater ponds, lakes, and marshes. In the west, named points include
      Poti and Colchic coastal wetlands, including shore associated with Churia
      Fen. In the east the checklist assigns a subspecies, but this page does
      not shade eastern regions on the map.
  - question: How can it be identified?
    answer: >-
      Low dark shell with yellow speckling, webbed feet, and no red ear stripe.
      A high dome is a tortoise; a red ear is a slider.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      In Caucasian Kura-basin forms the shell is often under 16 cm, maximum
      about 18 cm (Fritz 1998). A separate series for western Georgian
      populations is not given here.
  - question: How do I tell it from a red-eared slider?
    answer: >-
      The slider is introduced; T. s. elegans has a broad red stripe behind the
      eye. The pond turtle is native and lacks that stripe.
  - question: How do I tell it from a Caspian turtle?
    answer: >-
      The Caspian turtle is recorded from the southeast and often has pale
      lengthwise neck stripes. The pond turtle’s shell is usually darker with
      yellow speckling.
  - question: Is it venomous or dangerous?
    answer: >-
      It has no venom. It may bite if handled. Do not pick it up. Wash a bite
      and seek care if needed.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      IUCN lists it as Near Threatened. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List.
