overview: >-
  The Eurasian otter (Lutra lutra), also called the common otter, is a mammal
  of the weasel family (Mustelidae) that lives along rivers, lakes, reservoirs,
  and fish ponds. It is native to Georgia and lives here year-round. In
  the 2012–2013 national survey, otter signs were found at 245 of 631
  surveyed sites, most often at lakes and reservoirs. A study on the Alazani
  found that it feeds mainly on fish and amphibians.

  In the 2022 IUCN global assessment, the Eurasian otter is Near Threatened
  (NT). On Georgia’s 2014 Red List it has Vulnerable (VU) status, and hunting it
  is prohibited.

  Body length without the tail is about 59–90 cm, and the tail is about 35–47 cm
  long. These measurements apply to the species as a whole, not only to otters
  in Georgia.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia the otter lives along rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and fish ponds. It
  is found especially where there is enough food and places to hide.

  In the 2012–2013 national survey, otter signs were found at 245 of 631 surveyed
  sites, or about 39%. Most signs were found by fresh water. Lakes and
  reservoirs had the highest rate: signs were found at about 75% of those sites.
  On rivers and other freshwater sites the rate was 36%, and at 11 sea-coast
  sites it was about 18%. The higher the site, the less often otter signs were
  found.

  In Georgia the otter has been recorded up to about 2,800 m. Across the
  species’ whole range it occurs from sea level up to about 4,120 m.

  Examples of places in Georgia where the otter has been described include the
  Alazani basin, the Bakhvistskali valley, Borjomi Gorge, and the Kintrishi. On
  the Bakhvistskali in 2022, a hydropower environmental study recorded 55 otter
  activity points, mostly tracks. These figures come from an environmental
  study, not a peer-reviewed scientific paper.

  For Lagodekhi, the data do not fully agree. One baseline survey found no otter
  signs there, but a recent observation from Lagodekhi was added later. One
  suggested explanation for the missing signs is that trout were scarce in the
  Lagodekhi rivers.
diet: >-
  In 2006–2007, 791 otter spraints from a section of the Alazani were
  analysed. In the cold season, from November to March, fish were the most
  frequent food item at about 50%, while amphibians were 30%. In the warm
  season, from April to October, the pattern changed: amphibians made up about
  36% and fish 24%.

  Reptiles were recorded in about 12–13% in both seasons. They were only
  non-venomous water snakes: the [grass snake](natrix-natrix) (Natrix natrix)
  and the [dice snake](natrix-tessellata) (Natrix tessellata). The only
  amphibian found was the [marsh frog](pelophylax-ridibundus) (Pelophylax
  ridibundus).

  Among fish, crucian carp (Carassius carassius) and common carp were
  especially frequent. Most prey
  items were under 20 cm. Rodents, including mice and muskrat, were recorded in
  small numbers, as were birds, insects, molluscs, and crustaceans.
behavior: >-
  Along fresh water, the otter is often more active at dusk and at night. It can
  also appear by day, especially in quiet places where people disturb it less.
  It does not hibernate and stays active all year.

  Its den is often called a holt. The otter may make it among roots on the bank,
  in a rock crevice, in a natural hollow, or in a tunnel it digs itself.
  Sometimes the entrance is underwater.

  Otters often mark their territory with spraint, as their droppings are called.
  They leave it on rocks, logs, near bridges, and in other conspicuous places.
  Spraint often contains fish scales and bits of bone. Tracks show five toes,
  with webbing between them. In the 2022 Bakhvistskali study, otter presence was
  recorded mostly through tracks.

  Pregnancy lasts about 61–74 days. Litters are usually 2–3 young, although
  there may be anywhere from 1 to 5. The young are born blind and covered in
  short grey fur. The female raises them, and the male takes little part in
  their care. These figures apply to the species as a whole, not only to otters
  in Georgia.
conservation: >-
  In the 2022 IUCN global assessment, the Eurasian otter (Lutra lutra) is Near
  Threatened (NT), and its population is decreasing. The previous global
  assessment, in 2015, gave the same category.

  On Georgia’s 2014 Red List, the otter is listed as Vulnerable (VU). That
  assessment names the shrinking of its range as one of the main problems. The
  species is also on CITES Appendix I, and hunting it in Georgia is prohibited.

  Older Georgian assessments described the otter’s situation in more severe
  terms. Since later national surveys, those assessments are considered to rest
  on outdated data. One estimate puts Georgia’s population at more than 300
  animals, although this figure comes from a summary of other sources and is not
  based on an independent modern national count. Georgia’s population is
  described as small. A 2019 review of the otter’s world status
  listed Georgia among the countries where otter populations were still
  declining.

  The main threats to the otter are the loss of riverside forest and other bank
  habitats, water pollution, shrinking fish stocks, poaching, and conflict with
  fish farms. During the national otter survey, traps had been set near almost
  every fish pond. Hydropower construction and work on river beds may also
  damage its habitat.

  In 2019, the media reported that an otter had been killed in Tbilisi on the
  bank of the Mtkvari. This is a news report about a single case, not a field
  study of the population.

  Do not approach or pick up a wild otter.
interaction: >-
  The Eurasian otter is not venomous. If it is caught or cornered, it can bite,
  so do not feed it, touch it, or pick it up.

  Otters can really cause damage at fish ponds. Trout ponds are especially
  vulnerable: they hold fish at very high density, the water is clear, and there
  is no vegetation. An otter that reaches such a pond can cause a lot of damage.
  Even so, the otter is a protected species, and killing or trapping it is not
  allowed.

  Like any wild mammal, an otter could in theory carry rabies.

  summary: >-
    The Eurasian otter has a long, low body and short legs. Its fur is dark
    brown, its head broad and flat, and its feet webbed. The tail is thick at
    the base and gradually narrows toward the tip. When it swims, often only the
    head and part of the back show above the water.
  traits:
    - >-
      Males are usually larger than females. Male body length without the tail
      is about 60–90 cm, and female length is 59–70 cm. The tail reaches about
      35–47 cm. Males weigh about 5.5–11.4 kg, and females 3.4–7.6 kg. These
      measurements apply to the species as a whole.

      The head is broad and flat, the whiskers are long, and the ears are small
      and rounded. The legs are short and the feet are webbed. The tail is thick
      at the base and gradually narrows toward the tip.
    - >-
      The fur is very dense and protects it well from water. The back is dark
      brown and may look almost black when wet. The underside is paler, and the
      throat sometimes has a light patch.

      When swimming, often only the head and part of the back are visible.
    - >-
      No other otter species occurs in Georgia. Neogale vison (older name
      Mustela vison) is the American mink, not an otter. It appears in an older
      list of South Caucasian mustelids.

      The Eurasian otter is much larger than the American mink and has a thick
      tail that gradually narrows from the base toward the tip. The [least
      weasel](mustela-nivalis)
      is much smaller than the otter and lives on land; its body length without
      the tail is about 13–26 cm. The [Caucasian badger](meles-canescens) is
      heavier, has a
      black-and-white striped head, and is not adapted to life in water the way
      the otter is.

      From a distance, a swimming dog can be mistaken for an otter. The otter’s
      low silhouette in the water and its thick tail, broad at the base and
      narrowing toward the end, are especially useful clues.
    - >-
      Signs of an otter are often found before the animal itself is seen.
      Five-toed, webbed tracks may remain in bank mud. Spraint, sometimes with
      fish-scale remains, may be left on rocks. Slides may be visible on the
      bank.

      The otter is not venomous. Do not approach or pick up a wild otter.

facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758 as Mustela lutra. The accepted name is Lutra
    lutra. Order Carnivora, suborder Caniformia, family Mustelidae, subfamily
    Lutrinae, genus Lutra. Synonyms include Viverra lutra and Lutra vulgaris.
  - >-
    Caucasian literature often uses the subspecies L. l. meridionalis Ognev,
    1931. Its range is given as Georgia to the Persian Gulf. As of 2004,
    no otter DNA work had been done in the Caucasus. A 2024 study compared
    Caucasian and northern Eurasian samples from Russia and Armenia. Field identification
    is to species.
  - >-
    In Georgian the usual names are წავი and ჩვეულებრივი წავი. The Red List uses
    წავი. In English: Eurasian otter, also European otter and common otter.
  - >-
    The otter is native to Georgia. It is in the 2002 annotated list of the
    mammals of Georgia. The 2012–2013 national survey is a
    country-level picture together with the named localities, not a full
    regional map.

      intruder enters its territory, it may make a chittering sound. When it is
      cornered in a fight, it screams like a cat. You can listen to a recording
      of an otter on this page.
