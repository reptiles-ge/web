commonName: Common quail
location: Breeding migrant, passage migrant and winter visitor in Georgia
description: >-
  A small, cryptic grassland gamebird heard more often than seen. Confirmed in
  Georgia on the 2024 national bird checklist as a breeding bird, a passage
  migrant, and a winter visitor.
overview: >-
  The quail (Coturnix coturnix) is a small migratory, chicken-like bird in the pheasant family (Phasianidae). According to 2024 data, it breeds in Georgia and leaves its breeding areas after the breeding season. It is also found here during migration and in winter. Information about its seasonal presence is reliable. Its presence in Georgia is also well documented by a photograph, a sound recording and a bird obtained during a hunt. Adult quail are generally about 16–18 cm long. They pose no direct threat to people. Internationally, the species is assessed as having a low risk of extinction. Georgia’s 2024 assessment gives it the same status. The quail is a game bird in Georgia, and hunting it is regulated. It is not on the Red List.
habitat: >-
  Look for it in open country with cover: cereal fields, clover and other
  crops, meadows, and grassy steppe. It stays on the ground and is usually
  found by ear, not by eye. The 2024 checklist confirms country-level breeding,
  migrant, and winter-visitor status but does not publish a municipality map.
  Do not assume it occurs evenly across Georgia, including closed forest or
  high alpine rock. Named Georgian locality with published sampling: Lewis et
  al. (2013) report tens of thousands of common quail migrating through the
  Alazani River Valley sampling area in September, moving from Eurasian Steppe
  breeding areas toward wintering grounds in the Middle East and East Africa.
  That Alazani / Kakheti evidence is coloured on this page. A separate national
  elevation band for Georgia is not published here; older Georgian hunting-site
  accounts speak of birds on mountain slopes and alpine meadows in summer when
  lowland crops are cut — treat that as unverified for this atlas until a
  peer-reviewed Georgian elevation series is compiled.
diet: >-
  This page does not include a separate study from Georgia in which this bird’s food was examined by weighing it. In general, the species eats seeds, including leftover grain and weed seeds. It also eats beetles, bugs, ants, grasshoppers, crickets and related insects that live on the ground, as well as their larvae. The bird is traditionally hunted in Georgia. Hunting rules are separate from the Red List.
behavior: >-
  It is secretive by day, walking in cover rather than flying. When flushed it
  rises with a whirring burst, flies low and fast on relatively long wings for
  a gamebird, then drops back into cover. The male’s advertising song is a
  far-carrying three-note whistle often rendered “wet-my-lips”, given mainly at
  dawn, dusk, and sometimes at night. The clip on this page is xeno-canto
  XC476230 (Jens Loose, Dresden): a nocturnal flight call, not the advertising
  song, and not a Georgian recording. Nesting is a shallow scrape on the ground
  in vegetation; species-level clutches are often about 8–13 eggs with
  incubation of about 17–20 days — those figures are from the wider range, not
  a Georgian nest sample on this page. The 2024 checklist treats the species as
  a breeder, a migrant, and a winter visitor in Georgia. Exact arrival and
  departure dates for Georgia are not tabulated here.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Coturnix coturnix as Least Concern on the IUCN
  Red List, with a very large global range. The 2024 Georgian bird checklist
  assigns global and national category LC. Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government
  Resolution No. 190) does not list the common quail among the sources used
  here. Checklist evidence includes sound recording (SR) and hunter samples
  (CH), so regulated hunting is part of the documented Georgian picture. The
  Environmental Supervision Department’s published hunting seasons list Coturnix
  coturnix (მწყერი) from the fourth Saturday of August to 15 February, with a
  daily bag limit of 20 birds under the current posted rules — check the live
  DES notice for the year you hunt. Documented pressures at species level
  include intensive hunting on migration, agricultural change, and habitat
  loss; a measured Georgian population trend is not given on this page. Do not
  disturb nests or take birds outside legal seasons and permits.
interaction: >-
  It is not dangerous to people. Do not catch wild birds without a permit. Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica) and domestic crossbred quail are kept in captivity and may also be released. They look very much like wild quail. To tell them apart, pay attention to their calls and the circumstances in which you see the bird. Wild quail meat is traditionally eaten where hunting is legal. Follow the rules in force in Georgia.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small, mottled brown bird found in open grassy fields. You will often hear the male’s whistle before you see the bird. It has three notes and sounds like “wet-my-lips.” If you spot it, look for pale stripes on its back and a pale stripe above the eye. Males also have a dark mark on the throat. According to 2024 records, it breeds in Georgia and is also seen there during migration and in winter.
  traits:
    - >-
      In general, birds of this species are about 16–18 cm long. Their wingspan is often around 32–35 cm, and they often weigh around 70–140 g. They are heavier before migration. This page has no separate measurements from birds in Georgia. Their body is pear-shaped, and their wings are longer than those of many nonmigratory chicken-like birds. The upper part of the body is mottled brown with lengthwise pale cream stripes; the underside is lighter. Both males and females have a pale stripe above the eye and a dark stripe across it.
    - >-
      Males often have a black or dark stripe or spot of varying size in the middle of the throat, and warmer colors on the face. Females have a plainer, lighter throat. Young birds resemble females. The bird stays hidden. When startled, it makes a squeaky call, flies low for a short distance, then drops back into cover.
    - >-
      Grey partridge (Perdix perdix) is much larger, with a rusty face and a dark
      belly blotch in adults; it is YR-R on the 2024 checklist. Chukar (Alectoris
      chukar) is a larger rock-slope bird with bold flank bars; YR-R. Common
      pheasant (Phasianus colchicus) chicks are streaked but grow into a long-tailed
      bird; YR-R — see the atlas pheasant page. Corncrake (Crex crex) is a rail of
      tall grass with a dry “crex-crex” call, not a quail whistle; BB; M. Japanese
      quail (C. japonica) is not on the wild Georgian checklist used here as a
      native species; escapees and releases can look almost identical — the
      advertising song differs. This atlas does not yet have separate pages for
      grey partridge, chukar, or corncrake.
    - >-
      It is harmless. In Georgia, a three-note whistle heard from a grain field or meadow in late spring or summer usually comes from this species. If Japanese quail may have been released nearby, confirm the identification with a clear view of the bird or a recording.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Tetrao coturnix; now placed in Coturnix
    (Bonnaterre, 1791). The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the OSME Region List
    for names: order Galliformes, family Phasianidae, genus Coturnix. English
    checklist name is Common Quail.
  - >-
    Nominate C. c. coturnix is the form expected across the western Palearctic,
    including the Caucasus region in handbooks. African and Atlantic island
    subspecies are not treated as field splits for Georgia on this page. Japanese
    quail (C. japonica) is a separate species; hybrids with common quail occur in
    captivity.
  - >-
    Georgian is მწყერი. English is common quail (also European quail). Russian is
    перепел. Family name in Georgian checklists is ხოხბისებრნი; order ქათმისნაირნი.
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: BB; M; WV, data quality A, global and national Red List
    LC, occurrence quality H, evidence PH; SR; CH (photograph; sound recording;
    collected by hunter). An older SABUKO web checklist coded the species YR-R, M
    (year-round resident and migrant). This page follows the 2024 Sandgrouse
    checklist for current national status and reports the older coding as a
    disagreement. Country-level confirmation is not a regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the common quail found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed breeding bird, passage migrant, and winter visitor (BB; M; WV).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In open fields, meadows, and crops in the breeding and migration seasons;
      some birds also winter. A published September migration concentration is
      documented in the Alazani River Valley. The 2024 checklist does not publish
      a regional gazetteer, so this page does not colour the whole country.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Small streaked brown gamebird with pale back streaks and a pale eyebrow.
      Males often have a dark throat mark. The male’s “wet-my-lips” whistle is
      the best field cue. Grey partridge and chukar are larger; corncrake has a
      different call.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 16–18 cm long at species level, with a wingspan often given as about
      32–35 cm and mass about 70–140 g. A separate measured series from Georgia
      is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Seeds and ground invertebrates. Diet has not been studied separately in
      Georgia on this page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      As a breeding migrant, passage migrant, and winter visitor according to the
      2024 checklist. Exact Georgian arrival and departure dates are not tabulated
      here. Heavy September passage is documented in the Alazani Valley.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. BB on the 2024 checklist means breeding bird. Named nest sites inside
      Georgia are not listed on this page.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless. It is globally Least Concern and coded LC nationally on the
      2024 checklist. It is not on the 2014 Georgian Red List. It is a legal
      gamebird in season under current DES rules; follow permits and bag limits.
