overview: |-
  The eastern spadefoot (Pelobates syriacus) is a burrowing amphibian that digs with a large “spade” on its hind foot. It is harmless to people and lives in dry areas of eastern Georgia. It is the only Pelobates in Georgia, and its presence is confirmed by the 2026 national species checklist. It is neither a toad nor a marsh frog: it belongs to the family Pelobatidae. Across the species as a whole, adult body length is usually about 6–9 cm. Georgia’s 2014 Red List classes it as Endangered, with numbers declining in the country. Globally, the 2021 assessment rates it as Least Concern. Do not expect it in humid western Colchis.

  Pelobates syriacus Boettger, 1889 was first described from Haifa, not from Georgia. In English it is called the eastern spadefoot or Syrian spadefoot. After the genus was revised in 2019, Balkan spadefoots are often treated as a separate species, Pelobates balcanicus. In one stretch of DNA, spadefoots from Georgia and the Balkans differ by more than 10%. Based on their range, Georgian spadefoots are tentatively assigned to P. s. boettgeri. The closest relative, P. fuscus, does not occur in Georgia.
habitat: >-
  In Georgia you are most likely to meet it in dry or semi-arid steppe and
  shrubland of the east, and sometimes in human-altered land, where
  fishless ponds, pools, or canal widenings hold water in spring.
  Tadpoles were mapped in 88 ponds in south-eastern Georgia in 2003–2008. The
  range roughly matches semi-arid landscapes; Gori is given as a western
  reference. It is usually absent from forest, though a few breeding sites sit
  at forest edges. In that study the species did not occur where annual rainfall
  exceeded 760 mm. The 2026 checklist barcode is from Chachuna (Kakheti); the
  figure locality is Tbilisi. A 1996 review listed Turtle Lake near Tbilisi (a
  1928 record), lakes Kumisi and Chili-Tba, Tsodoreti and Akhaldaba lakes, the
  Tbilisi– Rustavi stretch, and a fossil from the lower Khrami; the Manglisi
  record needs checking because the landscape is more humid than typical. Some
  1996 sites may no longer exist — that does not cancel the 2000s pond map. It
  is not expected in the Black Sea basin of the Caucasus or the forest belt of
  the Greater Caucasus. Caucasus literature sometimes cites about 0–1900 m; a
  separate elevational range for Georgia is not published here. On the atlas
  map, only Kakheti is marked as its range, because only Kakheti has enough
  photo records with coordinates. The map has one record from Kvemo Kartli and
  none from Tbilisi or Shida Kartli, so it does not count those regions as its
  range. This does not cancel the sites named above.
diet: >-
  On land, adult spadefoots eat small invertebrates: molluscs, millipedes and centipedes, spiders, grasshoppers and similar small animals. This applies to the species in general. Tadpoles, in the water, eat mainly algae and plant debris. Do not think of it as a way to control garden pests.
behavior: |-
  It is mainly active at night and spends the day underground. It digs its own burrow with the large yellowish “spade” on its hind foot, but also uses rodent burrows and empty spaces under stones. In hot, dry spells it may stay hidden underground. In the Caucasus, drought has been linked to mass deaths of spadefoots. It spends the winter in soil, among roots or under stones, usually alone and rarely in groups of 2–6.

  In Georgia, breeding starts early and is short: according to 2009 data, it runs from late March to early April, for about two weeks. If the pond is dry at that time, that year's breeding is lost. The Marsh Frog and the Green Toad, by contrast, can still lay eggs later. Across the species as a whole, a single clutch holds about 5500–6500 eggs. Males have no vocal sac that bulges outward.

  It takes 2.5–3 months to go from egg to young spadefoot, so a pond must hold water from March to July. In Georgia it avoids ponds with fish. The tadpoles are big: by early June they are easy to spot, with bodies longer than 4–6 cm. In late June or early July they turn into small spadefoots. Do not catch wild amphibians.
conservation: |-
  In Georgia's 2014 Red List, the Eastern Spadefoot is listed as endangered, with numbers declining in the country. That is stricter than its global status: worldwide, the 2021 assessment rates it as of least concern. The global assessment was made after the species was split, and it no longer covers the Balkan P. balcanicus the way the old combined species did. An updated assessment for the same species was published in 2024.

  In the 1990s, Georgian spadefoots were considered threatened, and some of the places where they lived may have been lost. Yet in 2003–2008 more breeding ponds turned up in the south-east than the eight sites known in 1996 suggested. The spadefoot is threatened by breeding ponds drying out or disappearing, fish being released into them, drought, and land being turned to other uses. Suitable ponds are scarce to begin with: in 2009 data, the Eastern Spadefoot was present in fewer than 5% of the ponds where Marsh Frogs and Green Toads were found. It is not listed by CITES. Do not catch wild amphibians.
interaction: |-
  The Eastern Spadefoot (Pelobates syriacus) is not venomous to people and poses no direct danger. When threatened, it usually tries to hide or quickly get away from you. In Georgia it occurs in the east, especially in dry, open places. It is seen more often in the evening or at night, particularly after damp weather.

  Do not catch it, pick it up or disturb it. Do not move it somewhere else or take it home. If you touch one, wash your hands well with soap and water, and keep them away from your eyes and mouth. As a rule, such contact is not a health risk. Leaving wild amphibians in nature is an important part of protecting them, so leave the spadefoot where you found it.

  summary: >-
    The Eastern Spadefoot is a stocky, short-legged tailless amphibian with a vertical pupil and relatively smooth skin. Its key feature is the large yellowish “spade” on the hind foot, which it uses to dig. It is Georgia’s only Pelobates. It resembles a toad, but toads have a horizontal pupil and rough skin, so look at the pupil first.
  traits:
    -  |-
      The key mark is on the hind foot: on its inner side there is a large yellowish bump shaped like a spade, which it uses for digging. The body is sturdy, the head large and flat, the hind legs short. The pupil is a vertical slit, and the eardrum is not visible. The skin is fairly smooth with small warts, unlike a toad's rough skin. The webbing between the hind toes is deeply notched.

      The back is yellowish or grey with large greenish blotches, while the belly is whitish-grey and unspotted. Across the species, body length is often about 6–9 cm. In 2019 measurements of P. s. boettgeri, females averaged 68 mm (40–92 mm) and males 69 mm (57–83 mm). Males and females are similar in size, but in the breeding season the male's shoulder gland stands out more.
    - >-
      Its tadpoles are among the largest of any amphibian, with a known maximum length of about 165 mm. In Georgia's fishless ponds they are already large and easy to see in June. Young spadefoots that have just changed from tadpoles often bury themselves in the mud along the shore. In some ponds the tadpoles spend the winter and finish developing the following year. The species can do this, but it is not the rule for every Georgian pond. Do not pick it up: the pupil, the body shape and, if visible, the spade on the hind foot are enough to identify it.
    - >-
      The Green Toad (Bufotes viridis) and the Caucasian Toad (Bufo verrucosissimus) have horizontal pupils, rough skin and large glands behind the ears. The Marsh Frog (Pelophylax ridibundus) and the Caucasian Brown Frog (Rana macrocnemis) also have horizontal pupils, with folds of skin running along the sides of the body. Marsh frogs often live in the same ponds but stay close to the water. The Caucasian Parsley Frog (Pelodytes caucasicus) is smaller and slimmer, and the spade on its hind foot is small and rounded. It is tied to humid western Caucasian forest, not dry eastern steppe. Tree frogs (Hyla) are green and have pads on their toe tips. The common spadefoot (P. fuscus) does not occur in Georgia, so a spadefoot seen here cannot be that species.
    - >-
      If you see a stocky, toad-like amphibian at night in a dry place in the east, check its pupil and hind foot. A vertical pupil and a large spade on the hind foot fit the Eastern Spadefoot better than a true toad. Very large tadpoles in a fishless pond in June also point to a spadefoot. It is not venomous, but still do not pick it up.
    - >-
      It is Georgia’s only Pelobates, and the 2026 checklist confirms that it lives in Georgia. The common spadefoot (P. fuscus) does not occur here. The Caucasian parsley frog (Pelodytes caucasicus) is smaller and slimmer. It is linked to humid western Caucasian forest, while the eastern spadefoot is linked to dry eastern steppe. Do not expect the eastern spadefoot in humid western Colchis.
