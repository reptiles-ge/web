overview: |-
  The praying mantis (Mantis religiosa) is an insect, not a reptile.
  Its presence in Georgia has been confirmed. According to the IUCN, Georgia is part of its natural range. Ilia State University’s biodiversity database also lists it as a species found in Georgia. Observations recorded on this page report praying mantises in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Tbilisi, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Shida Kartli, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Adjara, Imereti and Abkhazia.
  You can recognize a praying mantis by its triangular, movable head, large eyes, long body and front legs, which it uses to catch prey. At rest, it folds these legs as if praying. It may be green, brownish, straw-colored or yellow-brown.
  Adult females are usually larger and heavier, while males are slimmer and more active.
habitat: |-
  The praying mantis usually lives in warm, sunny, open areas with plenty of plants: tall grass, shrubs, field edges, dry grassy areas, and gardens. It may also be found in urban green spaces where some natural habitat remains, if there are enough plants and prey.
  On this page, 9 regions of Georgia are marked as places where it occurs. This does not mean it is equally common everywhere: the number of records also depends on how actively people have observed it.
diet: |-
  A mantis hunts by lying in wait. It mainly catches other insects and similar small animals, holding them with front legs shaped to grip prey.
  A large mantis may also catch relatively large prey, though this page does not present a separate detailed study of the feeding habits of mantises in Georgia.
  Seeing a mantis in a garden does not mean a pest has appeared. There is no guarantee that it will reduce the number of pests in the garden, either.
behavior: |-
  A praying mantis often sits motionless on grass or a bush, waiting for prey to come close. Its movable head and large eyes help it watch its surroundings. It quickly folds its front legs to catch prey.
  A praying mantis does not undergo a complete transformation as it grows: the young mantis that emerges from the egg resembles an adult but does not yet have wings. This species spends the winter as eggs held together in a cluster.
  Across the wide area where it is found, adults are seen in many places, especially during the warm season. The sources used for this page do not separately confirm exactly when adults are seen in Georgia.
conservation: |-
  According to the IUCN, Mantis religiosa is classified as Least Concern worldwide, meaning its risk of extinction is low. This does not mean it is doing equally well everywhere.
  Changes to open areas, intensive management of those areas, pesticide use and the killing of these insects may harm the species in some places.
  The sources used on this page provide no numerical data on how its numbers are changing in Georgia, or a separate assessment of its conservation status in the country. We can therefore say that the species has been recorded in many places in Georgia, but more data is needed to determine exactly how common it is in different parts of the country.
interaction: |-
  A mantis is neither venomous nor poisonous to people and is not considered a health risk. If you pick one up, it may grip you with its front legs, scratch you or bite you, so there is no need to touch it.
  If you find a mantis in a garden or another green space, it is best to leave it where it is. Watch it from a distance and take a photo without touching it. There is no need to kill it just because it looks unusual.

identification:
  summary: >-
    It is a large insect with a long body, a movable triangular head and large eyes. It catches prey with its front legs. It can be green, brownish or straw-colored, so don’t rely on color alone to identify it.
  traits:
    - >-
      A praying mantis has a triangular, very mobile head and large, prominent eyes on the sides of its head. This often makes it look as though it is following a person or something else with its eyes.
    - >-
      Its front legs are adapted for catching prey and have strong spines. At rest, it keeps them folded in front of its body. When prey appears, it extends them very quickly and catches it.
    - >-
      The front part of the chest is elongated, and the body is long. Adults have developed wings. Females are usually bulkier and heavier, while males are relatively slimmer.
    - >-
      Color alone is not a reliable way to identify the species. In the wild, both green and brownish individuals occur, and they blend in well with their surroundings.
    - >-
      A young mantis looks like an adult, but is smaller. It has no wings yet, or only the beginnings of its wings are visible.

faq:
  - question: Is the European mantis found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes, Mantis religiosa has been confirmed in Georgia. According to the IUCN, it also occurs naturally in Georgia. Ilia State University’s biodiversity database also lists it as occurring in Georgia.
  - question: Where can it be found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      According to the records on this page, the mantis has been seen outdoors in Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Tbilisi, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Shida Kartli, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Adjara, Imereti, and Abkhazia. You are most likely to see it in warm, sunny places with plants — gardens, fields with tall grass, and areas with shrubs.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Look for a movable, triangular head, large eyes, an elongated body and front legs used to catch prey. When folded, the front legs resemble a praying pose. It may be green, brownish or straw-colored, so don’t rely on color alone to identify it.
  - question: Is it venomous?
    answer: >-
      No. A praying mantis is neither venomous nor poisonous to humans. If you pick one up, it may bite or grip you with its spiny front legs, but this is not considered a serious health risk.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      The mantis mainly catches other insects and small arthropods. It waits in ambush, then grabs its prey with its quick, spiny front legs.
  - question: When is it most visible?
    answer: >-
      Adult mantises are mostly seen during the warm season. In many places across the northern part of their wide range, they are seen especially in summer. However, the sources used for this page do not separately confirm exactly when they are seen in Georgia.
  - question: Should I pick it up or move it?
    answer: >-
      No. You don’t need to pick up a praying mantis. It’s better to watch it where it is or take a photo without touching it. Catching a wild animal rarely helps you identify it and may injure it.
