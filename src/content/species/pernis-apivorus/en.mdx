commonName: European honey buzzard
location: Passage migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A medium-sized woodland raptor with a small, pigeon-like head and a long tail.
  It is not a true buzzard and does not eat honey. Confirmed in Georgia on the
  2024 national bird checklist as a passage migrant. In autumn it is the most
  numerous raptor in the Batumi bottleneck.
overview: >-
  The European honey buzzard (Pernis apivorus) is a medium-sized raptor in the
  family Accipitridae, genus Pernis. It is not a Buteo buzzard; the resemblance
  is superficial. The full Georgian species name is ჩვეულებრივი ბოლოკარკაზი; the
  genus is ბოლოკარკაზი. Search and ornithological lists also use კვერნაჭამია,
  კრაზანაჭამია and ირაო. It passes through Georgia on migration. Its presence in
  Georgia is confirmed by photographs. Adults are often about 52–60 cm long at
  species level, with a wingspan of 135–150 cm. It is not a threat to people.
  IUCN lists the species as Least Concern. The 2024 checklist assigns the same
  category nationally.
habitat: >-
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level migrant status but does not publish a regional
  gazetteer. Do not assume it occurs evenly across Georgia, including treeless
  high plateaus or only closed forest. Named records: the Batumi raptor count has
  monitored autumn passage since 2008 from watchpoints at Sakhalvasho and
  Shuamta, north of Batumi in Adjara, between the Black Sea and the Lesser
  Caucasus. In the 2008–2009 counts, about 51% of autumn raptors were this
  species (55% after correcting unidentified birds). A 2023 analysis gives a
  13-year mean of about 528,467 birds per autumn. A 2020 analysis gives a mean of
  528,059 and a record season of 666,364. The count’s own species summary gives a
  mean of 500,066, a highest season of 656,171 (2014), and a record day of
  178,796 (3 September 2012). Those figures cover
  different year windows; they describe passage, not a breeding-range map.
  Breeding habitat at species level is woodland. A separate national elevation
  belt for Georgia is not published here. Photographs on this page were not taken
  in Georgia.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. The English name and the
  Latin apivorus (“bee-eating”) mislead: it does not eat honey. At species level
  it specialises on social wasps and hornets — mainly larvae and pupae in the
  comb. It digs nests from the ground or from tree cavities; scale-like feathers
  around the face protect against stings. It also takes frogs, reptiles, small
  mammals and nestling birds, especially early in the season before wasp nests
  grow. It is not food for people.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. When soaring it holds the wings flat, not the shallow V
  often seen in common buzzard; in a glide the wingtips often droop slightly.
  The head is small and protruding, the tail longer than a buzzard’s. Calls are
  scarce on passage; at species level the territorial call is a clear peee-lu.
  This page has no Georgian recording. The nest is a stick platform in a tree.
  Clutches at species level are often two eggs; those figures are from the wider
  range, not a sample of Georgian nests. The 2024 checklist treats the species
  in Georgia only as a migrant. Literature from 2013 treated it as a summer
  breeder and bird of passage and gave 200–450 pairs in 2001–2012; a
  protected-areas checklist also treats it as a summer breeder and passage
  migrant. Breeding in Georgia is not confirmed today, so those older
  assessments are disputed. Named nests are not listed here. At Batumi, 90% of
  autumn adults pass in the last week of August and the first week of September;
  juveniles about ten days later, and they make up about 5.9% of the count.
  Spring totals at Batumi are lower: about 89,000 in 2019 and 44,000 in 2020. A
  second-calendar-year female was documented at Batumi on 17 September 2017 — an
  age rarely seen on autumn passage in the Western Palearctic.
conservation: >-
  On the IUCN Red List, Pernis apivorus is Least Concern (2021). The 2024 Georgian checklist assigns global and national
  category LC. The species is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List (Government Decree
  No. 190). The global assessment indicates decline, while adult passage at
  Batumi has been stable in the years counted; those two signals
  do not match, and the Batumi figure is a passage index for source populations,
  not a national breeding census. Accipitridae are on CITES Appendix II. Raptor
  shooting in the Batumi bottleneck is documented;
  that is recorded pressure, not a measured loss of this species on this page.
  Do not catch or keep wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people if left alone. The talons are
  strong; do not handle it. A bird digging at a wasp nest is rarely seen in the
  field; do not approach or disturb it.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A medium-sized raptor with a small, pigeon-like head, a long tail and flat
    soaring wings. Confirmed in Georgia as a passage migrant on the 2024
    checklist. It does not eat honey. The usual confusion is common buzzard and,
    rarely, crested honey buzzard.
  traits:
    - >-
      Length at species level is often 52–60 cm, wingspan 135–150 cm. Published
      measurements give wing 375–475 mm, tail 250–270 mm and mass 700–800 g; the
      female is slightly larger than the male. There is no separately measured
      Georgian series on this page. The head is small and elongated, the bill
      relatively slim, the tail longer than a buzzard’s. Wings are held flat
      when soaring. The tail often shows two narrow dark bars and a broad dark
      terminal band. The carpal patch on the underwing is weaker than in common
      buzzard.
    - >-
      Plumage is highly variable, from pale sandy to dark brown. The male often
      has a blue-grey head, the female a brown one — unusual among large raptors.
      Juveniles are more barred, and the nostrils are slit-like — a diagnostic
      feature in a head-on view. Scale-like facial feathers protect against wasp
      stings.
    - >-
      Common buzzard (Buteo buteo) has its own page in this atlas: shorter tail,
      rounder head, often a shallow V when soaring, and a darker carpal patch on
      the underwing. Crested honey buzzard (Pernis ptilorhynchus) is also M on the
      2024 checklist; Batumi counts average 18 per season, with a record of 38 —
      it is scarce. Adults often show a crest and a different
      underwing pattern; this atlas does not yet have a page for it. Black kite
      (Milvus migrans) is also abundant at Batumi: forked tail. Eurasian
      sparrowhawk (Accipiter nisus) and northern goshawk (Accipiter gentilis) have
      pages here — short broad wings, a different silhouette. Peregrine is a
      pointed-wing falcon. Golden eagle is much larger.
    - >-
      It is not a threat to people. Leave it alone. A medium raptor with a small
      head and long tail streaming over Batumi in autumn is more likely this
      species than a common buzzard.
facts:
  - >-
    Linnaeus described it in 1758 as Falco apivorus. The accepted name is Pernis
    apivorus. In the 2024 Georgian checklist: order Accipitriformes, family
    Accipitridae, genus Pernis. World bird lists treat the species as monotypic
    — no subspecies. Field identification is to
    species. The genus is closer to kites than to Buteo buzzards.
  - >-
    Encyclopedic Georgian uses ბოლოკარკაზი; the full species form is
    ჩვეულებრივი ბოლოკარკაზი. კვერნაჭამია appears in this atlas’s news and on the
    common-buzzard page. A protected-areas checklist gives კრაზანაჭამია (or ირაო). A
    biological dictionary lists ცუდქორა. English names include European Honey
    Buzzard (2024 checklist) and European Honey-buzzard (other lists). Russian:
    обыкновенный осоед. Turkish: arı şahini.
  - >-
    In Georgia it is a passage migrant, confirmed by photographs. It is Least
    Concern both globally and nationally. Literature from 2013 and a
    protected-areas checklist also indicate breeding; the 2024 checklist does
    not. That is country-level confirmation plus named Batumi counts, not a full
    regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the European honey buzzard found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. It is a confirmed passage migrant in Georgia, documented with
      photographs.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      The most concentrated autumn passage is named at the Batumi bottleneck,
      from the Sakhalvasho and Shuamta watchpoints. Spring passage in the same
      corridor is smaller. This page does not map every region.
  - question: How do I tell it from a common buzzard?
    answer: >-
      Small pigeon-like head, longer tail with two narrow bars and a broad
      terminal band, flat soaring wings, weaker carpal patch. Common buzzard has
      a shorter tail and often holds the wings in a shallow V.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      At species level, length is often 52–60 cm, wingspan 135–150 cm. Mass is
      700–800 g. There is no separately measured Georgian series on this
      page.
  - question: Does it eat honey? What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      It does not eat honey. It mainly takes wasp and hornet larvae and pupae.
      Diet in Georgia is not separately studied on this page.
  - question: When is it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      As a passage migrant. At Batumi the adult mass is documented in late
      August and early September. The checklist does not accept it as a winter
      visitor.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In Georgia it is known only as a migrant. Literature from 2013 treated it
      as a summer breeder, and a protected-areas checklist lists it as a breeder
      and migrant. Named nests are not listed here. That is a disagreement, not
      a confirmed national breeding map.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      Harmless if left alone. Globally Least Concern, and LC nationally on the
      2024 checklist. It is not on the 2014 Red List. Do not catch wild birds.
