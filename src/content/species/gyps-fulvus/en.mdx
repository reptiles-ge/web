commonName: Griffon vulture
location: Year-round resident and migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A large pale-bodied vulture with dark flight feathers and a short tail. Confirmed
  in Georgia as a breeding resident and a migrant on the 2024 national bird
  checklist. Named colonies are documented in Vashlovani and Kazbegi.
overview: >-
  The griffon vulture (Gyps fulvus) is a large cliff-nesting scavenger,
  confirmed in Georgia as a year-round resident and migrant. It belongs to the
  family Accipitridae. Georgian uses ორბი; ჩვეულებრივი ორბი and თეთრთავა ორბი
  also appear in dictionaries and search. The 2024 annotated checklist of the
  birds of Georgia codes it YR-R; M: a breeder present through the year, and a
  passage migrant. Seasonality is classed as high-quality (code A). Occurrence
  quality is high (H), with photographs. Birds of all ages have pale brown body
  plumage contrasting with dark flight and tail feathers. It is not a threat to
  people on ordinary encounters; colonies and nests should not be approached.
  IUCN lists the species as Least Concern. Georgia’s 2014 Red List treats it as
  Vulnerable (VU, D1). The 2024 checklist assigns national category NT; those two
  national assessments disagree.
habitat: >-
  This page does not colour a municipality-by-municipality map. The 2024
  checklist confirms country-level resident and migrant status but does not
  publish a regional gazetteer. Do not assume it nests evenly on the Colchis
  plain or in closed forest. Gavashelishvili and McGrady (2006) modelled
  Caucasian nest-site use: occupancy of a safe cliff ledge was negatively
  related to annual rainfall at the ledge and positively related to the share
  of open ground and the annual biomass of dead livestock within 20 km. Named
  Georgian breeding sites with recent counts: Eagle Canyon Natural Monument in
  Vashlovani Protected Areas, Kakheti, including cliffs at Khornabuji fortress
  and adjacent faces (Javakhishvili & Paposhvili, CNF/UNDP; 21 pairs in 2021,
  24 pairs in 2023). Those authors treat the canyon and neighbouring cliffs as
  one eastern Georgian breeding group, not the whole national population.
  Kazbegi National Park and nearby cliffs in Mtskheta–Mtianeti, including the
  Dariali Gorge near Gveleti and a colony near Mount Kabarjini (Javakhishvili
  & Melikishvili, 2021; Javakhishvili & Paposhvili, 2023): 27 pairs in 2021 and
  14 pairs in 2023. Gavashelishvili (2005) and Gavashelishvili and McGrady (2006)
  had earlier mapped four to five breeding colonies in the Kazbegi area. Adults
  stay near colonies year-round; immatures mostly move south after the breeding
  season and return in spring (Galvez, Gavashelishvili & Javakhishvili 2005).
  Batumi Raptor Count autumn totals for this species were 1–9 birds per season
  in 2011–2018 (Wehrmann et al. 2019; Birds of the World). That is passage, not
  a breeding map. Photographs on this page were not taken in Georgia.
diet: >-
  It is an obligate scavenger: it feeds on carcasses of large domestic and wild
  mammals, not as a hunter of live large prey. Galvez et al. (2005) and the
  Vashlovani and Kazbegi monitoring reports use that diet for Georgia. No
  percentage prey table from a Georgian nest sample is given on this page.
  Gavashelishvili and McGrady (2006) treat dead livestock biomass near the colony
  as a predictor of nest-site use in the Caucasus. Do not put out poisoned
  carcasses or bait wild birds.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. It is social and nests in small cliff colonies, not as
  scattered tree nesters like cinereous vulture in south-east Georgia. Courtship
  is mainly in December–January; eggs are laid from February to April; the clutch
  is typically one egg. Both sexes incubate. Galvez et al. (2005) give incubation
  49–65 days and fledging at 97–136 days; Gavashelishvili (2005) gives incubation
  48–54 days and fledging at 110–115 days. Chicks hatch from March to late April.
  The 2024 checklist treats the species as a breeding resident and also as a
  migrant. Voice is rarely heard in flight; hisses and grunts occur at carcasses
  and at the nest. Do not handle wild birds or approach colonies.
conservation: >-
  BirdLife International assesses Gyps fulvus as Least Concern on the IUCN Red
  List (2021). The 2024 Georgian bird checklist assigns global category LC and
  national category NT. Government Decree No. 190 of 2014 lists the species on
  Georgia’s Red List as ორბი, Gyps fulvus Hablizl, 1783, status VU, criterion D1
  (small population). That is the legal list; the 2024 ornithological checklist
  codes it NT nationally. The European Red List of Birds annex reports 120–1,300
  breeding pairs in Georgia (2013–2017, partial method) and an increasing
  short-term trend (2005–2017, partial); the long-term trend is unknown. That
  pair range is a partial estimate, not a complete census. Site counts above are
  colony samples. Abuladze (2014), cited in Birds of the World, records eleven
  birds killed by poachers in Georgia since 1973. The Vashlovani monitoring
  report lists poisoning, disturbance, food shortage, nest-site competition and
  habitat change among factors known to reduce vulture breeding populations in
  general; it does not quantify a national loss. Do not catch or kill wild birds.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people on ordinary encounters. It is not venomous. The
  bill is used on carcasses; do not handle it. Colonies can be sensitive to
  visitors. Do not approach nests. Capture or killing of a Red List species is
  prohibited. Poisoned baits are a direct risk to scavengers.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A large pale-bodied vulture with contrasting dark flight feathers, a short
    tail and a pale ruff in adults. Confirmed in Georgia as a breeding resident
    and a migrant on the 2024 checklist. Usual confusion is with cinereous,
    bearded and Egyptian vultures and with golden eagle.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level length is about 93–122 cm and wingspan about 2.3–2.8 m
      (Galvez et al. 2005 give 240–280 cm). A separate measured series from Georgia
      is not given on this page. The head and neck are largely bare; adults have
      a white or pale ruff. Body coverts are pale sandy brown; flight and tail
      feathers are dark. In soaring flight the wings are long and broad with
      fingered tips and are often held in a shallow V; the tail is short. Sexes
      are alike in the field; females are generally larger.
    - >-
      Juveniles have a browner ruff and more mottled upperparts; they take
      several years to reach adult plumage. Adults remain near colonies. Immatures
      are the age class most often involved in long-distance movement.
    - >-
      Cinereous vulture (Aegypius monachus) is also YR-R; M on the 2024
      checklist; this atlas has a page for that species. It is uniformly dark,
      with a short wedge-shaped tail, and often soars with flatter wings. Bearded
      vulture (Gypaetus barbatus) is YR-R: narrower wings, a long diamond-shaped
      tail, and a rusty body with a black “beard” in adults. Egyptian vulture
      (Neophron percnopterus) is BB; M and much smaller; adults are white with black
      flight feathers and a yellow face. Golden eagle (Aquila chrysaetos) has a
      page in this atlas: longer tail, golden nape, feathered tarsi; it hunts live
      prey. Common buzzard (Buteo buteo) is much smaller. Bearded and Egyptian
      vultures do not yet have separate pages here.
    - >-
      It is not a direct threat to people on an ordinary hike. Leave colonies
      alone. A large pale vulture with dark flight feathers over a cliff colony
      is more likely this species than an eagle.
facts:
  - >-
    Described by Hablizl in 1783 as Vultur fulvus; the accepted name is Gyps
    fulvus. The 2024 Georgian checklist follows the OSME Region List: order
    Accipitriformes, family Accipitridae. NPLG still places the genus under
    Falconiformes; current world lists use Accipitriformes. NPLG records two
    subspecies and assigns Georgia G. f. fulvus. G. f. fulvescens is an eastern
    form (Himalaya–India) and is not on the Georgian list. Field identification
    on this page is to species.
  - >-
    The official Georgian and Red List name is ორბი. ჩვეულებრივი ორბი appears
    in NPLG and in Kutubidze (1973). თეთრთავა ორბი is a descriptive name used in
    search. English names include Griffon Vulture and Eurasian Griffon Vulture
    (2024 checklist).
  - >-
    2024 checklist codes: YR-R; M, data quality A, global Red List LC,
    checklist-national Red List NT, occurrence quality H, evidence PH
    (photograph). The legal Red List (2014) is VU D1. That is country-level
    confirmation plus the named Vashlovani and Kazbegi colony counts, not a
    complete regional map.
faq:
  - question: Is the griffon vulture found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      year-round resident and a migrant (YR-R; M), with photographs.
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Named breeding colonies are documented at Eagle Canyon and Khornabuji in
      Vashlovani Protected Areas (Kakheti) and in Kazbegi National Park,
      including Dariali Gorge near Gveleti. Autumn passage at Batumi is recorded
      in very small numbers. This page does not map every region. Do not approach
      nests.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      A large pale sandy body, dark flight feathers, a short tail and, in
      adults, a pale ruff. It often soars with wings in a shallow V. Cinereous
      vulture is uniformly dark. Golden eagle has a longer tail and a golden
      nape.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 93–122 cm long at species level, wingspan about 2.3–2.8 m. A
      separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Carrion of large mammals, including livestock. It is not a typical hunter
      of live large prey. No Georgian percentage diet table is given on this
      page.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year as a breeding resident, according to the 2024 checklist, which
      also codes it as a migrant. Adults stay near colonies. Immatures mostly
      move south after breeding. Batumi autumn counts of this species are in
      single figures.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R means resident breeder. Recent nest counts include 24 pairs at
      Eagle Canyon in 2023 and 14 pairs in Kazbegi in 2023. The clutch is
      typically one egg.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is harmless on ordinary encounters; do not approach colonies. It is
      globally Least Concern. The 2014 Red List lists it as VU D1; the 2024
      checklist codes it NT nationally. Do not catch wild birds.
