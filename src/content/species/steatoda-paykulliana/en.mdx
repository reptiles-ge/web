commonName: False black widow

description: >-
  A cobweb spider with a globular abdomen, often mistaken for a true widow. It
  is confirmed in Georgia; a bite can be painful, but this is not the karakurt.
overview: >-
  The false black widow (Steatoda paykulliana) is a cobweb-weaving spider in the
  family Theridiidae. The accepted name is Steatoda paykulliana (Walckenaer,
  1806). The World Spider Catalog treats the species as accepted. Theridion
  paykullianum of 1805 is a nomen nudum; the valid description is 1806. It was
  historically listed as Lithyphantes paykullianus; Steatoda latrodectoides was
  synonymized with this species by Breitling et al. 2016. In Georgian the names
  are ცრუ ყარაყურთი and ცრუ შავი ქვრივი — both because it resembles the true
  karakurt (Latrodectus tredecimguttatus) but belongs to a different genus.
  Nentwig et al. give female body length as 8.0–13.0 mm and male as 4.5–8.4 mm.
  Otto 2022 lists the species for Georgia. That is a country-level confirmation,
  not even density in all twelve regions. It has venom for prey; a bite to a
  person can be painful, but this is not a karakurt bite. IUCN has not assessed
  the species. It is not on Georgia’s 2014 Red List; the arachnid on that list
  is the fen raft spider (Dolomedes plantarius).
habitat: >-
  At species level Nentwig et al. give habitat as dry or semi-dry places — dry
  grassland, dunes, heath, calcareous hills and quarries, and rock crevices;
  rarely fallow orchards and meadows. That is not a measured Georgian elevation
  belt. This atlas colours regions only from named records. Three specimens in
  the Zoological Research Museum Alexander Koenig (ZFMK), identified by
  Krammer, are labelled Sighnaghi (Kakheti; ZFMK-Ar-21375), 0.65 km east of
  Kodiani on the pipeline road between Sakire and Tsikhisjvari
  (Samtskhe–Javakheti; 1,850 m; ZFMK-Ar-20728), and Kveda Chkhutuneti (Adjara;
  ZFMK-Ar-21512). Those records have no date. Otto 2022 lists the species for
  Georgia; Otto 2015 counted 72 Caucasian records (Russia / Georgia /
  Azerbaijan; Armenia was then absent). That is a Caucasus-level frequency, not
  a Georgian population map. Other regions are not coloured on this page. GBIF
  also holds citizen observations elsewhere; this atlas does not colour regions
  from those points. The photographs on this page were not taken in Georgia.
diet: >-
  There is no separate Georgian diet study on this page. At species level it
  takes insects in an irregular cobweb — often under a stone, in a crevice, or
  near the ground, not in a large orb web. Prey is wrapped in silk and then
  bitten. That is a general trophic niche, not a measured Georgian menu.
behavior: >-
  The web is irregular. Look under stones, in rock crevices, in dry grass, and
  in building cracks — not in an orb web spun across open grass. Nentwig et al.
  note that females may be found through the year; a female with an egg sac is
  recorded in March at species level. That is European phenology, not a measured
  Georgian calendar. A full national calendar is not on this page. The male is
  smaller than the female. Do not pick it up — handling is what makes a bite
  more likely.
conservation: >-
  IUCN has not assessed the species. It is not on Resolution No. 190 of 2014 —
  the arachnid on Georgia’s official Red List is the fen raft spider (Dolomedes
  plantarius, VU). Otto 2015 called Caucasian records “very abundant” (72
  records across the Russian North Caucasus, Georgia, and Azerbaijan together);
  that is not a Georgian population trend. Zarikian 2022 lists the species in
  Armenia — a neighbouring country, not a Georgian range. Do not collect wild
  spiders.
interaction: >-
  The spider has venom for prey. Maretić, Levi and Levi 1964 reported that the
  venom of this species is toxic to mammals. Bites to people are usually rare
  unless the spider is pressed or handled. Bites by the genus Steatoda are
  called steatodism in the literature: local pain is almost always present;
  systemic symptoms (nausea, headache, malaise) are less frequent and milder
  than latrodectism from a true widow (Isbister and Gray 2003 — Australian
  Steatoda, not a separate Georgian series). This is not a karakurt
  (Latrodectus) bite and not a Levantine viper bite. An allergic reaction is
  possible after any bite; if the face, breathing, or general condition worsens,
  call 112. Do not pick it up. The web may be in dry grass, under a stone, or in
  a building crack; destroy it only if it blocks a path — the spider is not a
  pest.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small cobweb spider with a globular dark abdomen and a red or yellow band
    on the front edge. The female is larger than the male. This is not a
    karakurt.
  traits:
    - >-
      At species level the female body is 8.0–13.0 mm (Nentwig et al.); the male
      is 4.5–8.4 mm. Legs are not included. There is no separate Georgian
      measurement series on this page.
    - >-
      The female carapace (prosoma) is dark purplish brown to black. The abdomen
      (opisthosoma) is black, with a red or yellow band around the front, sometimes
      with a median series of chevrons. That is the main field mark against a true
      widow: the band is on the front of the back, not an hourglass on the
      underside. The legs are brown. The male has a whitish front band and a
      strongly dentate median longitudinal band.
    - >-
      The web is irregular, often under a stone, in a crevice, or close to the
      ground. There is no orb web and no stabilimentum — those belong to Argiope,
      not this species.
    - >-
      In Georgia the true lookalike is the karakurt (Latrodectus
      tredecimguttatus) — this atlas has no profile for it yet. The female
      karakurt is often larger and may have red spots or a different pattern;
      field separation is not always easy. If you are unsure, do not pick it up.
      Otto’s Georgia checklist also names other Steatoda (for example S. grossa,
      S. triangulosa, S. albomaculata) — they lack this anterior red band or
      have a different pattern.

faq:
  - question: Is the false black widow found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Otto 2022 lists the species on the Georgia checklist. That is a
      country-level confirmation.
  - question: Where might I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Named museum records are Sighnaghi (Kakheti), Kodiani between Sakire and
      Tsikhisjvari (Samtskhe–Javakheti, 1,850 m), and Kveda Chkhutuneti
      (Adjara). Look in dry grass, under stones, and in crevices. Other regions
      are not coloured on this page.
  - question: How do I tell it from a karakurt?
    answer: >-
      The female of this species has a red or yellow band on the front edge of
      the abdomen. The karakurt (Latrodectus) is a different genus; it often has
      red spots and a different underside pattern. Confusion in the field is
      easy. Do not pick it up.
  - question: How large does it grow?
    answer: >-
      At species level the female is about 8–13 mm, the male about 4.5–8 mm.
      Legs are not included. There is no Georgian series on this page.
  - question: Is it venomous and dangerous to people?
    answer: >-
      It has venom for prey. A bite can be painful; at genus level systemic
      symptoms are less common than with a true widow. This is not a karakurt
      bite. Do not pick it up. If symptoms are serious, call 112.
  - question: When is it visible?
    answer: >-
      At species level females may be found at different times of year; an egg
      sac is noted in March in the European dataset. A full Georgian calendar is
      not on this page.
  - question: Is it protected?
    answer: >-
      IUCN has not assessed it. It is not on the 2014 Red List.
