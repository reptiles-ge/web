commonName: Eurasian sparrowhawk
location: Year-round resident and migrant in Georgia
description: >-
  A small woodland hawk with short wings and a long tail. Confirmed in Georgia as
  a year-round resident and passage migrant on the 2024 national bird checklist.
overview: >-
  The Eurasian sparrowhawk (Accipiter nisus) is a small hawk that breeds and is
  present year-round in Georgia. Other sparrowhawks pass through in spring and
  autumn. The 2024 annotated checklist of Georgia's birds confirms both
  statuses (YR-R; M). Females are markedly larger than males, so birds of this
  one species can look strikingly different in size. The northern goshawk
  (Accipiter gentilis) is a different species.
habitat: >-
  Sparrowhawks breed in woodland and also hunt along forest edges, in parks,
  gardens, and urban green spaces. Abuladze (2011, 2013) describes the species
  in Georgia as a common breeder, passage migrant, and winter visitor. The
  Agency of Protected Areas lists it among the birds of Batsara-Babaneuri
  Protected Areas. Autumn passage can be watched near Batumi at Sakhalvasho and
  Shuamta; sparrowhawks often fly low and singly, which makes complete counts
  difficult. The map below shows public iNaturalist photo observations, not the
  species' full range. No records, or just one record, in a region does not mean
  the species is absent there.
diet: >-
  Eurasian sparrowhawks prey mainly on birds. Males usually take smaller birds,
  such as sparrows; the larger females can also catch thrush-sized birds and
  occasionally pigeons. Small mammals are taken less often. They frequently
  catch prey with a sudden dash from cover, along woodland edges or in gardens
  where small birds gather. These are general traits of the species; this page
  does not present a separate study of its diet in Georgia.
behavior: >-
  It is active by day. Flight is a flap-flap-glide; the silhouette is T-shaped,
  with a long, almost square-ended tail and blunt wing-tips. It is highly
  manoeuvrable in trees. The call is a sharp, rapid kek-kek-kek or similar
  series, more often around the nest; prey alarm calls are often heard first.
  It builds a stick nest in a tree and usually lays four to five eggs. The
  female incubates them for about a month while the male supplies her with
  food. These are general species traits, not findings from a Georgian nest
  study presented on this page.
conservation: >-
  Globally, the Eurasian sparrowhawk is Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List.
  The 2024 Georgian bird checklist also prints LC in its national category
  column, but its authors explain that this comes from a draft update that has
  not been adopted. The 2014 Georgian Red List in force does not include this
  species; it lists the Levant sparrowhawk (Accipiter brevipes) as Vulnerable.
  Abuladze's 2011 summary estimated several hundred breeding pairs; this is not
  a current national count. Illegal shooting near the Batumi migration corridor
  and trapping for falconry in Ajara have been documented. Do not catch wild
  birds without a permit.
interaction: >-
  It is not a threat to people. It may take small birds in gardens. Wild birds
  must not be captured; the talons are strong.

identification:
  summary: >-
    A small hawk with short broad wings, a long tail, and yellow eyes. The male
    is blue-grey above with rufous barring below; the female is larger and
    browner. It may be confused with Levant sparrowhawk on migration or northern
    goshawk by size.
  traits:
    - >-
      Silhouette: short, rounded wings and a long, almost square-ended tail.
      Flight is flap-flap-glide, not the pointed wings of a falcon. The adult
      eye is yellow to orange; the legs are long and yellow. Length about 29–41
      cm at species level. Males about 29–34 cm and 105–196 g, females 35–41 cm
      and 185–350 g — among the largest size gaps between the sexes in raptors.
      A separate measured series from Georgia is not given on this page.
    - >-
      The male is slate blue-grey above, with rufous-barred cheeks and breast;
      the underparts can look plain orange at a distance. The female is dark
      brown or grey-brown above, brown-barred below, often with a clearer
      supercilium. Juveniles resemble the female but the barring is coarser or
      blotchy, and the eye is paler yellow. Juveniles usually lack a dark
      mid-throat stripe — a Levant sparrowhawk mark.
    - >-
      Levant sparrowhawk (Accipiter brevipes; IOC now Tachyspiza brevipes) is a
      breeding migrant in Georgia on the 2024 checklist (BB; M) and is VU on
      the 2014 national Red List in force. It has more pointed wings, dark
      wing-tips, a less barred underwing, and a dark adult eye, not yellow. It often migrates in
      flocks; Eurasian sparrowhawk usually passes singly. Northern goshawk
      (Accipiter gentilis; IOC Astur gentilis) is bulkier, with broader
      wings, a white supercilium, and a more rounded tail. A large female
      sparrowhawk can look goshawk-like in a brief or distant view, but is less
      bulky. A juvenile goshawk is streaked below; a juvenile sparrowhawk is
      barred. Shikra (Accipiter badius) is casual (Cas) on the checklist; field
      identification is specialist. A juvenile peregrine (Falco peregrinus)
      can also resemble a sparrowhawk in fast, direct flight; when visible, its
      pointed wings and proportionately shorter tail separate it. Kestrel and
      hobby are pointed-winged falcons too. This atlas has a separate page for
      goshawk; not yet for Levant sparrowhawk or shikra.

facts:
  - >-
    Described by Linnaeus in 1758 in Systema Naturae. The 2024 Georgian
    checklist follows the OSME Region List for names: order Accipitriformes,
    family Accipitridae, genus Accipiter. IOC World Bird List keeps Accipiter
    nisus in Accipiter; northern goshawk is now Astur gentilis and Levant
    sparrowhawk Tachyspiza brevipes. Field identification is to species; the
    checklist still uses the older genus names.
  - >-
    Handbooks assign Caucasus birds to the nominate subspecies, A. n. nisus
    (Europe and Asia Minor east to western Siberia and Iran). Eastern A. n.
    nisosimilis is a more Siberian form and is not a field split in Georgia.
    Macaronesian A. n. granti does not occur here.
  - >-
    The usual Georgian name is მიმინო. English names in use are Eurasian
    Sparrowhawk (2024 checklist), northern sparrowhawk, and sparrowhawk.
    Russian is ястреб-перепелятник. ქორი (goshawk) and ქორცქვიტა (Levant
    sparrowhawk) are other species.
  - >-
    In the 2024 checklist, YR-R means resident breeder and M means passage
    migrant. Seasonality has quality code A, occurrence quality H, and PH means
    a photograph or video. These codes describe the species at country level;
    they do not establish its complete regional distribution.
faq:
  - question: Is the Eurasian sparrowhawk found in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The 2024 annotated checklist of the birds of Georgia lists it as a
      confirmed year-round resident and passage migrant (YR-R; M).
  - question: Where can I see it in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      In woodland, forest edge, parks, and towns, where it hunts small birds. It
      is listed for Batsara-Babaneuri Protected Areas. It can also be seen on
      autumn passage near Batumi. The map shows public observations, not its
      complete range.
  - question: How can I identify it?
    answer: >-
      Short broad wings, long tail, yellow eye, flap-flap-glide. Male blue-grey
      above with rufous-barred breast; female larger and browner. Levant
      sparrowhawk has a dark eye and more pointed wings; goshawk is much bulkier.
  - question: How large is it?
    answer: >-
      About 29–41 cm at species level. Females are clearly larger than males
      (about 185–350 g versus 105–196 g). A separate measured series from Georgia
      is not given on this page.
  - question: What does it eat?
    answer: >-
      Mainly small birds. This page cites no Georgia-specific diet study.
  - question: When is it present in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      All year as a resident, plus spring and autumn passage, according to the
      2024 checklist.
  - question: Does it breed in Georgia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. YR-R on the 2024 checklist means resident breeder.
  - question: Is it dangerous or protected?
    answer: >-
      It is not a threat to people. It is globally Least Concern. The national
      LC in the 2024 checklist comes from a draft update; the 2014 Georgian Red
      List in force does not include this species. Do not catch wild birds.
